# Chapter 13 — Quiet Floors
*POV: Kaito*

Takamatsu pretends the war is a rumor you can drown in harbor noise.

Karaoke murder three streets inland. Tram bells. Diesel arguing with salt. A claw machine winning nothing for someone who still has coins to waste. The city is loud the way cities get loud when they think water makes them safe — as if harbors were absolution and hills were only scenery. Mira doesn't buy the pretend. She billets us in a waterfront room with blackout tape and a map of the hospital stack drawn on the back of a menu from a place that still serves oden like the world is made of broth.

Suzumori is not in the room.

That matters. I keep catching myself inventing her there — humming, correcting corridors with a finger, already billeted like the extract had the manners to finish itself — because my grief wants the rescue scheduled before my feet have earned it. Mira catches the inventing the way she catches unfinished clauses. The waterfront room smells like harbor damp and blackout adhesive. Outside, karaoke murders a chorus for a city that still thinks water is absolution.

"She's still inside," Mira says, mild, tapping the menu's west wing. "Quiet floors. Asset posture. We recon tonight. We take her when the living minds are where we need them — not when your guilt schedules a rescue. Extract is a finished clause. Recon is counting the mouths that hold the keys."

"I wasn't—"

"You were." She doesn't look up. Pencil tick on a camera icon. "Boys who invent rescues write muddy sentences. Muddy sentences bleed you harder. Look at the map."

I look. Corridors. Stair cores. A loading bay that faces the wrong street on purpose. Camera icons in Mira's neat hand. A circle around a ward labeled only *bell* — not because we are sentimental, because the elder's debt came with a name and a hum and a warning: if they file her east, the rooms stop being real. Transfer pending. East. The words sit on the menu like a second weather.

"Living minds," Mira reminds me, finally looking up. Scar pale in the lamp. "Never the lock. Never the door. The person who loves the key. You know this."

"I know," I say.

"You know with your mouth," she says. "Your shoulders still want to kick wood."

She's right. I want wood. Wood doesn't look at you while it obeys. Wood doesn't empty behind the eyes and then fill with the ordinary urge to sleep in its own bed. Wood doesn't remind me that every door I open is a person I have borrowed against their will. The horror in miniature from Kuroishi sits here too: no workaround that isn't a face. Recon means learning which faces, in which order, without spending the Bellkeeper early like a boy scheduling grief into a heist.

---

Afternoon is staging, not heroics. Mira walks me past the stack from three angles — Plaza approach, waterfront service road, a residential cut where laundry still pretends the war is elsewhere. She makes me name every living mind I can see who loves a door: porter, nurse, guard, driver on a smoke break, clerk with a stamp that could become a lie. I hate how natural the inventory becomes. Grammar as hunger. Hunger as map.

"You're flinching at the count," Mira says.

"I'm counting people like locks."

"They are," she says. Mild. No cruelty in it, which makes it worse. "That's the power's honesty. Kick wood if you want to feel righteous. Wood won't open. Tonight you practice opening without taking. Recon. Not extract."

I nod because arguing would be an unfinished clause.

---

Recon is people.

We go after the hour when hospitals pretend they are only buildings. Mira wears the calm of someone who belongs on a clipboard. I wear silence until silence needs a verb. The stack rises against the harbor dark like a held breath — glass, concrete, the soft stink of disinfectant fighting diesel. Gray-green at the main entrance with asset posture, not bored checkpoint. Radios murmur *acquisition* in the same font as every lie I've learned to hate.

A night porter walks us past a camera because I ask him to take his break on the east stair. Clean. Who. What. Finished. His eyes empty, then fill with the sudden holiness of a cigarette he has been denying himself. He goes. We go. Echo paints my wrist. Mira's cloth is ready.

A charge nurse unlocks a wing because I tell her the audit is early and she should show us the quiet floors first. She hesitates — spine of *no* built over years of bad paperwork — and then the sentence lands and her hesitation becomes a route. Keys. Soft shoes on linoleum. The fluorescent hum changes pitch as we climb, or maybe that is only my throat inventing weather.

"How many on quiet floors tonight," Mira asks her, ordinary voice, ordinary curiosity.

"Four," the nurse says, dreamy-precise. "One transfer pending east. Old woman. Doesn't speak much. Hums." She blinks at us as if we are a dream she is politely hosting. "You have the forms."

"We have the forms," Mira says, and does not smile.

We do not take Suzumori.

That is the knife of the night: walking the corridor outside the room where an old woman hums behind glass and deciding that wanting is not the same as finishing. Through a wire-reinforced pane I see a narrow cot, a water cup, a shape small enough to be mistaken for inventory. The hum is thin, checking the air for cracks. It finds me. For half a second the hum changes — recognition without sight — and my mouth wants to say *we're here, hold on, open—*

Mira's fingers on my wrist. Bruise map. Warning.

"Not yet," she breathes. "If you pull her now, the building screams. Soft lockdown is better than sirens, but only if we choose the hour. Count the minds. Count the doors. Leave."

Leaving tastes like cowardice. Leaving is also the only clean sentence available. Through the glass Suzumori's hum thins, then steadies, as if she has filed our almost and chosen patience. I tell the charge nurse to log a routine pass and return to her station. She does. We descend the stair the porter emptied for us. On a landing a guard turns, radio half-raised, mint optional on his breath, and for a half-second the plum tree opens under my feet again. I do not say *stop*. I do not say *kill*. I give him the smallest useful clause I have left: "Face the courtyard and stay there until your relief comes."

He faces the courtyard. Echo thickens. Copper under the tongue. Blood warm on my upper lip. Mira wipes my face in the stairwell shadow like she's polishing a tool she refuses to sentimentalize, and I let her, because the alternative is asking Haru to look at me and Haru is a soft problem somewhere in this city I will not hunt tonight. Outside the loading-bay angle we mapped earlier, harbor dark waits without applause. Recon complete means knowing the cage without opening it. My shoulders still want wood. My mouth knows better. That gap is the whole night.

On the quiet floors the air changed. Less beeping. More waiting. FDC gray-green moved differently there: not bored, but filing. I heard *carrier* once in a radio murmur and thought of Kanzaki's watch on a birthday roof, of boys alive and catalogued, of a throne split and hidden until someone like Mira decided guilt could be aimed.

"They're hunting carriers," I say, back in the waterfront room, map menu spread again, blackout tape peeling at one corner.

Mira's mild eyes don't deny it. "They're hunting a throne. You're what a throne looks like when it's still learning grammar."

"And the Bellkeeper?"

"Reads the rooms," Mira says. "Names the furniture. Aims you where aiming still means something other than grief." She taps the loading bay. "We stage the extract when the living minds are where we need them — nurse, bay guard, driver. Runners on anyone you don't touch with your mouth. Soft and finished. No wood. No heroics. Not tonight. Tonight you proved you can walk past the cage without inventing the rescue."

"It felt like leaving her."

"It was," Mira says. "Leaving on purpose is different from losing. Learn the difference before the hill teaches you the expensive version."

Through the corridor window of our billet I see the city. Trams. Harbor. A bar sign somewhere blinking MINATO like a dare — harbor name, no salt in sight. For half a second I imagine Haru under that kind of light, apologizing to a pass, ledgering everyone's mood, holding a city instead of chasing a ghost. The image hurts worse than the echo. I almost say his name. I don't. Saying it would make the room a shrine, and I am done building shrines I can't protect.

"Move," I told a guard tonight, and he moved, and I followed my own voice into a stairwell like it was the only road I had left.

Tonight I don't give the order to anyone. I sit on the mattress facing the wall and listen to Mira sharpen a plan into clauses — times, faces, which radios must murmur *routine* and which must simply be elsewhere. Somewhere in the stack an old woman hums on a cot that is still a cage. Somewhere under polite mansion lights a soft boy may be learning not to run. The still-man's coat is a private weather warning at the edge of every sentence I almost finish in public. I keep my mouth shut on purpose and feel, absurdly, like that is the bravest clause I have left.

I don't hope. I check the scrape in my throat, taste metal, and practice the sentences I will need when recon becomes extract — who, what, finished — until sleep takes the unfinished ones away. Extract stays tomorrow's grammar. Tonight stays ghosts in a hospital we refused to empty early, and a Bellkeeper still humming behind glass like a kept thing waiting for the clause to finish.
