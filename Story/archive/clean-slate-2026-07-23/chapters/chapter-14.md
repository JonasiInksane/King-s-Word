# Chapter 14 — Dry Fire
*POV: Haruki*

Bar Minato sits two ridges from any real salt and still insists on being a harbor.

Back lane off the Central Arcade, past a karaoke tower's service entrance — a wooden face the width of two doors, red lantern gone brown at the seams, and over the curtain a hand-painted sign: **みなと**. Harbor. In a town that has to borrow the word from memory. Riku finds it the way he finds exits: by refusing to look lost. I find it by following him and pretending my ledger isn't already pricing the room for moods.

Mama Machi sets down two bowls of oden before Riku and I have finished sitting. Fifty-something, bun speared with a chopstick, forearms that have carried kegs and coffins. Flat gaze that prices you in one pass — not unkind, just finished with the idea that boys arrive unexplained. The noren still swings from our entrance. Oil and soy and something sweet underneath that might be mirin or mercy.

"Trouble eats standing up," she says. "That's how I know. Sit anyway."

We sit. The broth hits like a temporary absolution — daikon, egg, the kind of kindness that doesn't ask for your origin ward. Machi claims the *Mama* is unauthorized. The street uses it anyway. She does not ask where we are from. In her bar, apparently, everyone is from the same place, and the place is called *before*. Riku's boots leave a polite rectangle of yard dust on the floorboards; Machi looks at it once and decides not to charge rent yet.

Gorō occupies the corner stool like a tax office — retired tram driver, ginger-root hands, cardigan in all seasons, one beer all evening. He demands *one new fact per night* from anyone under thirty, collected with the weary rhythm of a man who rang the same three bell-notes his whole life and still believes information should pay rent. His laugh is a gearbox warming up. His eyes miss nothing that moves like war.

Riku gives him a cut-through behind the yards. I give him that the mansion's lights change when the wards get nervous. Gorō snorts. "Hill's always nervous. Nervous is how landlords sleep."

"Useful," Machi says, which from her is a receipt.

The bar becomes a weather station. Thursdays especially — working boys, displaced harbor stories, gray-green politeness discussed like tide tables. Someone complains about tram squeezes. Someone else pretends the hospital stack's quiet floors are only a rumor hospitals tell to keep nurses humble. A tofu grandmother argues that the '09 snow was worse than any flood because snow just sits on you and waits; Gorō sides with floods because floods at least give a tram driver something to do. I wash dishes when cash runs thin. The water prunes my hands. The lantern's brown edge watches like an old eye. Riku fists my shoulder once when I apologize to a dropped cup, and Machi says, without looking up from the register, "Break it or pay for it. Don't marry it."

I almost laugh. It comes out soft. Soft is still the only dialect I trust.

Machi feeds us on principle and taxes us in work. I learn the sink — hot water, cold rinse, the particular shame of a glass that still smells like someone else's night. Riku learns which kegs need a second back and which only need a look that says *don't*. Gorō learns that I notice when the mansion lights change and files it under *hill weather* with a grunt that might be approval. Nobody asks for our origin wards. Nobody offers absolution. Minato is a harbor made of broth and stubbornness, and for the first time since Nagihama the word *hold* has furniture to sit on.

On a slower night Machi makes me chop scallions until my eyes water and says, without ceremony, "Boys who chase ghosts forget how to eat. Eating is how you stay in a city long enough for the city to spit something back." I chop. I eat. The knife is honest in a way speeches aren't. I do not ask her how she knows about ghosts. Harbormasters know weather when it walks in.

---

Riku teaches dry fire in a back lot with a borrowed piece that never gets a magazine.

Night. Bleach-building shadow. Takamatsu noise rising around us — karaoke, a tram bell, someone winning nothing in a claw machine three streets over. He stands behind my shoulder and corrects my grip by moving my fingers like I am a broken tool he has decided, against advice, to keep. Stance. Breath. The difference between pointing and deciding.

"Again," he says. Click of tongue. "You're aiming with your manners."

"I'm aiming with my eyes."

"Your eyes are apologizing to the wall." He taps my elbow. "Decide. Or don't pick it up. A gun you won't decide with is just a rumor in your hand."

We dry-fire until my shoulders burn and the empty click stops sounding like a joke. Night air cools the metal. My breath fogs once and vanishes. His curriculum, stated without softness: so nobody gets to use you. Not paper. Not causes. Not friends with beautiful reasons. I think of Mira's open palm in a kitchen. I think of Kaito's shoulder turning inland. I do not say either name. Riku doesn't ask. He teaches bodies, not thrones, and for that I am stupidly grateful — grateful enough that the soft boy in me almost apologizes for the gratitude, and then doesn't.

Between clicks he talks about breath the way Mira would talk about grammar if she were kinder and meaner at once. In. Hold. Decide. The decide is the whole lesson. Pointing is theater. Deciding is the thing that keeps a hand from becoming someone else's tool. My soft instincts arrive late enough, finally, that the empty click feels like a choice instead of an apology.

"Why me," I ask once, during a pause when the lot smells like dust and iron.

"Because you walk like the road owes you manners," he says. "And because pay, boredom, spite — pick one. I'm not writing you a poem."

"Spite at what."

"At men who put hands on bags," he says. "At clipboards. At soft boys who apologize before the swing. At Tuesdays." He grins, crooked tooth sudden. "Don't make it a speech. Feet tomorrow. Dishes after if Machi'll have you."

Machi has us. Thursdays especially. The lot cools. We walk back through an arcade that still believes in lanterns. Somewhere on the hill a pupil holds its polite brightness. Somewhere in my ledger a loud boy's name still tries to become a chase route. I let it sit unpaid.

---

One slow hour Gorō asks for his nightly fact and I give him something too big for the stool.

The bar is thin — two working boys half-asleep over barley tea, Machi wiping a glass that is already clean, the lantern breathing brown light. Outside, curfew rumors thicken like weather that hasn't decided to become rain. Inside, Minato keeps serving: broth, patience, the soft clink of a spoon that refuses to become a siren. I look at my oden and hear, somewhere under the daikon, the shape of a thought that has been walking with me since the plum tree: that the war isn't weather. That weather doesn't schedule boys off roofs or stamp widows into lanes. That someone, somewhere, stands in a kitchen and stirs — not metaphor as decoration, metaphor as diagnosis.

I don't stand on a chair. I don't roar it. Full roar is a different night, a different courage, a room thick enough to hold a banner without choking. I say it into my oden like a confession, voice soft enough that it might still be manners:

"I keep thinking it isn't just happening. Like — like weather. Like someone has a kitchen and they're deciding what gets cooked."

Silence for a beat — the Takamatsu kind, where a point has been scored on an invisible board. Gorō grunts, which in his language means *filed*. His ginger-root hands turn the beer glass a quarter inch. "Hills cook," he says. "Always have. Question is who eats."

Machi's eyes flick once. Flat. Accurate. "Keep that thought fed," she says. "Starved ideals bite the wrong people."

Riku clicks his tongue. "Soft boy getting political. Gross. Don't make it a speech yet."

"I'm not," I say, and mean it. The thought is an ember, not a banner. Embers need air and time and a room that won't laugh them into ash. Tonight Minato gives it a stool and a grunt and a warning, which is more audience than a ledger usually gets. I file Machi's line next to Sugi's tide-flat *no* — different harbors, same refusal to let soft boys turn pain into a cute idea without feeding it first.

Outside, curfew rumors thicken. Somewhere on the hill the mansion lights hold their polite brightness. Somewhere in a hospital stack a quiet floor waits for a theft I cannot see. Somewhere a loud boy may be wiping blood off a mouth that used to apologize for nothing. I file both facts — the ember and the lockdown weather — and try not to wonder whether Kaito would have laughed at the kitchen metaphor or turned it into a command with who and what and finished.

Silencing is becoming his dialect. Mine is still trying to be a stove.

I wash another glass. Hot. Rinse. The prune of honest work. Riku dries without being asked, exit-eyes softer in lantern light. Gorō's beer goes flat the honest way. Machi turns the sign and says, "Tomorrow's Thursday. Don't be interesting on the way home."

We aren't. We walk the arcade like boys who have decided holding a city includes knowing which lanterns stay lit. The empty click of dry fire still lives in my hands. The ember lives under my ribs. Neither is a speech yet. The hill keeps its polite brightness; we keep our stubborn, quiet feet.

For now, that is enough heat to cook with — broth, empty click, a kitchen thought kept fed and quiet until a louder Thursday earns the roar.
