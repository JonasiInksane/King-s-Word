# Chapter 1 — The Loud Quiet
*POV: Haruki*

"I'm going to tell you what happened," I say.

Sugi doesn't ask me to. She just sits. Nagihama kitchen, late, kettle clicked off and still ticking heat. Her hands are flat on either side of a cup the way some people pray. Outside, tide-smell and diesel. Inside, the arcade cat on the shelf with its one milky eye — still here, still watching, not yet given to any water.

Not because she asked. Because if I don't start talking, the quiet fills with the wrong version — the one that makes him a monster, or me a saint, or the roof into weather. None of that is true. True is smaller and worse. True fits in three words that used to mean nothing.

So I tell it. From the beginning. From before the words had teeth.

---

The salaryman's pass was already falling when my hand closed on it.

Plastic, warm from his pocket, a hand's width above the Rail Loop tiles. I hadn't meant to move. The motion lived somewhere under thought — the same place that steps around puddles and catches glasses before they tip. I straightened, held it out, and said, "Sorry," which made no sense — I hadn't done anything wrong — and he blinked at me like I'd apologized for the weather. Heat was already coming up off the platform before the sun had cleared the buildings. The vending machine by the gates jingled its four notes for nobody. Neon over Sakaba-dōri still buzzed pink in daylight, useless; the karaoke tower with the dead 'K' still promised ARAOKE to the morning commute. My phone buzzed against my palm: the war app, two ugly tones, Haiga on fire again three prefectures east. I turned the screen face-down against my thigh like that would help.

"You apologized to a pass," said a voice behind me. "You apologized to an inanimate rectangle."

"I apologized to the *man*," I said, not turning around, because I'd know that voice at the bottom of a river.

"You said sorry before he could even be mad. You defused a fight that didn't exist." Kurosawa Kaito dropped onto the bench beside me with the boneless confidence of someone who had never once worried a seat wouldn't hold him — half a head taller even sitting, wet hair from a shower he'd clearly lost a fight with, the thin white nick through his left eyebrow catching platform light when he grinned. Yesterday's dark jacket over today's uniform. Knuckles scraped, as usual. He smelled like cheap soap and the mint gum he never quite admitted stealing. "One day you're going to apologize to a guy who's actually swinging at you and he's going to be so confused he'll just leave."

"That's the plan," I said. "De-escalation. It's a life skill."

"It's a *personality disorder*, Haru." He said my name the way he said everything — too loud, a little too fond, like the volume was the point. A salarywoman two benches down glanced over, already half-annoyed, and then her mouth did that soft thing mouths did around him — irritation folding into something almost like fondness.

The train doors chimed. We got on just ahead of them. Two women across the car glanced over and then, oddly, softened, the way people always seemed to soften around Kaito right when you'd expect them to bristle. Dark eyes, hard-bright edge, and somehow you wanted to be annoyed at him and then you weren't. He had that. He'd always had that. I used to think it was charm. Charm is a word people use when they don't want to look closer.

The train pushed off. Through the window the city unrolled itself — the river the color of weak tea, the elevated highway, the enormous municipal screen on the side of the Daigo Building that was supposed to show weather and increasingly showed the war instead. FRONT STABLE, it said this morning, in the specific font the government used for things that were not true. EASTERN WARDS: CONTAINED. A ribbon of smaller text underneath scrolled the names of the newly missing and I did what everyone in the car did, which was become very interested in my own knees.

"Ryn's off today," Kaito said, already changing the subject, which he did whenever the screen came up. "Grandma's stall. She's doing the pickle thing."

"She hates the pickle thing."

"She loves the pickle thing. She just hates that she loves it." He stretched, and his sleeve rode up, and I caught the edge of the bruise on his forearm — greenish, three days old, the shape of someone's grip. I didn't ask. Kaito collected bruises the way I collected apologies; it was how each of us tried to keep the people around us from getting hurt, and neither method worked, and we both knew it, and we never said so. "You bringing your mom's thing tonight?"

"Her *thing* is a homemade cake and you will show it respect."

"I will show it *reverence*," he said, and put his hand over his heart, and a third-year two seats down snorted into his phone, and just like that the whole car was a fraction warmer, and none of us could have told you why.

---

We came up the school hill late enough that the track already smelled baked and the vending machine that ate coins was already mid-theft. Cicadas had started their long complaint from the pines behind the gym. Kōgetsu Municipal High sat above the Rail Loop like it was trying to see over the city's shoulder to the east and had decided, wisely, not to look. The rooftop door still wore its chain and its padlock that had been broken so many times the school had given up replacing the lock and just replaced the chain — a polite fiction between us and the administration, like FRONT STABLE.

First period was Doi-sensei, who taught modern history and looked, every single morning, like a man who had been informed his deadline was yesterday. Soft midsection, thinning hair combed forward in a losing battle, wire glasses already askew. He shoved through the door clutching a stack of papers to his chest the way a smaller man might clutch a life preserver, ink on his fingers, tie loosened before the day had earned it.

"Sit down," he said to the room, to no one, to the general concept of teenagers. "Sit down, sit down, I have — please — I have handouts, and I have exactly enough, which means if any of you have multiplied since Friday we are in trouble."

Kaito was still on his feet in the aisle, arguing a point with a third-year who'd wandered in from the hall — something about a soccer match, the eternal war of the practice fields. The third-year had half a head and a year on him and was doing the chin-up thing older boys do when they want a room to watch. Kaito's grin had gone bright and hard. His hands were already talking.

I started to stand. Habit. Someone had to soften it before it became a fight.

Kaito didn't look at me. He looked at the third-year and said, mild as weather, "Back off. Go back to your class."

The third-year's mouth opened on a joke and then — nothing. He blinked. He took a step back like the floor had tilted. Then another. Then he was in the hall, and the door was swinging, and Kaito was wiping under his nose with the side of his thumb, a pinprick of red he looked at like it had insulted him.

"Sit," Doi-sensei said again, ordinary, and the room sat, and I shoved the moment in with a hundred others that didn't add up and that I was too much of a coward to add up then. Dogs that sat when Kaito told them to sit. Bus doors that reopened. Luck, I told myself. Presence. The story I preferred because the other stories had teeth.

Kaito dropped into the seat beside mine and nudged my knee with his. "See? De-escalation. Life skill."

"That wasn't de-escalation," I said. "That was a magic trick."

"Same difference," he said, and grinned with blood still drying under his nose.

---

After school the Fullmoon Mart smelled like cold sweet drinks and floor wax and the particular citrus cleaner Ryn's grandmother ordered by the case. Fluorescent light made everything honest and a little cruel. Amano Ryn was behind the counter with her sleeves shoved up and her apron strings double-knotted, dark hair clipped back, silver stud catching the light every time she turned her head. She had ink from price tags on her fingers and a look that could price a person's trouble in one pass. When she saw us she pointed at the drink fridge without looking up from the register.

"If you breathe on the ice cream case I'm charging you rent," she said.

"We're browsing," Kaito said.

"You're loitering. Loitering has a fee."

A boy — younger than us, Haiga dust still gray in the seams of his sneakers — bolted for the door with an egg sandwich crushed under his arm. Ryn vaulted the counter like the counter had always been a suggestion. Half a block. She came back winded, furious, and laughing, the rescued sandwich held up like a trophy, and then she looked at the boy's shoes and the laugh went out of her face the way a light goes out.

"You can't wash it out," she said, quiet, mostly to herself. Then, louder, to him: "Go. Before I decide I'm a responsible employee."

He ran. She put the sandwich back like it had done something wrong, and when she looked at us her eyes were older than both of us put together.

"Saturday," Kaito said, too fast, filling the hole. "Roof. Your birthday. Nothing is going to ruin it. *Nothing.*"

Ryn snorted. "You can't promise weather, idiot."

"I'm not promising weather," he said. "I'm promising us."

She looked at him, then at me, and the rare sincere smile arrived — the one that made the whole stupid city feel briefly like it had been built for three people and a cake.

"The roof," she said. "Fine. But if either of you brings sparklers I'm throwing you off it myself."

"Deal," I said.

"And no war talk," she added, quieter. "Not Saturday. I get one night where the east is just a direction."

"One night," Kaito said, like a vow, like a dare.

Outside, the Daigo screen kept lying in its clean font. My phone buzzed once more and I didn't look. Somewhere east, Haiga burned. Here, the karaoke tower still said ARAOKE, and Kaito's knuckles were scraped, and Ryn's cowlick refused the clip, and I was still the boy who apologized to falling plastic before it hit the ground.

I didn't know yet that *let* was a loaded word. That *nothing* was a dare the night would take personally.

I only knew we had a Saturday, and a roof, and one more ordinary evening to spend like money we thought we'd earn again.
