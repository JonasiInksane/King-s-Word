# Chapter 15 — Bell
*POV: Kaito*

We take Suzumori on a night the hospital thinks is ordinary.

That is the point of recon: ordinary is a door you can walk through if you have already counted the living minds who love the locks. Two nights ago we walked past her glass and left the cage shut on purpose. Tonight the purpose finishes. Mira's runners wait in a van that smells like oil and old oranges. Blackout tape still peels in our waterfront room where the menu-map has been folded into Mira's pocket. My throat is a scraped wire pretending it is ready. It isn't. Ready is a lie tools tell themselves. Harbor dark presses the stack's glass like a held breath finally deciding to exhale wrong.

Living minds only. The recon map is a ghost in my pocket; tonight we spend it.

A charge nurse walks us to the quiet floors because I tell her the transfer audit moved up. Clean. Who. What. Finished. Her keys become our route. Her soft shoes invent permission on linoleum that still smells like the night we walked past the cage and left it shut. Echo paints my face by the first stairwell; Mira wipes without comment, cloth already pink at the corner. I taste metal and keep going because stopping would invent a speech about guilt, and speeches bleed harder than clean clauses.

A guard clears a loading bay because I ask him to smoke outside and stay there until the van leaves. He wants to argue — spine of procedure, radio half-raised — and then the sentence lands and argument becomes a cigarette in the courtyard dark. He faces the wrong way for every useful definition of duty. Good. Useful. Cost. I do not watch him long enough to see if his eyes empty the way the Kuroishi watchman's did. Watching makes it a story. It is not a story. It is a person learning a temporary instruction.

A driver decides the east gate is the only gate that exists. I do not touch the gate. I touch the man who believes in it. He nods, dreamy-precise, and the geometry of exit rearranges itself around his belief. Mira's runners handle the bodies that still have will left — the ones I don't touch with my mouth. A clipboard diverted with ordinary lies. A radio murmuring *routine* because someone with a calm face told it to. I am not a cartoon villain. I am a boy with a sanded throat and a vow in stolen clothes, opening people like doors because wood will not listen, grieving with a weapon and still — for now — noticing the difference.

Suzumori is smaller than memory.

Cot. Water cup. The hum that checked me through glass on recon and found a crack shaped like recognition. She looks up when the charge nurse — still hosting our dream — unlocks the ward, and the hum becomes a tune with a name in it. The room smells like disinfectant and waiting. Transfer papers sit on a side tray like a sentence that hasn't been spoken yet. East. Asset. File. All the verbs FDC uses when it means *cage with better handwriting*.

"Loud one," Suzumori says. Eyes on my mouth, not unkind. "Mira's hands. Yes."

"Can you walk?" I ask.

"I can leave," she says, which is different and better.

She stands like a kept thing deciding to be a person again. No rush. Pre-modern patience. Mira offers an arm; Suzumori takes the air beside it instead, as if balance were a rite. For a stupid second I want to command the building itself — *open, forget, sleep* — and then the living-minds rule sits on my tongue like a bit: never the lock, never the door, only the person. Honesty as horror. I swallow it and move.

Quiet floors stay quiet — less beeping, more waiting — and for a stretch of corridor measured in heartbeats the building does not admit it is being robbed. A second nurse turns the corner; Mira's runner intercepts with a clipboard lie while I keep my mouth shut on purpose. Not every door needs my grammar. Some need ordinary theft. That almost feels like mercy.

Echo paints my face by the third door. Mira wipes. On the ramp to the bay, Suzumori pauses and looks up toward the hill as if the mansion has a pitch she can hear through concrete and polite light.

"He'll know," she says. "Not the theft. The shape of it. Cities feel when a kept thing moves."

"Then we move faster," Mira says.

"Fast is a muddy word," Suzumori murmurs. "Finished is better." She glances at me. "Literal. Still. Good. Intent would make you crueler than you can afford tonight."

"I'm already cruel," I say, thinking of a plum tree, of a soldier's body learning a new instruction, of Haru's face on the wrong side of an arm.

"You're grieving with a weapon," she corrects. "Cruelty is when you stop noticing the difference."

The difference sits in my mouth like metal. I notice it. I notice it so hard it almost becomes a prayer. Then we are in the van, and the driver who believes in only one gate takes us through it, and the smoking guard stays in the courtyard with his cigarette until the relief he will not recognize arrives too late to matter.

---

In the van Suzumori sits between us and studies the air by my mouth again. Harbor dark slides past the windows. Mira's runners do not speak. The city outside is still pretending to be ordinary — karaoke, a tram, a claw machine — and then the pretend thins.

No J-Alert. No sirens worth the name. Just a soft lockdown language arriving on phones and shop shutters: stay in, stay quiet, verification in progress. Harbor noise thins the way a room thins when someone important enters without announcing. Trams pause like animals listening. Karaoke dies mid-murder somewhere inland. The mansion lights on the hill brighten like a pupil dilating — polite architecture remembering it has a nervous system.

Mira exhales through her nose. "Aihara."

Suzumori's humming stops. "The filing hand. He was at a school the night a city learned the Word again. He likes boys alive and catalogued." She turns her face to me. Small. Calm. The kind of calm that has outlived kings. Rain-memory on the van window throws her reflection into fragments. "When you meet him, decide in advance whether you want answers or a corpse. The mouth you have now will give you whichever you schedule."

I taste metal. Schedule. The word fits too well. *Kill yourself* arrived fully built once because desperation finished the clause for me under a plum tree while Jun's trust-look still hung in the air. Scheduling would mean choosing the finish in advance — grief with a calendar. The thought should revolt me. Part of me — the hollow new room that nodded yes on the inland road — only files it next to *never lose* in its stolen clothes.

"Not tonight," Mira says. Debt and map and mildness. "Tonight we billet a bell. Tomorrow we name a hill."

Suzumori's eyes flick to Mira, then back to the air by my mouth. "Tomorrow is also a muddy word," she says. "Finished is better. But I will drink your water and ring your bell and wait until the sentences are clean."

We billet Suzumori in a room behind a shuttered arcade that smells like dust and old sugar. She asks for water and a bell if we have one. We have a bicycle bell scavenged from a repair shop's graveyard. She rings it once, soft, and smiles like the sound has been lonely. The lockdown language thickens on Mira's stolen handset — verification wards expanding, tram holds, a polite request that citizens remain helpful. Helpful. The word makes my scraped throat itch.

"Amatsu kept bells for rooms," Suzumori says, not quite to us. "To know which ones were still real." She looks at me again. "You carry half a throne and all of a grief. Don't let the girl with lists convince you those are the same weight."

Mira does not flinch. "Lists keep him alive."

"Lists aim him," Suzumori says. "Alive is a different verb." She drinks. Hums once, checking the room. Finds it real enough. "Sleep facing the wall if you must. Habit. In the morning I will tell you which doors on the hill are people. Not tonight. Tonight the city is learning we moved a kept thing. Let it learn without your mouth helping."

Outside, Takamatsu holds its breath. Soft lockdown. Expensive quiet. No scream, which means someone important is paying for manners. Somewhere under that breath, if the ledger still runs, Haru is feeling the quiet and calling it weather — holding a city, washing a glass, not chasing a ghost through verification language that would turn him into another unfinished sentence. I almost hope he stays soft enough to refuse the chase. Hope is still unfinished. I hold the almost anyway, private, useless, mine.

I don't go looking. Looking would be unfinished. Mira lays out the next map — mansion approaches, servant schedules, the polite gray-green around the hill — and I listen with the part of me that used to listen for Ryn's laugh. Suzumori watches my listening the way she watches air: for cracks.

That part is getting quieter. The sentences are getting louder. Suzumori's bicycle bell sits on the table like a small bright refusal to be inventory.

I am not sure which one I'm becoming — the grief, the grammar, or the boy who still notices the difference.

I sleep facing the wall anyway. Habit. In the dark I do not say Haru's name. I say nothing. Soft lockdown presses the shuttered arcade like a hand on a mouth that belongs to a whole city. The bicycle bell ticks once in settling air, small and stubbornly, fiercely real. For once, silence is not cowardice. It is the only clause that doesn't open a living mind I haven't chosen to spend — and the only mercy I can still afford without Mira's cloth.
