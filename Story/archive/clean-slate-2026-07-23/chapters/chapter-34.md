# Chapter 34 — After
*POV: Haruki*

After zero, the corridor is a mouth with nothing left to say.

I am on my knees in a maintenance niche that smells like chemical sweet and floor wax and the particular silence of completed forms. My ears ring until the world becomes a narrow tunnel. Somewhere shutters hold. Somewhere vents cycle down like a throat finishing a cough. Somewhere Habu's soft voice may still be thanking crews for clean floors. I do not hear words cleanly. I hear absence. A/B was a river. Now A/B is a stamp. The kitchen thought that stood up full size inside my chest sixty breaths ago lies on its side like a dropped spoon.

Then the building hears a boy.

Not Habu. Not gallery manners. A ruined radio that has remembered how to be loud — volume first, then grammar, then something heavier than either, a pressure that finds every skull that still has ears including mine in this niche with chemical sweet on my tongue. The speakers stutter. The PA eats itself. Twin metal taste hits the back of my throat the way pad bruise hit, the way sticky doors hit, the way two Voices colliding once shook a city bowl.

**I'd reshape this whole world to free it of this warfare.**

My words.

Minato's Thursday. Contractors laughing. A still man leaving a coin. My confession stood up in a bar and paid for itself in ridicule and dignity and a stove that finally caught. Now it comes back through Habu's vents with a heavier hand — angrier, finished, aimed at a building that just made children into a stamp. Not my mouth. His. Kaito. The louder boy spending my kitchen ideal like a weapon and the Word agreeing so hard the fluorescents seem to flinch.

I shake.

Not from cold. From recognition. From the particular horror of hearing yourself come back wrong — same clause, same reshape, same free-of-this-warfare, except the hand on it is no longer a soft boy's toast. It is a Voice promising the world a stir that will not leave adults standing. Somewhere above he keeps going — *not your clean floors, not your inventory* — and every word is a coin I once could not spend, now spent for me in blood dialect. *Reshaped by whom?* The still man's question sits up in my chest like a second knife. Tonight I know one answer, and the answer is wearing my best friend's throat.

Useless.

The word fits. I try to stand and my legs file a refusal that has nothing to do with throngs or pads or unfinished *don't*. Ordinary body refusal. Horror as gravity. Riku's hand finds my collar and hauls anyway — crooked tooth red, eyes emptied and filling with a ditch he has been carrying since fourteen, now flooded. He does not joke. The absence of joke is louder than alarms.

"Soft boy," he says, and the name is a rope, not a tease. "Breathe. Don't you invent a freeze on me."

I invent it anyway. Freeze is easy. Freeze is what the ledger does when columns tip past arithmetic into weather you cannot invoice. Kids I stuck doors open for. Kids who laughed illegal and brief. Kids who asked *is it over* like over were a stamp you could request. Over arrived. Over was protocol. Not a Voice. Not a roof. A building deciding inventory prefers zero. My sticky mouth rented agreements from guards while Habu's binder outran every soft verb I own. Ideals. Vain — until they weren't, until they came back through the walls with his hand on them and shook me harder than Habu's soft *inventory* ever did. The word *reshape* sits in my mouth like a coin that finally admits it can spend, and the spending is a massacre gathering somewhere I can feel like weather behind glass.

Mei refuses the freeze for me.

She is blunt and warm and furious in the fear-is-boring register, scarred knuckles white on my wrist, face close enough that I have to see a living person when the blocks are empty. No speeches. No borrowed names from graves I carry. She does not reach for someone else's dead girl to make me move. She uses the present tense like a tool.

"Up," Mei says. "You're not a statue. Statues get counted. Living first — that includes the idiot on the floor."

"They're—" My voice breaks on a word that isn't big enough. "I opened— I thought open meant—"

"Open meant open," Mei says. "Habu meant zero. Those are different kitchens. Don't you dare cook them into one pot so you can hate yourself efficiently. Hate later. Move now."

Riku's arm hooks under my shoulders. Partner-in-practice without the word. His body shakes once — not fear, aftermath, the aftershock of using yourself as a door while a corridor became math. "She's right. Curriculum doesn't end because the test was evil. Stand up so I can bill you for the standing."

I stand. Badly. The niche tilts. Through the hatch lip's crack I see what empty means when empty is not metaphor: armbands, stillness, fluorescents still polite, a hymn that has no throat left. Horror of empty blocks. The east wing's glass reflects lockdown amber like a closed eye that learned to shine. Somewhere behind seals a louder boy watched this and became something flatter; I know it without seeing him the way I knew doors opening that our keys did not touch. Twin frequencies. Opposite shores. Same metal taste. I still do not own what I am. I know what useless feels like. It feels like this.

Mei wipes my face with the heel of her hand — not gentle, effective — and leaves a smear of dust that feels like a stamp I might survive. "Service core still has a throat. Loading bay if the secondary seals haven't eaten it. Service road inland. We drag you if we have to."

"Leave me," I hear myself say, soft boy offering himself as incomplete apology. "I made the river. I made them easy to—"

Riku hits my shoulder with a fist — short, precise, curriculum. "You didn't invent Habu. You didn't invent vents. You invented a hatch. If you want to apologize, apologize by walking so your apology has legs."

Mei snorts once — almost a laugh, almost a blade. "Listen to the road dog. Soft boys romanticize blame because blame feels like work. Real work is the shutter that still opens."

I look at the empty.

I look at them.

Flee versus stay. Stay means digging through zero for a justice that has already been stamped complete. Stay means becoming a second inventory column for Habu's soft voice. Stay means waiting for the louder boy to answer protocol with sentences — and I can feel that weather building somewhere in the complex like a second storm behind glass. Flee means the loading bay. Means a service road. Means leaving kids who cannot be left because leaving is what already happened to them. The ledger tips. Numbers scatter. The only column that still balances is living first — Mei's phrase, Riku's haul, my legs learning obedience from people who will not let me freeze into a monument.

A girl from B — or the shape of her — stays stuck behind my eyes: fingers on a hatch lip that did not save her. A cough-laugh that became inventory mid-gesture. The hymn that lost its throat. I try to make the images into a reason to stay and dig, to put soft hands on sealed shutters until Habu's manners crack, and the images refuse to become a plan. Plans need living targets. Zero has none. That is the particular cruelty of protocol: it does not leave you a villain to punch, only a floor to kneel on and a soft voice already thanking crews for clean wax.

Riku reads the kneeling on my face before I do it again. "Don't. You kneel, I carry you like luggage, and luggage doesn't get a vote. Stand."

Mei adds, quieter, still warm: "If you need a kitchen thought that isn't vain yet — the living ones who made it into the core still need a road. Be useful to a pulse. Not to a stamp."

Useful to a pulse. I can almost hold that. It is smaller than reshape-the-world. It is larger than freeze. It smells like Minato broth and Gorō's *I walked* and Machi's door that refuses to feed trouble a closed one — proof that unfinished mercy sometimes has legs even when ideals bruise.

I lean flee before I admit I lean.

Not courage. Not ideals repaired. The ugly arithmetic of two sets of hands deciding soft boys do not get to become furniture in a wipe. Mei takes my left. Riku takes my right. They drag-walk me into the service core's darker concrete while my mouth keeps trying to invent a prayer and invents only breath. Behind us the volunteer floors hold their completed quiet. Ahead, bay rumor and diesel ghost and the inland dark that is not safety — only not-here.

"Feeds will lie," Mei says as we move, practical as keys. "They always do. Don't learn your dead from a chyron."

"What do I learn them from?" I ask, and hate how young it sounds.

"From the fact you still have ears," she says. "Use them later. Right now use feet."

Riku glances back once — toward empty, toward east, toward whatever massacre weather is gathering where gray-green still has pulses — and his crooked tooth shows without a smile. "Your jacket's still in the building. I heard him. Whole complex heard him. Soft boy got religion, and the loud one just spent it."

"Those were my words," I say, and hate how small it sounds against a Voice that used them like a fist.

"Then bill him later," Riku says. "Right now walk."

Knowing is not owning. Knowing is not going back. Knowing is a coin in the pocket while two people drag you toward a shutter that might still remember how to open. The Word — panic file, refusal file, pad skid, sticky doors — sits unnamed under my tongue. I will not call it a throne while children are a stamp. I will not call myself a Voice while my ideals lie vain on a waxed floor. We opened. Habu zeroed. The river ended. Afterward I had to be hauled like cargo that forgot it grew legs.

The service core slopes down.

Somewhere above, a gallery gun speaks once and then stops — not the wipe continuing, the aftermath sorting itself into quieter cruelties. My ears invent Habu saying *inventory* again; maybe he is; maybe the building has learned to vent soft words into every skull that survived. Riku's grip tightens when I flinch at nothing. Mei does not flinch at nothing. She flinches at seals, at camera angles, at the geometry of getting three bodies through a throat that might close. Fear is boring. Geometry is work.

Loading-bay air reaches us — colder, less recycled, the smell of trucks and night. A secondary seal hangs half-descended like a held breath. Mei's scarred knuckles find the manual release; Riku's shoulder provides the argument steel understands. I put my sticky useless mouth on a last living mind — a dock tech hiding behind a crate — and somehow he decides the bay door wanting open is his idea. Habit. Horror. Softness that survived zero by being too small to vent. He looks at me afterward like a man who has misplaced a decision and cannot find the receipt. I almost apologize. The apology dies unfinished. Good. Unfinished is the only grammar I trust when finished means Habu's stamp.

The shutter lifts enough for bodies. Through the gap: concrete apron, a chain-link suggestion of outside, the service road's gray ribbon waiting like a sentence that has not decided its verb. Behind us, empty blocks. Ahead, inland dark. Between them, my knees arguing with usefulness and losing to two people who will not let loss be the whole column.

"On three," Riku says. "We don't count pretty. We count moving."

"One," Mei says, already hauling.

I do not hear two. I hear gravel in the future and the lie of distance and the particular sound of a soft boy being saved against his inefficient will.

Mei does not ask if I am ready. Ready is a luxury. She and Riku drag me into bay shadow, toward a service road that runs inland like a thought that refuses to finish inside Habu's walls. Behind us Shizuhara holds its dead manners and its living storm. Ahead is gravel, dark, the lie of distance. I do not look back long. Looking back is how statues get made. Mei was right about that. She is right about feet. She is right about not borrowing graves to move me — and the not-borrowing somehow hurts more than a name would have, because it leaves the empty as only itself.

Flee.

Leaning. Dragged. Still moving.

The empty blocks stay empty. The kitchen spoon stays dropped. Ideals stay vain for one more breath of night — and still we move, because motion is the only argument left that Habu's soft voice has not yet stamped complete.
