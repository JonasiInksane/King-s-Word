# Chapter 32 — Open
*POV: Haruki*

Mei doesn't give a speech. Speeches get stamped.

She gives a timing, a ring of borrowed keys still warm from a junior's hook, and a look that prices fear as boring. Block C's maintenance alcove smells like cold pipe and floor wax. Riku names the route the way he named exits in East Yards — aloud, twice, until my mouth can find them without light. Service core. Down two. Loading bay. Don't invent a speech when the shutter sticks. I hold the ninety seconds like a ledger column that will either balance or kill us. Somewhere behind east-wing glass a louder boy may be building sentences aimed at living minds who own locks. I do not know that cleanly. I feel it the way you feel weather through concrete: pressure, wrongness, a soft shelf tipping in a building that prefers columns.

"Keys," Mei said an hour ago, and then she went and got them — scarred knuckles, practical hair, war-prisoner issue worn like a choice. Not a volunteer. She corrected a corridor count out loud on our second day here and smiled like the numbers were always hers. Tonight the numbers are exits. "You two crack what bodies can crack. When the core hatch opens for shift, we push A and B through like cargo that grew legs."

"And Habu," Riku said.

"Habu will call contingency if he smells a Voice." Her eyes cut toward the sealed glass that swallowed a jacket and a hymn. "If outer minds clear while our hands clear the cells, the kids get a weather neither of us ordered clean. We don't wave. We don't coordinate. Call it aid when they're past the loading bay."

Riku knocked his fist against my shoulder — curriculum without sermon. "Tonight's job is don't freeze when it works. Working is when people die for real."

I am still here. The disposal truth landed in us like a stamp: volunteers not relocated, inventory that can be zeroed, Yano's cheerful hand marking who is still useful. Riku's wound detonated and then scabbed into motion. My kitchen ideal bruised and stood up anyway. *Reshape* means nothing if I won't stand for the people in the cells when the building reaches for them. I do not own what happened on a Takamatsu pad — the skid, the city shake, Mira saying *two Voices* like a second knife. I file it under panic. Under refusal. Under unfinished. Tonight unfinished means open.

The light cycles.

Mei moves first.

Bolts yield to borrowed metal and to hands that have practiced not looking like hands that practice. Riku takes the heavy doors — shoulder, hip, the ugly arithmetic of a road dog who teaches soft boys so nobody gets to use them. I take the sticky ones: a guard who suddenly decides helping a lost errand boy is his idea; a latch that softens when I apologize to it like a train pass; a captain whose eyes glaze into agreement when I say, careful and unfinished, "You want this hatch clear for a minute." Stickiness. Belief. Fear. Dialects I will not call a throne. The Word — if that is what Mira named — stays in the panic file where I can pretend it is only manners wearing my mouth.

Cellblock A opens like a held breath finally spent.

Kids pour. Armbands. Thin blankets dropped like shed skins. Someone laughs once — illegal, bright — and someone else shoves them forward because laughter is a luxury corridors charge interest on. Mei counts with her chin. Left. Left. Don't stop. Don't look. Riku becomes a moving wall, crooked tooth bared, body used as a door against any gray-green who remembers their job mid-flood. I keep saying soft things at thresholds — *it's okay, come on, the way is open* — and people come because soft things have always rented me doors I didn't earn.

Then the outer locks open too.

Not our keys. Not Mei's ring. A gallery hatch that should stay sealed for shift change swings because the living mind that owns it has walked away into an errand that isn't an errand. A cordon door sighs wide as if the building forgot it preferred inventory. I taste metal that isn't only mine. Somewhere outside our hands, sentences are clearing paths. I do not see him. I do not need to. The pad's twin frequencies still sit in my bones like a bruise that learned geography. If Kaito is Wording guards into weather, the reader can infer it from doors that behave like people were asked politely by a mouth that does not do polite. I file the inference under *overlap* and keep moving. Mei was right. We don't wave.

"Core," Riku snaps. "Now. Don't romanticize the yard."

We hit the service core as the river of volunteers thickens — B-block joining A, irregulars from C cutting in where Mei's map said irregulars cut. Chaos is not a metaphor. Chaos is elbows, breath, a kid falling and being hauled up by two strangers who have decided strangers are temporary family. Gray-green shouts try to become order and fail against volume. A rifle comes up; Riku breaks the geometry of the arms holding it with a short ugly lesson; the rifle becomes furniture. Alarms find their scream late, the way sentences find targets. Fluorescent strips flicker and keep working, because the building's manners outlive its contents.

Mei grabs my sleeve hard enough to leave a future bruise. "Soft boy — the sticky door on the medical spur. If it stays shut we bottle. Make it want open."

I want to apologize for existing. Habit. Ledger. The apology dies unfinished, which feels like the only grammar I trust. I put my palm on the spur door's living owner — a medic tech with Yano's cheerful disease in his eyes — and I say, soft as Minato broth, "You should unlock this. You already decided to." His fingers obey the decision he thinks is his. The door yields. I hate myself in the clean way that still lets me run.

Through the spur: more corridor, more wax smell, the east wing's sealed glass flashing lockdown amber at the edge of vision. For one stuttering heartbeat I see a smear of movement behind specials glass — Mira's stillness, a shoulder I would know at the bottom of a river, a small humming shape that does not flinch — and then only reflection. Extract weather. Parallel plan. Same locks. Mei is not in that glass. Mei is here, choosing cells over whatever legitimacy a humming grandmother came to collect.

"Don't look east," Mei says, reading my face without kindness or cruelty — blunt warm, the engine of someone who refuses freeze as a personality. "Looking is how you become a statue. Statues get counted."

Riku laughs once through blood — not humor, pressure valve. "Listen to the girl. Survive a Tuesday."

"That's my line," I try, and it comes out thinner than joke, thin as a coin I still can't spend. *Reshaped by whom?* Yet. Yet is still unfinished. Yet is running with kids who thought morning was a promise.

We flood Level Two.

The corridor becomes a throat trying to clear itself with bodies. Cameras blink red. A PA crackles — Habu's soft voice beginning a sentence about calm and return to bunks — and the sentence loses to the sound of feet. Someone from B starts a hymn and loses the tune and keeps the breath anyway. I stick another guard into helpful confusion. Riku puts another man on the floor. Mei redirects a girl who tries to go back for a shoe as if shoes were people. "Living first," Mei says. "Dead don't need an escort." She hasn't earned the right to sound like she has seen dead; she sounds like it anyway. Fear is boring. Motion is the alternative.

The core hatch gapes.

Cold lip. Darker air. The promise of loading bay and service road inland if luck is a real substance and not a rumor Habu taxes. Kids pour toward it like water finding a break in a seawall. For a moment — one stupid, luminous moment — the kitchen thought stands up full size inside my chest: *someone stands in a kitchen and stirs, and tonight the stirrers are us, and the pot is open.* Ideals. Bruised. Upright. Almost believable.

Then a shutter halfway down the gallery twitches like an eyelid considering sleep.

I feel the building remember it has contingencies. Not yet fired. Not yet zero. But the binder is waking. Habu's softness on the PA acquires a new patience — the patience of a man who erases children without changing tone. Riku hears it too; his shoulders set for a bill he hasn't been handed yet.

"Don't stop," he says. "Halfway is a sealed corridor."

"Don't stop," I repeat to the nearest kids, sticky and useless and necessary. "The way is open. Keep going."

Mei hits the hatch lip first, scarred knuckles white, counting living under her breath as if chalk could still argue with steel. Riku shoves me after a cluster of armbands — not gentle, partner-in-practice without the word. I grab a small wrist, haul, taste dust and disinfectant and the metal of a grammar I refuse to own. Behind us the volunteer blocks empty into a river that has no banks. Ahead the service core breathes like a throat that hasn't decided whether to swallow or scream.

Somewhere the louder boy's sentences are still clearing outer minds. Somewhere Suzumori hums through wrongness. Somewhere Mira recalculates around a number that is currently hauling children instead of standing still for extract. I do not coordinate. I do not wave. I run inside the overlap like a soft boy who finally chose a door that faces living throats instead of a map.

The hatch takes us into darker concrete. Loading-bay rumor pulls from below. Alarms braid with Habu's soft contingency language beginning to thicken — not yet the word *zero*, but the weather before rain. Kids keep pouring. Riku keeps breaking geometry. Mei keeps counting. My sticky mouth keeps renting agreements from people who should know better.

We are not safe.

Safety would be an ending, and endings are a lie this corridor hasn't earned. We are mid-escape: momentum, elbows, open locks that shouldn't be open, a river of inventory growing legs, and a building inhaling before it decides whether to become a gun.

I keep moving.

The story keeps the page open.

The locks — ours, his, the ones that were never the building's — stay open for one more breath of chaos, and we spend the breath like people who know breath can be taxed.
