# Chapter 38 — Market Roof
*POV: Haruki*

Ashiro arrives as fry oil and wet reed and a roof that does not ask for inventory papers.

We hit the market town on legs that have stopped believing in distance. Service ditch behind us. Shizuhara's amber stutter somewhere in the memory of the handset Mei killed with her thumb. The ditch smelled like wet iron and weeds that did not care what we had witnessed. Gravel gave way to packed earth, then to cobblestones that knew the weight of carts and ordinary commerce. Ahead: stall streets, a reed canal the color of old tea, boarding rooms over shops that sell batteries and grilled things that have no business tasting good after a wipe. Curfew is a suggestion. Brown lanterns throw stubborn light like Minato cousins that never learned the hill's manners. Riku prices exits with his eyes before he prices food. Mei counts camera poles and finds almost none — Ashiro is the kind of place Federation fonts forget until someone useful needs a raid. I am still cargo that remembered it has legs. Useless still fits. Breath is the first job that finishes without becoming a stamp.

"Roof first," Mei says. "Food second. Guilt third, and only if it pays rent."

Riku snorts. Crooked tooth red at the edge from a scrape that has been healing wrong since the bay. "Soft boy, you freeze on a market stair, I invent a ladder fee."

"Bill me," I say, and this time the bar-road cadence almost lives without zero sitting on it. Almost is enough for a first roof.

We take a boarding room above a stall that sells dried fish and rumors. The stair creaks in a rhythm that says other feet have learned it — travelers, smugglers, people who paid coin instead of names. The landlady looks at our prison soles and our dust and decides not to ask for names that would become reports. Her face is round and practical. She hands us a key without ceremony, the way you hand someone a bucket when the roof leaks. Mei pays with coin stolen clean from a dock tech who still thinks the bay door was his idea. Stickiness. I hate that it works. I use it anyway because living first includes soft boys who rent rooms with mouths they refuse to own.

The room has one window on the reed canal and a futon that smells like someone else's winter. Cedar and old sweat and the ghost of a heating unit that worked last season. Riku takes the floor without discussion — exits banking rest, back against the door, boots still within reach. Mei takes the window ledge, scarred knuckles on the sill, geometry of a town she has already started finishing — sight lines, pole counts, the angle of the canal bridge two stalls down. I sit on the futon edge and listen to the market invent ordinary noise: spatulas against iron, laughter that does not know our names, a radio muttering soft lockdown weather that has not yet learned our faces. Below us, someone grills something that smells like squid and forgiveness neither of us has earned.

Mei tilts the dead handset once more before she kills the screen for good. Same lie. Better font. *Remnant terror.* *Anomalous vocal actor.* My words in his throat — reshape, free of this warfare — cut into a villain chyron while Habu's zero stays polite and absent. The screen's glow paints her knuckles pale for a second. I hate the font. I hate that hate is not a plan.

"Don't learn your dead from that," Mei says, not looking at me. "Learn them from the fact you still have ears and a roof. Roofs are not apologies. Roofs are arithmetic."

Riku kicks his boots off. The laces slap the floorboards once. "Curriculum for the night: sleep like people who might have to run before the oil cools. Soft boy watches the door first shift. I take second. Girl takes dawn because she already owns the numbers."

"Fear is boring," Mei says. "Dawn is work."

I watch the door.

Not because I am brave. Because watching is the soft job that isn't soft — keep the page open. Count the canal's slap against stone. Count Riku's maintenance-breath — slow, practiced, the rhythm of a man who has slept in worse rooms with worse doors. Count the ways a market can sound like peace while a louder boy paints a detention complex in blood dialect somewhere west of our last amber look. Twin metal still sits under my tongue when I think of him. Pad bruise. Same breath — *Clear the pad!* / *Stop!* — a city shudder, Mira naming knives. I filed the shout under panic. Panic is getting expensive. *Reshaped by whom?* The coin rattles. Yet. Yet is a boarding room. Yet is two people who will not let me freeze into a monument on someone else's futon.

The canal keeps time. A boat pole thunks against a piling. Someone on the stall street below closes shutters — not curfew, just a vendor done for the night. Brown lantern light slides across the ceiling when the wind moves the window frame. I think of Sugi's kitchen and then stop thinking of it, because thinking of kitchens makes the ledger remember what kitchens cost when the war finds them. This kitchen is fry oil and strangers. That has to be enough.

Riku's breathing slows. Not sleep yet — banking. Mei does not move from the sill. Her stillness is not rest. It is a camera that decided the lens stays open.

Hours pass the way hours pass in rooms where the door is the only job that matters. The canal darkens. A late fisherman poles past below, lantern swinging, not looking up. The radio on the stall street changes stations — static, then a song about rain, then static again. I count the song's refrain twice and then stop counting, because counting refrains is how you start building a monument out of ordinary things.

Once, footsteps on the stair — my pulse jumps before Riku's hand lifts off the floor, silent, already awake. The landlady's tread. A bucket. Water running in the shared basin down the hall. Then nothing. Mei exhales through her nose. The moment passes without becoming a stamp.

Morning tastes like grilled squid that has no business this far from salt and tea that pretends it is kindness.

We walk the stall streets for breath. Ashiro does not know us. That is the mercy. A woman sells pickled plum from a bucket; the smell hits like Sugi's kitchen for half a second and then the ledger remembers Sugi is a different roof in a different war. My throat tightens. I swallow it down. Children chase a plastic ball between reed carts. No armbands. No inventory tablets. A boy's laugh when the ball skids into the canal is the loudest honest sound I have heard since before the pad. Ordinary is a kind of lie I am willing to rent for an hour.

Riku buys onigiri with a face that says the price is a crime and the crime is acceptable. Mei corrects a stall's change count out loud, smiling like numbers were always hers — camera of her, paid again without ceremony — and the stall auntie laughs instead of calling gray-green. The auntie's hands are flour-dusted and unafraid. Fear is boring. Geometry is work. I wash my hands in the canal because clean is a job that finishes, and finished jobs are the only theology I trust when ideals lie vain. The water is cold and brown and carries a leaf past my wrist like a small boat that does not know about detention complexes. For a minute I stand at the canal edge and let Ashiro pretend it is only a town — reed carts, battery stalls, a barber pole turning without asking who we fled from. Pretending does not erase the chyron. Pretending is just breath you rent before the next road invoices you.

"We can't stay," Riku says by afternoon, crooked tooth worrying his lip. We have retreated to the boarding room to eat standing up, like people who have not unlearned motion. "Market towns get remembered the day after someone useful needs a story. Soft lockdown fonts are already hunting Remnant weather. We look like Remnant weather's leftovers."

Mei nods once. She has been watching the canal bridge angle again — I can tell by the way her eyes refocus when she turns back to us. "Next road is smog. Susuichi. Code town. Law thin. Debts thick. I know a name that opens a tarp if we pay what we swear."

"Smuggler code," I say, tasting it. Not FDC paper. Not Remnant liturgy. Something that might still sell us if the debt turns. Something that might shelter us if we swear true. The word *code* sits differently than *protocol*. Protocol is Habu's binder. Code is a handshake that remembers your face.

"That's the rental agreement," Mei says, almost kind. "Better than Habu's lobby."

I look at Ashiro's brown lanterns one more time — Minato cousins, stubborn, lit. Somewhere on a hill a still man once laughed and asked *whom*. Somewhere a louder boy spent my confession through speakers until the building shook. Somewhere Kurohata's pine may be teaching him that anger is a flag. I am on a market roof that asked for coin instead of inventory. That has to be enough for one day of breath.

"Code road tomorrow," Riku says. "Tonight you eat. Ideals don't fill stomachs. They just decide who starves — and I'm not starving a soft boy on my tuition watch."

I eat. The onigiri is good — rice still warm from the stall, nori with the faint taste of the grill. Good is not forgiveness. Good is fuel. The reed canal keeps slapping stone like a clock that refuses Habu's soft voice. Below us, the dried-fish stall closes. The landlady's footsteps cross the hall — unhurried, domestic, a sound that belongs to a world where people sleep because sleep is ordinary and not a lie you tell corpses. Through the floorboards I smell the stall's last grill going cold. Oil cooling is its own clock. Riku banks rest on the floor. Mei watches geometry until dusk paints the canal the color of old bruises. I watch the door again because someone has to, and soft jobs are still jobs, and Ashiro is the first roof after Quiet Field that let us be three living pulses instead of a chyron.

Flee leaning. Breath paid. Next weather: soot.
