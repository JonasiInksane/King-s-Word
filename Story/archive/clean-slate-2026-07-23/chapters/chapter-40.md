# Chapter 40 — Code Road
*POV: Haruki*

Ashiro cannot hold us past the second morning.

Riku says it first, which means it is already true. Mei says it second, which means the geometry agrees. I say nothing useful, which means I am still myself. Soft lockdown fonts have thickened on a shared screen in the fish stall downstairs — Remnant hunt language bleeding inland, verification weather looking for irregulars who look like leftovers from a wipe. The chyron does not know our faces yet. That is not safety. That is a clock that has not finished its first tick.

We look like leftovers. Prison soles. Dust. A soft boy who still flinches at amber light. Market towns get remembered the day after someone useful needs a story. Ashiro was breath. Breath is not a plan.

"Susuichi," Mei says, packing nothing because we own nothing. Her hands move with corridor efficiency — fold, tuck, check seams, no wasted motion. "Soot Market. Law thin. Code thick. Name that opens a tarp: Auntie Bazzu — she doesn't like that name, use it anyway. We pay what we swear. We don't sell kids. We don't burn the market. We don't ask FDC for manners."

"Smuggler theology," Riku says, crooked tooth flashing. "I speak a dialect of it. Soft boy, you keep your sticky mouth for doors and turns. Speeches get stamped. Even in code towns."

We leave the boarding room with the landlady's eyes already forgetting our faces — the best gift Ashiro offered. She looked at our prison soles yesterday and decided not to ask for names. Today she looks through us the way people look through weather they cannot afford to remember. Reed canal slap behind us. Brown lanterns ahead for one more block, then the road west where the air starts tasting like someone burned a ledger and forgot to apologize.

The road to Susuichi is ditch and truck ruts and a sky the color of unwashed metal. My legs have stopped believing in distance but they keep walking anyway — the same betrayal Quiet Field taught them, the same betrayal the bay door taught them when Mei rented a tech's living mind and Riku priced the tide. Feet do not get a vote. Feet get geometry.

Soft lockdown thins the farther we get from Quiet Field's polite fist; what replaces it is not freedom. It is indifference with teeth. Fences go slack. Signage stops pretending. The land looks like it has been argued over too many times to care who wins the next argument. A checkpoint cone line appears once — bored gray-green, paper theater. Two men with stamps and the expression of people who have already decided boredom is a kind of virtue.

I put my sticky useless mouth on the living mind with the stamp and somehow he decides waving us through was his idea.

The touch is not Echo. It is smaller — a lean, a suggestion, the town itself seeming to agree that three dusty irregulars are not worth the paperwork. His eyes glaze for half a second. His hand lifts. The cone line parts. Habit. Horror. Softness that survived zero by being too small to vent.

Riku watches me afterward like a man pricing a tool he doesn't trust and can't afford to throw away.

"That wasn't luck," he says quietly.

"It was luck," I say, too fast. Panic file. Soft shelf. *Yet.*

Mei snorts. "Luck doesn't open cone lines. Soft boys romanticize luck because owning a mouth feels like work. Walk."

We walk.

The morning thins into afternoon without ceremony. My prison soles collect new dust — redder here, iron in the dirt, the color of a land that has been scraped and scraped and never quite cleaned. Truck ruts fill with standing water the color of old tea. Once, a cargo hauler passes without slowing, tarp flapping, driver invisible behind soot-streaked glass. Nobody asks our names. Nobody needs to. Out here, names are what you spend when you reach a gate. Before the gate, you are just heat and movement and the shape of people who keep walking because stopping is a kind of confession.

By afternoon the air thickens — coal-sweet, diesel, grilled offal under a haze that never quite becomes fog or night. My throat tastes like someone is cooking debt over an open flame. The light goes flat. Not dark — flat, the way paper goes flat when too many stamps have pressed the same box. Susuichi announces itself as a bruise on the horizon: tarp roofs, warehouse teeth, a gate that opens for coin and closes for manners.

We reach the gate at the hour when honest markets pretend they are only selling dinner.

A boy with a scarf over his mouth asks what we swear before he asks our names. The scarf is gray with soot at the edges. His eyes are young and old at once — the Susuichi combination, age measured in codes broken and codes kept. Mei answers with Auntie Bazzu and a debt structure I only half follow — silence-for-hire, three nights, no Federation fonts, no Remnant flags on the door. The words come out of her like she has been rehearsing them since the corridor, since the east wing, since before the east wing, since a life where numbers were hers and names were someone else's filing system.

Riku adds a clause about fists that makes the scarf-boy grin like Tuesday. Partner sound. Partner grammar. The gate dialect I will never speak cleanly and do not need to speak cleanly because Mei and Riku speak it like they were born behind a tarp instead of behind Habu's inventory glass.

I add nothing. Soft boys who invent speeches at soot gates invent reports.

The scarf-boy leads us through a throat of warehouse shadow and tarp alley and the smell of wet cardboard cooking in diesel heat. Susuichi is louder than Ashiro and meaner and more honest about the meanness. Nobody pretends the soot is weather. Nobody pretends the silence is free. Kids invent games with bottle caps in alleys where the light is the color of a bruise that decided not to heal. A woman sells grilled offal from a cart and prices fear separately from meat. The market breathes like an animal that decided law was a joke and code was the only theology left.

Auntie Bazzu is a woman with vinegar hands and eyes that have priced every kind of fleeing. She sits in a room that smells like disinfectant losing a war against grease. Her table has a ledger — actual paper, actual columns, the kind of arrogance only smugglers can afford. She looks at Mei's scarred knuckles, Riku's crooked tooth, my apologetic face, and decides we are not selling children. That seems to be the entrance exam.

"Pay what you swear," she says. "Don't burn my market. Don't bring Silence in on a leash and pretend it's weather. Chinmoku already sniffs soot like dogs that forgot paper. Remnant rents tarp space like they invented debt. I rent to who pays. I sell who breaks code. Clear?"

"Clear," Mei says.

Riku clicks his tongue once. Partner sound. "Clear."

I nod. Nodding is not a Word. Nodding is a soft boy agreeing to a kitchen that is not Habu's and not Mira's and might still cook us if the debt turns.

She gives us a room above a tarp alley that smells like wet cardboard and metal that isn't only Echo. The floor tilts slightly — not enough to be dangerous, enough to remind you that Susuichi was built by people who cared more about cover than level. Through a gap in the plastic I see Susuichi's clearer edge — kids inventing a game with a bottle cap, soot-light turning their laughter into something almost like Ashiro's ordinary lie. Almost. Ordinary here wears a scarf and prices silence.

Riku maps exits: alley, roof lip, warehouse throat, gate we already paid. He does it without speaking, the way he always does — eyes moving, jaw set, crooked tooth worrying his lip when the geometry finds a gap that is also a trap. Mei counts tarp seams the way she counted corridor tallies. Her finger traces a split in the plastic, a patched join, a knot in the rope that holds the roof against wind that smells like coal and argument.

I sit on a crate and feel the soft shelf tip under my tongue without anyone asking me to speak — the town itself seems to lean toward agreement, toward rented help, toward mouths that make people decide things. Panic file. Charisma file. Pad *Stop!* file. Mira's two Voices like a knife drawer I still refuse to open. Twin metal under the tongue when I think of the louder boy — not twin metal like Echo. Twin metal like inheritance. Like a spoon that was broken in half and each half still remembers the shape of the whole.

He spent my confession through speakers until the building shook. I filed the shout under panic. Panic is getting expensive.

"Tomorrow we work the market," Mei says. "Earn keep. Learn code. Soft boy, if you rent a mouth, aim it at living doors we need. Not at your guilt."

"I know."

"You don't," she says, almost kind. Same line as the bay. True again. Kindness from Mei is always arithmetic — it counts the cost before it pays.

Riku fist-knocks my shoulder. "Survive a soot Tuesday."

"That's my line," I try, thin, and thin is still a sound, and sound is still not Habu's completed form.

He grins. Crooked tooth. Partner grin. "Borrow it. Pay later."

Outside, Susuichi breathes smog like an animal that decided law was a joke and code was the only theology left. The tarp alley rattles once when a truck passes — a long low sound like a throat clearing. Somewhere west, Remnant pine may be naming Mei as a flag. Somewhere a louder boy may be sharpening collect into a schedule. Somewhere a still man with a festival laugh may already like towns where *whom* has no ministry to hide behind. I do not turn toward any of them. Turning would be a kind of admission, and admission is a door I am not ready to rent.

We have earned a tarp room and a swear we have to mean. The crate is hard under me and the plastic gap shows soot-light and the bottle-cap game continues below with the seriousness children bring to small worlds they build because the large ones keep trying to stamp them flat.

Branch days tomorrow. Admission waiting under the soot whether I file it as luck or not.
