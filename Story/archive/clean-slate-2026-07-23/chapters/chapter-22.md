# Chapter 22 — The Hill
*POV: Haruki*

We enter through a kitchen story Machi invents and a servant who believes me too easily.

Dusk still clings to the service road when we reach the latch — mansion teeth bright above, pine resin already in the air like a promise we haven't earned. The story is simple on purpose: delivery, timing, a name that belongs to a favor owed to a bar that keeps its door open when verification language wants doors shut. My voice does the rest — soft, apologetic, the tone that makes people decide helping me is their idea. Stickiness. Riku hates the ease. "Stop being sticky," he mutters, as a side latch yields and a hallway rearranges itself around a boy who still apologizes to train passes in his sleep. I hate it too. Stickiness is a word I don't have yet for a power I won't own. Tonight it gets us into a mansion that smells like polished wood and quiet money — the same smell, I think wildly, that blood under a market nose walked out of yesterday while Mira punctuated his shoulder and patrol gray-green cut the road into lanes we couldn't cross. Last night I held a breath across lanterns. Tonight I spend the breath on a threshold. The ledger accepts both entries if I don't shake the page.

Gorō's tram-memory holds in my ear like a driver's window that never closed. Service corridor with laundry damp and soap that costs less than the front stairs. A stair captains don't watch because pretty doors are theater and theater is where gray-green stands too straight. A garden wing where koi pretend politics don't exist under lantern light that makes everything look like a celebration for someone who collects boys. We move like people who have rehearsed exits without a stage. Riku's hand signals are economy — fist, two fingers, a chin-cut toward a turn. My job is to be believed at thresholds. I am disgusting at my job and grateful and sick about both. Belief opens doors. Fear opens them too. I have been borrowing both without naming either since Machi's kitchen phone went quiet and the dusk made the mansion lights look like teeth.

We find him in a side parlor with a bruise on his cheek and his cardigan buttoned wrong, arguing with a captain about tram schedules like captivity is a meeting agenda. Ginger-root hands. Gearbox eyes bright with insult. The empty stool at Minato was a wound; this is the man who refuses to be the wound's shape. The captain has gray-green manners and a face that thinks inventory is mercy. Gorō is teaching him that mercy can be a timetable argument if you have forty years of depot logic and one beer of dignity left.

"Tell Machi I walked," he says when he sees us, eyes bright. "I'm not a package."

"You're walking now," Riku says, and cuts him free of the polite restraint with a knife and an exit plan that starts at the parlor door and ends in pines. No speech. No hero temperature. Just the arithmetic of a Tuesday that decided to grow teeth. I mouth *Machi* once, uselessly, like a stamp. Gorō nods as if stamps still run the world. Maybe in his chest they do.

We are almost out when the house changes temperature.

Not a draft. Not a door. A shift in the polished air, the way soft lockdown shifts a street without sirens — expensive quiet becoming something with a direction. A shot that isn't a shot — a body-sound from the garden wing, the soft wrongness of a person becoming furniture. Then feet that are not ours: Mira's runners moving like punctuation through a hallway that suddenly has too many directions. Then a voice I would know at the bottom of a river, flatter than it should be, saying something about extract and pad and *move*. The flatness is new. The flatness is a door. My stomach files it before my heart can refuse the entry. Somewhere under the flatness is the boy who called me a personality disorder on a Rail Loop bench. Somewhere under the flatness is blood and a schedule.

Kaito rounds the corner with blood under his nose and eyes that have scheduled something. Dark jacket. Nick through the eyebrow catching lantern spill from a side window. Mira's mild weather gathering at the edge of the frame even before she arrives fully — blunt hair, level gaze, the anti-warmth that once said *it wasn't your fault* and meant *become useful*. He sees Gorō. Sees Riku. Sees me. For a fraction of a second the nick is just a nick again, and we are on a Rail Loop bench, and wet hair is a joke, and the war is a screen we turn face-down, and *let* is slang with no teeth — and then the fraction dies the way fractions have been dying since a roof learned white sky.

"Haru," he says. Not soft. Not loud. A location.

"Kaito." My ledger fails. Columns tip. Numbers that used to keep rooms from fists scatter across polished wood. "What did you do?"

His mouth twitches. Almost a grin. Almost a flinch. The almosts are getting rarer on him; I can see the scarcity the way you see a bruise under a sleeve. "Freed a city."

Behind him, through an open garden door, I see a chair and a man who will not stand up again, tea cold beside a hand that learned obedience. Civilian cardigan. Mild as filing. Koi circling like the grammar never reached the water. Aihara — the name Gorō spit once before Machi shut the hill-talk down with one word; the filing hand; the spreadsheet with gardens; the uncle who files cities. Premeditated — I can read it on Kaito the way I used to read bruises on his forearms after fights he started so other people wouldn't have to. Not collision. Calendar. The boy who collided with *kill yourself* under a plum tree has learned to write the clause in advance. Free as in erase. The Word heard him. The city will breathe thinner or easier; I can't tell which from a corpse and a flat voice.

I move without a plan. Mid-lunge. Not a punch with a thesis. A body deciding that a best friend does not get to become a schedule in a garden room while I watch. The distance between us is a hallway and a lifetime and half a breath from a market that already taught us we couldn't cross. Riku swears — low, vicious — and grabs Gorō, already choosing pines over speeches, already reading the exit that keeps an old man from becoming collateral in a boys' war. Mira appears like weather at Kaito's shoulder, mildness sharpening into extract math.

"Don't," Kaito says to me — unfinished on purpose or by habit — and my body hiccups, stalls, refuses the lunge for half a heartbeat that feels like betrayal from the inside.

Not persuasion. Not fear. A soft shelf I live on tipping — hands mid-reach, knees arguing with a verb that didn't finish naming its object. *Don't* — don't what? Don't move? Don't breathe? Don't be Haru in a hallway that has already filed me as in the way? The stall is half a heartbeat and a year. Metal taste blooms where it shouldn't. I don't own that. I file it under panic. Under chase. Under the stubborn ledger that will not sit when told.

Then I tear free of whatever soft shelf that was — panic, refusal, love with fists — and the moment becomes a chase: stairs that smell like laundry and money, servants scattering like papers in wind, gray-green waking up from polite inventory into boots and shouts, rotors already waking on the roof like an argument clearing its throat. The house shakes like a held breath. Or I shake. Or both.

"Walk," Riku tells Gorō, and shoves him toward a side exit that smells like pine resin and wet earth. Gorō looks back once — bruise, wrong buttons, eyes bright — and goes, because walking was the dignity he asked for. *Tell Machi I walked.* The only clean exit of the night flees into trees while we — Riku and I — go up, because the loud boy is going up, and ledgers follow heat even when the heat burns, even when the heat has learned to schedule deaths and call it freedom.

On the stair I taste metal that isn't mine. Or is. I don't know. Knowing would be owning, and owning would mean accepting that the stickiness that opened the kitchen door and the stall that froze my lunge are dialects of the same mouth that killed a lieutenant with a calendar. I will not accept that cleanly. Not on a stair. Not while Kaito's flatter voice is still ahead of me saying pad and move like the mansion is a sentence he can finish.

Riku clicks his tongue once, grim, crooked tooth bared, blood already starting somewhere from a scrape I didn't see him earn. "Survive a Tuesday, soft boy."

"That's my line," I try, and it comes out thinner than joke, thinner than dignity, thin as the coin a still man left when he asked *reshaped by whom?* and I said *I don't know yet*. Tonight *whom* is upstairs with rotors. Tonight *whom* has blood under his nose and a corpse in a garden and Mira's hand on the extract path. Tonight I am still stuck on the kitchen door — except the kitchen door opened, and the hill took us in, and the only fight left faces him instead of a map.

I run. Toward him. Toward the grammar. Toward the only fight I've ever wanted to lose without anyone dying for the privilege — knowing already that someone has died in a chair behind us, and the privilege is spent, and the fight is still the only door that faces him instead of opposite shores.

Above us, a pad. A humming I can almost hear through rotor-wash that hasn't fully started. A clash that hasn't started and has already started in the chest, before the fists, before the Words, before the city learns how to shake.
