# Chapter 8 — The Other Side
*POV: Haruki*

Afterward the house was all shutters.

Sugi moved through the kitchen like grief was a guest she had hosted before and refused to offer a second cup. Boards wiped. Cups stacked. The kettle filled and set to heat for nobody in particular. The arcade cat watched from the shelf with its one milky eye, birthday still stuck in the plastic, nine hundred yen of a joke that had outlived its punchline. Outside, FDC asked questions with the bored patience of men filling forms — had we seen irregular activity, had we hosted unregistered travelers, had we heard anyone give unusual orders. Their gray-green filled the lane and then thinned. Boots on wet stones. A loudspeaker that had already done its polite knife work and was now doing paperwork.

I said no in the soft voice that makes people stop pushing. Soft is my weather. Soft is how I survive rooms with rifles. Soft is also how I failed under a bank of vending machines when a sentence might have mattered. One soldier asked again, slower, like volume could find a different answer; I gave him the same no with manners on it, and his partner made a note that meant nothing and everything. The soldiers looked at my face, decided I was furniture that apologized, and moved on to the next doorway. Sugi did not look at me while they were there. Looking would have been a tell. When they were gone she exhaled once, sharp, and went back to wiping a board that was already clean.

Sato arrived while they were still in the lane, leftover stamps in her good hand, sling white against dark cloth, split-lip anger that had finally chosen a direction: away from Mira. She smelled like alley cardboard and boiled cloth. The arm wound from the unfinished *Don't—* was a fact she wore without asking for pity.

"They went inland before night," she said. No greeting. No ranking glance this time — she'd already ranked me and found me insufficient, or maybe she'd ranked the whole saint-and-list economy and found it wanting. "Toward Kuroishi. If you're stupid enough to follow — stop him if you can." She almost laughed. The almost had blood in it. "I can't. I'm done being useful to saints with lists."

She laid paper machinery on the table that would clear my name for one checkpoint and one day — stamps, a route scrap, the kind of ordinary violence that lets a body walk past a cone line without becoming a file. Then she looked at the cat, looked at me, and left Mira's work the way you leave a burning room: without packing, without speech, without goodbye.

The FDC men took her stamps as cooperation and left with nothing that mattered. Sugi washed the table twice. The water in the basin went cloudy and then clear and then cloudy again, like the house was trying to decide whether cleanliness was a rite or a joke.

---

Hanae came at dusk with dust in her hair and hands that shook before she spoke.

Older than Jun by a handful of years. Refugee dust on her sleeves. Eyes that had already spent their screaming somewhere I wasn't invited. Behind her, the widow-aunt with sunken eyes held her elbow the way Sugi had held my wrist on the flats — hard, practical, a tether against the tide. The lane behind them still smelled faintly of plum leaves and something metallic the rain hadn't finished arguing with.

"Did he suffer?" Hanae asked.

I looked at her face and understood that the true sentence — Stop loop, trust-look, muddy clauses, *Kill yourself*, the other body learning faster than refusal — was not a currency she could spend. Practical love wants facts that fit in a kitchen. Soft lies are my native language. I hated that it made me useful.

"It was quick," I said.

A soft lie. The kind I am built for. She nodded once, spent it, and went back into the lane with her widow-aunt's hand on her elbow. Jun's absence followed them like a third set of footsteps that never quite landed. I stood in the doorway until I couldn't hear them, then longer, because ledgers don't know when to close. The east was a bruise again. The port pretended it was only fish. Somewhere a radio muttered FRONT STABLE for an audience of gulls.

Sugi set tea beside me. Steam. Umeboshi sharpness still ghosting the air from a jar left open. "You don't get to carry both boys," she said. "One is walking. One is gone. Pick the work that still has feet."

"I should have—"

"Should is a religion for people with spare rooms," she said. "You have a road or a shelf. Choose."

I wanted to argue that ledgers can hold more than one column. Instead I drank the tea. It tasted like someone had decided the world was still allowed to be warm in small rooms, which was either mercy or mockery. Possibly both. Outside, a gull screamed once and then thought better of it. The cat's milky eye did not blink. Plastic doesn't absolve.

---

That night Sugi packed onigiri without asking if I was leaving.

Rice, salt, a scrap of pickled plum tucked into the center like a small red judgment. She wrapped them in cloth the way widows wrap rites — neat, unsentimental, aware the sea takes what it takes. Outside, the shutters held the dark. Inside, the house made its night noises: kettle ticking cool, wind under the eaves, distant diesel like a heartbeat that belonged to a town still pretending.

"You're going to ask me not to," I said.

"I'm going to tell you the door opens both ways," she said. "And that cats don't travel well. And that if you find him, you don't get to bring me a third missing person to count."

She held my wrist the way she had on the flats. Hard. Tide-flat hard. Then she let go. That was the blessing. That was also the refusal to stop me. Coast love: tether, then release, then the work that still has feet.

I left the arcade cat on the shelf. One milky eye. Birthday. Nine hundred yen. Ryn's joke about cheating. Some remains stay where the rite can still find them; some boys leave so the shelf has someone to keep. I touched the pale patch behind my own ear once, private, useless — the match I'd seen on Kaito in a swaying train dark and filed next to a soldier's grip opening — and did not say the matching word aloud even to an empty kitchen. Evidence wants a verdict. The kitchen was not a court. The road would not be either.

Bag light. Shoes. Sato's paper in the inner pocket like a one-day ghost. Onigiri in the cloth. The entryway sand took my footprints and did not ask for a third set.

---

Dawn on the inland road smelled like dust instead of salt.

The first kilometer still carried harbor damp in the weeds; by the third, the wet was gone and the dust had claimed my laces the way Haiga ash once had. My bag was light. My phone had no signal worth the name — war app tones delayed into silence, FRONT STABLE a rumor from a dead font. I ate half an onigiri without tasting it and wrapped the rest because Sugi's packing was a rite and rites deserve finishing. Behind me Nagihama was a port that had already done its part: nets, widows, a shrine, a plum tree, a split that had finally finished splitting. Ahead, Kuroishi was a direction Sato had thrown like a stone. Ridge. Shutters. Mira's list. A boy with a ruined voice learning how to finish clauses before the world finished them for him.

A checkpoint cone line waited where the road narrowed — gray-green, bored, the day's first test of Sato's leftover stamps. I handed the paper over with the same soft voice. The soldier looked at my face, looked at the stamp, and waved me through like weather. One day. One clearance. After that the ledger would have to invent new luck.

I thought about a soldier's hand opening on a roof. A cart on a dock and empty hands blinking at their own betrayal. An unfinished *Don't—* turning a girl into a statue long enough for a wild shot to find her arm. A pier Tuesday Haru hadn't been told clean. A sea-wall night when he'd said *not tonight* and I'd hated how relieved I sounded. A plum tree. Papers. Mira's open palm finally closing. Kaito's shoulder turning inland half a degree, then all the degrees. My own silence under the vending bank, still unpaid. The still man in the coat, watching empty hands like tide tables, picking up a gun to read it and setting it down exactly where it fell.

This is where staying ends in the only way that matters: I put my feet on the road and I followed him.

Not to save him. Not yet. I didn't have that sentence. Soft boys don't get clean hero verbs on day one; we get dust and the stubborn decision not to let the absences become a grave with only one name on it.

West. Always west of the thing that burned. Inland of the water we used to stand on together — flats shining like hammered tin, hands open and closed, the cat going out alone because he couldn't. Somewhere ahead a boy with a sanded throat was nodding yes to a hollow new room. Somewhere behind, Sugi's house held shutters and an empty shelf and a guest named grief who would not get a second cup.

The sun cleared the ridge. My shoes took on a new dust. Checkpoint paper sat against my ribs like a short-lived permission. I did not look back at the sea. Looking back is how unfinished clauses start.

We were not on the same side of the water.

I walked anyway.
