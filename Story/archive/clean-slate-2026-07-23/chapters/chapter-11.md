# Chapter 11 — The Summons
*POV: Kaito*

The summons isn't a letter. It's a debt wearing a woman's voice.

Mira takes the call on a stolen handset in a Kuroishi bus shelter that smells like rain and fried batter. Evening. The schedule board still lies in tidy characters. A croquette cart's grease hangs in the wet air like a second weather. She doesn't put the call on speaker. She doesn't need to. Her face does the translation: the elder who pulled her out of an FDC fire once, the one voice she can't refuse, calling in a rescue that isn't a heist.

I watch from the shelter bench with my hands empty and my throat already bracing. Mira's mildness usually sits on her like a mask on a hook. Tonight the mask slips for the length of a held breath — younger, rawer, a girl who still owes someone for being alive. Then she nods once at the air, says "Yes," like the word costs rent, and hangs up.

She looks younger for half a second more. Then the mildness comes back.

"Takamatsu," she says. "Hospital stack. Quiet floors. They have Suzumori — the Bellkeeper. Amatsu memory on two legs. If the Federation ships her east, we lose the only person who can read what you carry."

"I carry guilt," I say.

"You carry a throne someone split in half and hid in ordinary boys," Mira says, too calm. Rain ticks the shelter roof. Fried batter ghost in the wet air. "Don't flinch at the furniture. We need the woman who knows which rooms are real."

I think of the pale patch behind my ear. Haru never mentioned his. I never asked. Asking would have been a sentence with an ending, and endings are what I keep failing at — Ryn off a roof, Jun under a plum, Haru on the wrong side of an aunt's arm. A throne. The word wants to be ridiculous. It lands like metal anyway. In the shelter glass my face looks like someone practicing to be a weapon and failing to look like anything else.

"Why does she answer to you?" I ask. Meaning the elder. Meaning the debt.

Mira's eyes don't soften. "She doesn't answer to me. I answer to her. There's a difference. Learn it before you start confusing obedience with love."

I almost laugh. It comes out wrong — sanded throat, no humor left that isn't edged. "I'm not confusing them."

"You are," Mira says. "Every time you wait for the soft boy to absolve you. Absolution isn't a living mind you can command. Move."

Rain thickens on the shelter roof. A bus that is not ours sighs past in gray-green indifference. I stand because sitting would invent another argument I cannot finish clean.

---

We pack light. Mira's runners argue about routes in the dried-goods nest — ridge road versus depot bus versus a farmer's truck that might remember how to be kind. I don't join. Routes are just unfinished fights with geography. Someone presses a rice ball into my hand. Someone else avoids looking at my mouth. The radio lies about FRONT STABLE in the Daigo font and nobody bothers to turn it off. In the back room a girl sharpens a pencil to write another name that doesn't shake; she glances at me once and I see the calculation: *useful / dangerous / temporary*. Fair. I am all three.

"Bellkeeper," I say, testing the word while Mira checks a forged transfer strip under bad light. "Why call her that."

"Because she kept bells for rooms," Mira says, not looking up. "Amatsu memory. Which corridors were real. Which names still answered. FDC calls her an asset. The elder calls her a debt I owe in full." She finally looks at me. Mild. Certain. "You call her the woman who can tell you what your mouth is for when grief stops being a good enough reason."

"Grief is the reason."

"Grief is the fuel," Mira says. "Reasons are what you tell soft boys so they don't stop you. Eat. Then practice. Then bus."

I eat. The rice tastes like charcoal and obedience. Before we leave the ridge, Mira puts a clean sentence in front of me like a bowl of rice.

"Say it."

The nest's night watch stands at the side gate we already own. He would have opened it for her without my voice. That is not the point. Practice. Echo. Blood. The lesson that small work keeps the blade from remembering only the plum tree.

"Open the side gate and go home," I tell him.

He does. Eyes empty, then full of bed. The gate yawns on wet dark. My nose paints its thin loyal line. Mira's cloth is ready before I am. She wipes, studies the red like weather data, and nods.

"Good," she says. "Takamatsu has more doors. More living minds. You'll like the harbor. People think water makes them safe."

"Do I like things now?" I ask.

"You like not losing," she says. "I'm giving you a job that looks like that."

Not losing. The vow in its stolen clothes. I follow her into the rain because staying would mean inventing a different sentence, and I have used up the part of me that invents soft ones.

---

The overnight bus smells like damp coats and orange peel and the particular resignation of people traveling because staying became more expensive. Fluorescent strips flicker in a rhythm that wants to be sleep and fails. A child asleep against a grandmother's shoulder. Two gray-green near the front with the bored posture of men who are not on duty hard enough to matter yet — mint optional, clipboards asleep in their laps. An old man peels the orange one segment at a time like the war can be managed if he just concentrates. Mira takes a window seat and puts me on the aisle like luggage that might need to speak. My scraped throat tickles with unused sentences. I swallow them. Paper practice in my pocket: who, what, finished — useless here unless I want the whole bus to stand up and walk into traffic.

Three rows back, a man in a coat too warm for the aisle sits and does not sleep.

Unremarkable face. Still. The same still from the dried-goods shop — motion declined, as if he had reviewed the evening and found nothing worth participating in. He does not look at his phone. He does not look out the window. He looks at the space where sentences happen: mouths, air, the half-second before sound becomes meaning.

When I almost turn to tell him to stop watching, Mira's fingers bite my wrist hard enough to bruise. Same bruise map as Kuroishi. She is writing a warning into my skin.

"Don't finish a sentence he can hear," she breathes. "Some hunters rewrite."

Rewrite. Another word for the private list. Edit. Rewrite. Chinmoku or worse. I face forward and taste metal and keep my mouth shut all the way through the mountain dark. The bus groans. The child whimpers once and settles. Rain becomes mist becomes the thinner dark of a city approaching. I count my own breaths the way Haru would count other people's moods — inventory against panic — and every breath wants to become a command: *sleep, look away, forget, get off, leave us alone.*

I do not finish any of them.

Mira's shoulder against mine is not comfort. It is a closed parenthesis. Stay inside it. Do not give the coat a draft to revise.

When the bus windows finally fill with harbor dark and a hill of polite lights, the coat-man is still awake. Still still. He stands when we stand, not following exactly, only existing in the same geometry like a coincidence that knows your name. On the platform the air changes — diesel, salt pretending to be friends, the thin metal smell of a city that files people. Mira steers me into a stream of night workers and does not look back. I do not look back either. Looking would be unfinished. Behind us, somewhere in the crowd's rearrange, the warm coat is a gap where a person used to be priced.

Takamatsu. Tram lines sleeping under polite lamps. A hospital stack waiting somewhere in that geometry like a held breath — glass and concrete and quiet floors where an old woman hums because humming is how she checks which rooms are still real. So does a mansion on a hill, landlord-bright. So does, if the world still has a sense of humor, a soft boy with a ledger and dust in his teeth, holding instead of chasing because that is the only spine left that isn't a sprint.

We walk waterfront streets that smell like blackout tape before we even reach the room. Mira's mildness returns full: debt filed, map already unfolding behind her eyes. A runner meets us with keys and no questions. Somewhere a karaoke tower murders a song for nobody. Somewhere gray-green holds a corner like furniture.

I don't hope. Hope is an unfinished clause. I check the scrape in my throat and follow Mira into a city that doesn't know it's about to learn my grammar — living minds only, doors that open because people open them, a Bellkeeper humming on quiet floors I have not yet walked.

"Tomorrow we recon," Mira says. "Tonight you sleep facing the wall. Habit. Don't invent the extract in your head before you've seen the building. Boys who invent rescues write muddy sentences."

"I'm not inventing," I say.

"You're grieving," she says. "Same risk. Sleep."

In the room I wash my face in a sink that tastes faintly of rust and try not to see Jun's trust-look in the mirror. Fail. The pale patch behind my ear is a private useless fact. I touch it once and stop. Asking Haru about his would have been a sentence with an ending. We never ended that one. We ended everything else instead.

I sleep facing the wall in damp dark. Outside, polite lights hold the hill. Somewhere under them, if the ledger still runs, Haru is still walking. I do not say his name into the dark.

I say nothing at all. For once, silence is the cleanest clause I have.
