# Chapter 3 — Low Tide
*POV: Haruki*

My hand was over Kaito's eyes before he could see the street.

Not because he asked. Because his feet had stopped working the second we came out through the place where she'd landed, and somebody had to steer. I kept my own eyes open. One of us had to count curbs. How we got past Kanzaki's men — smoke, a service corridor, the near explosion that ate the east stairwell — is the other story. Later. What matters is that by the time the river of people found the coast road, we were already in it, ash on our shoes and the east behind us the color I will now always mean when I say *east*.

I won't describe the landing. I know what Kaito did when he saw it, and what sound he didn't make. That stays mine.

---

By dawn we were part of the river.

Not the screaming crush you'd imagine — a slow gray current, the whole west-fleeing population of a dying city reduced to the pace of its most tired member. Grandmothers with shopping carts full of photographs. Salarymen still in work shoes. A man carrying a birdcage with great care and nothing else. Children who had stopped crying because crying takes energy you eventually run out of. The air smelled of ash and diesel and food left behind in open shops. I looked down hours in and saw the ash worked into my laces. Haiga dust. The exact gray Ryn had pointed out on a frightened boy's sneakers four days ago — *you can't wash it out* — and now it was on both our shoes.

Kaito didn't talk. Not sulking. Structural. He had always been the volume of us, and something in him had been switched off at the roof lip. When he did speak — twice, to ask if I was okay, in that cracked half-a-radio voice the roof had left him — it cost him visibly, and he'd touch his throat afterward like a man checking a wound he keeps forgetting he has. Blood dried under his nose in a thin brown crescent. I wiped it once with my sleeve; he let me; neither of us commented.

I couldn't stop counting. Hours. Kilometers. The thing neither of us had said since the vending bank — *why did the hand open* — that we both walked around now the way you walk around a hole in a floor in the dark. Every time someone ahead of us said *let me past* I felt Kaito flinch beside me. I flinched too. Our bodies had learned faster than our minds.

We caught a train in the end. A local two-car thing, half its lights out, that a railway man in a filthy vest was running up the branch line into the hills because, he said, spitting off the platform, somebody ought to. Diesel and wet wool and too many people. In the crush I got pushed up against Kaito, closer than we'd stood since, and his collar had torn open, and that was when I saw it.

Behind his left ear, at the soft place where the jaw begins, a small pale patch. Lighter than the skin around it. Irregular as a thumbprint left by something that wasn't a thumb. I have one too. Same place. Same side. My mother used to touch mine when I was small, absently, the way you touch a freckle you don't think about. There was a reason to notice it now. I didn't have the reason. I stood in the swaying dark and did not say *Kaito, look, we match*, because some part of me had started, without permission, noticing things about the two of us that were beginning not to add up. This was one of them. Silent. Unshared. Sitting beside a soldier's grip opening on command and a lieutenant who'd said *do not listen to a single word he says.*

---

After that — Sugi, you know this part.

Salt before the harbor. Diesel. Fish. Tide-line rot-sweetness and woodsmoke. Something in my chest unclenched a single notch when I couldn't hear the low third tone anymore. Gulls over water like the east had never happened.

You opened the door and counted our feet.

Two. Not three. You didn't make us say it. You said, "Come inside," and fed us rice and umeboshi and tea, and you set out a third bowl by habit and put it away without comment. That small motion nearly undid me more than the road had.

"Eat," you said. "Talk later. Or never. Both are allowed."

Kaito's hands shook around the chopsticks. He ate anyway.

And Mira — you were in this kitchen when she came. Same table. Same late hour. I don't mean the girl. I mean the woman with the burn-scar on her forearm who looked at me once, filed me under *finished*, and locked on him. You saw your own mouth go thin. You didn't throw her out. You didn't offer her a second cup. She told him it wasn't his fault — the sentence I hadn't given him under the machines — and he said *Say that again*, hungry, and permission sat under the grief where I could feel it growing teeth.

---

My voice ran out.

"That's what happened," I said. Useless. True.

She nodded once. Not absolution. Just hearing it out loud.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Low tide. Grief needs a surface. We'll give her one."

"I know."

"Good." She stood and set the kettle back. "Sleep if you can. Or don't. Both are allowed."

I sat until the kettle stopped ticking. Outside, Nagihama did port things in the dark.


---

The next day Sugi puts a folded square of white cloth on the table. That is the whole announcement. Widows come with a paper lantern, salt, a bottle of clear liquor with no label. They build a rite the coast has needed before — for boys who went east and became a name on a scrolling ribbon — and they do it with their hands, the way Sugi builds a meal: out of whatever is left that grief hasn't taken.

Kaito is the one who brings what we will give the sea.

He comes in from the entryway holding something in both hands the way I held the cake box a lifetime ago, careful like it might wake, and sets it on the table next to the cloth. The hideous cat. Arcade. Birthday. Nine hundred yen and my accidental win. Crushed flat down one side, stiff with dried salt, its one remaining plastic eye gone milky. I have no idea — none — that any of that night survived this far.

"She gave it back to me," he says. "On the roof. Before." His jaw works. "Said it was too ugly to be hers. Said I'd won it fair, technically, because you cheated." A sound tries to be a laugh and dies before it gets a third of the way. "I put it in my jacket. I don't remember doing it. It was just there after."

So that is what we have. A nine-hundred-yen cat with one milky eye. That is all of her that made it out of the city, and it made it out in the pocket of the boy who cannot forgive himself, and if you told me the world designed it that way on purpose I would not argue.

Low tide at Nagihama pulls the sea back a long way, over flats that shine like hammered tin under a sky going lilac and gray. We walk out onto them at dusk — widows, Sugi, Kaito, me, the salt-stiff cat riding on white cloth in Sugi's careful hands — and our feet leave prints the sea will take back within the hour, which feels, tonight, like the entire point. The light is old brass. Salt finds every cut I earned on the road and didn't notice until now. Out east, even here, that low uneven glow along the edge of the world — the war going on being the war, indifferent.

The widows sing something low and old that I don't know and will never find again. One of them says, gentle, ancient, meaning nothing by it: *now we let her go, now we let her go home to the water* — and the words go into Kaito like a blade, clean, barely a sound. I flinch too. Two nights of walking taught our bodies to fear those exact words, and here they are dressed up in kindness at the edge of the sea, and neither of us has the language yet to explain why they are unbearable.

Sugi holds out the folded cloth with the cat on it. To both of us. An offering, and a question.

I open my hands.

Kaito's stay closed. Fisted. White at the knuckles. "I can't," he says, the ruined radio voice cracking clean through. "I'm never — I'm never doing it again."

He means the letting go. He means the words. He means both and neither, and whatever vow is forming in his mouth is already the wrong shape. I set the cat on the water alone. The tide takes it — first a hesitation, then a pull. Sugi reaches back without looking and holds my wrist, hard, and does not let go until the milky eye is only a rumor of white on dark water.

On the sea wall above the flats, Mira watches. She does not come down. She does not call. She looks at Kaito's closed hands the way a person looks at a tool they have been waiting to pick up, and for a second — only a second — something almost like tenderness crosses her face. Then she turns inland and leaves us for now.
