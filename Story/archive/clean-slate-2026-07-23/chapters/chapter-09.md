# Chapter 9 — Inland
*POV: Kaito*

The road inland doesn't care who you killed to get on it.

Dust takes the salt out of your clothes by the second switchback. By the third, even Jun's blood-smell is only imagination, and imagination is a liar with good manners. Mira walks like someone who has already spent the map — not fast, not slow, just finished with the idea that roads negotiate. I match her pace because falling behind means thinking, and thinking still has Jun's trust-look in it, and Haru's face on the wrong side of Sugi's arm, and a soldier's body learning a new instruction faster than a person can refuse.

My throat is a scraped wire. When I speak, people lean in. When I don't, the quiet feels like a loaded chamber.

"Kuroishi first," Mira says. Not a question. Wind frets the burn-scar pale on her forearm; she doesn't cover it. "Then the city that thinks it's safe because it has a harbor and a hill."

"Takamatsu," I guess, because the way she says *hill* has a landlord in it.

She almost smiles. Almost is the only smile she spends. "Good. You're listening past the nouns."

We don't talk about Nagihama. We don't talk about plum trees. Mira's runners peel off at a ridge fork with a nod that means *radio if the gray-green thickens*, and then it is just her and me and a road that smells like hot rubber and cedar and the kind of hunger that has stopped being dramatic. I keep wanting to ask if Haru followed. I keep not asking. Asking would be an unfinished clause. Mira has taught me what those cost.

At a roadside stand that sells tea in paper cups and lies about oil prices, she buys two cups and puts one in my hand without looking at me.

"Drink," she says. Clean. Who. What. Finished — but aimed at me, and for a stupid half-second my body almost obeys like a stranger's. Then I understand she is only talking, and the shame of almost is worse than the burn in my throat.

"I'm not a tool you practice on," I say.

"You're a boy who opens doors with living minds," Mira says, mild. "Drink so you don't faint on the ridge and make me carry you. I'm not sentimental about luggage."

I drink. The tea tastes like smoke and patience. Somewhere east, a soft boy is probably still standing in a kitchen that smells like onigiri and refusal. I do not let myself turn around.

---

Kuroishi is a ridge town with too many shutters and a shrine that smells like old smoke. Black stone gate. Main street of tool shops that closed their mouths when the war learned inland. A croquette cart that has never moved in its life. Clipboard gray-green at a bend that is not quite a checkpoint — just enough uniform to make refugees flatten into ditches when they pass.

Mira's people keep a nest behind a dried-goods shop: mattresses, a radio that lies in the same font as the Daigo screen, a list of names written in a hand that doesn't shake. They look at me the way you look at a new knife — useful, dangerous, not yet trusted to stay in the drawer. One girl with a sling-white memory in her eyes — not Sato; Sato is done — stares at my mouth like it might go off without warning. Fair.

Mira puts me on small work before I can invent a speech about guilt.

A watchman who won't open a gate. Middle-aged, tired, key on a string under his shirt. We meet him at dusk by a side alley that smells like drainage and fried batter. Mira stands where he can see she is not alone and not impressed.

"Open the gate and go home," I tell him, clean. Who. What. Finished.

He does. Eyes empty for a beat — nobody home — then fill with the ordinary urge to sleep in his own bed. The key turns. The gate yawns. He walks away without looking at us, already rearranging the evening into something that will let him live with himself. My nose paints a thin line on my wrist. Echo. Scraped-wire burn climbing my throat like a tide that only goes one direction.

Mira nods once, as if I've completed a worksheet.

"Again," she says, about the clerk.

The clerk is a woman behind a stamp window who won't put her seal on a paper that would move a crate of batteries without a form. Fluorescent hum. Ink on her fingers. The kind of face that has said *no* so many times *no* has become her spine. I stand at the counter with Mira's forged request and feel Haru's ledger watching me from a hundred kilometers of dust.

"Stamp it and take your break," I say. Cleaner than I want to be. Bounded. No spare words.

Her hand moves. The stamp falls. Wet red. She stands, hangs the CLOSED sign with the mild confusion of someone remembering a dream, and leaves us with a paper that will feed Mira's runners for three days. Echo again — thicker this time, copper taste under the tongue. Mira wipes my face with a cloth like she's polishing a tool. I let her. Letting her is easier than asking anyone to look at me as a boy.

"Again," she says, about the drunk FDC private who won't forget a face.

That one is harder. Not because the sentence is hard. Because he wears gray-green, and gray-green still smells like mint and boredom and a shot under a plum tree. We find him outside a pachinko parlor that has learned to serve beer after curfew if you know which door. He laughs when he sees Mira. Remembers her from some shed raid that went wrong for his side. His hand starts toward a radio.

"Forget her face. Forget mine. Finish your drink and sleep," I say, and hate how the grammar arrives fully built now, like a blade that has stopped pretending it was brought for some other reason.

He blinks. Laughs once more, emptier. Turns back to his glass. By the time we leave the alley his eyes slide over Mira like she is weather. My nose won't stop. Mira's cloth is already red at the corner.

"Good," she says. Not praise. Inventory. "You're learning to finish before the world does."

I hate that it gets easier. I hate that easier feels like relief. Relief is the trap with nice manners. On the roof I opened a hand because I was weak and loud. In the lane I closed a life because I was desperate and finally clear. Mira calls both *necessary*. My body calls both *cost*. The vow still sits wrong in my mouth: *never lose* wearing *never again*'s clothes, and every clean sentence is a stitch that pulls the clothes tighter.

Between jobs Mira makes me write the sentences down before I speak them. Paper. Pencil. Who. What. Bounds. No spare words. My handwriting looks like someone else's — tighter than the boy who used to scrawl soccer scores on Ryn's stall napkins. Mira reads, crosses out a loophole I didn't see, and hands it back.

"You're still leaving exits in the grammar," she says. "Exits are how the world escapes you. Finish."

I finish on paper. Then I finish out loud on a runner who volunteered to be practiced on, eyes closed, jaw set, as if bravery could make obedience kinder. It doesn't. He opens a crate, counts to ten, sits. Echo. He opens his eyes afterward and will not look at my mouth. I don't blame him. I wouldn't look either.

At night I lie on a mattress that isn't mine and practice not saying Haru's name. The nest breathes around me — someone snoring, someone cleaning a radio crystal, someone writing another name that doesn't shake. Outside, shrine smoke and ridge cold. Inside, the radio lies about CONTAINED in the Daigo font until someone finally kills the volume. I sleep facing the wall. Habit. Somewhere behind me, on a different road, a soft boy is probably apologizing to dust. I don't let myself hope he's following. Hope is another unfinished clause.

---

On the third evening a man in a coat too warm for the ridge stands across from the dried-goods shop and does not buy anything.

Unremarkable face. Still the way people aren't still — not frozen, just *decided*, as if motion were optional and he had declined. Shoppers bend around him without noticing they are bending. He looks at me. Then at Mira. Then he shows a phone screen for half a second — not filming, showing — a frame of nothing that matters until you realize the nothing includes my mouth mid-sentence from yesterday's gate, captured from an angle that means someone was already hunting grammar.

He walks away before either of us can pretend it was nothing.

My throat wants a sentence. *Stop. Come here. Drop the phone. Explain—*

Mira's fingers bite my wrist hard enough to bruise. "Don't."

"Chinmoku?" I ask, because Mira has used the word once like a weather warning, soft as a shut door.

"Or worse," she says. Eyes on the empty place where the coat was. "Don't speak near him. Don't finish a sentence he can edit."

Edit. The word lands wrong. I file it next to *Word* from Kanzaki's radio and don't ask for the dictionary yet. Asking would make it real in a way that still feels like betrayal of a birthday cake and a girl who hated the pickle thing and loved it anyway. Edit means there are hunters who don't need rifles. Edit means my mouth is not only a weapon — it is a draft someone else can revise.

We stand in the shop shadow until the ridge air remembers how to move. Mira touches my shoulder — light, claimed.

"Sleep," she says. "Tomorrow we move toward the harbor city. There's a woman in quiet floors who knows what you are. We take her before the Federation files her east."

"What I am," I repeat.

"Someone who can make a door open without touching the lock," she says. "As long as a living mind holds the key."

Living minds. Never the wood. Never the steel. Always the person who loves the key enough to carry it. The horror in miniature, Mira doesn't say, but I hear it anyway: the power has no workaround that isn't a person.

I sleep facing the wall. Habit. In the dark I almost say Haru's name the way you almost touch a bruise — to check if it still hurts. It does. Somewhere on a different road he is probably counting footsteps alone, soft and stubborn, ledgering everyone's mood including his own. I don't hope he's following.

I check the scrape in my throat instead, and listen to Mira's breathing on the other mattress, and wait for morning to hand me another small command that will feel like relief until it doesn't.
