# Chapter 5 — The Pier
*POV: Kaito*

My throat still feels like someone sanded it and called it healing.

Every word scrapes. Even the soft ones. Especially the soft ones, because soft used to be Haru's job and now my mouth keeps trying to do both — shout and soothe — and fails at the joint. Mira says that's the echo. She doesn't say of what. She says *echo* the way Sugi says *tide* — like the word already knows its job and you're the idiot still asking for a manual.

We walk the breakwater and she talks in clean little sentences. Who. What. No spare words. Wind off the water hard enough to make your eyes water so you can pretend that's why. Scar on her forearm pale in the light, old fire worn into a badge. She never makes me repeat anything in front of Haru. She just leaves the shapes lying around where I can trip on them: *name the body, name the action, finish the clause before the world invents an ending for you.*

Haru watches from the house. I can feel his ledger even when I can't see him — that quiet counting, moods and absences and the exact angle of my shoulders when I come back from a walk. It makes me want to punch a wall and apologize to the wall.

"You're still waiting for him to absolve you," Mira said once, not unkind. Spray on the rocks. Gulls arguing like they had jurisdiction.

"I'm waiting for him to stop looking at me like I'm a math problem."

"Then stop being one," she said. "Become an answer."

I hated how much I wanted that. Want is a ledge. I've already watched someone stand on one.

On the fourth day she had me stand at the end of the breakwater and say nothing for ten full minutes while a fishing boat crawled past. "Feel the sentence wanting out," she said. "Don't feed it. Hunger teaches aim." I stood there with my hands in my jacket pockets and my ruined voice locked and thought about Ryn's joke about cheating and the cat's one eye and Haru's hands opening on the flats when mine wouldn't. The hunger didn't go away. It just learned my shape.

---

The pier job wasn't a job. It was a Tuesday that went wrong.

I'd gone down for ice and gossip — Sugi's list, ordinary, the kind of errand that makes you feel like a person instead of a wound with feet. The quay was loud in the honest way: men shouting weights, hose water running pink, ice smoke hanging low. Jun was at the cone line with papers that should have been enough and weren't. Two gray-greens. One crate. The kind of detention that looks temporary until it isn't — soldier boredom, refugee hope, the arithmetic already finished before anyone speaks.

Mira was inland on her list. Haru was at Sugi's with a shopping bag and that face he makes when he's pretending errands are strategy. I was there because I can't walk past unfairness without my feet deciding for me. Always have been. Chest first. The old way.

Jun saw me and grinned that umeboshi grin, like backup had arrived. God. He thought I was backup. Haiga dust still ghosting his shoe seams. Papers held out like a door key in a city that had stopped believing in doors.

"Let him go," I said.

One of the soldier's hands opened. The other didn't. Half a release — ugly, incomplete, the world obeying with one limb and arguing with the other. Jun jerked half-free and the free hand slammed him into the crate corner hard enough that I heard teeth. Temple. Ribs. He made a sound I will not describe because describing it makes it mine, and I already own too many sounds.

"Stop," I said. Then louder. "Stop — get back—"

Bad words. Muddy. Two jobs in one breath, no who, no finish. The soldier froze wrong — mid-step, mid-breath, eyes wide — and then the freeze cracked and he swung the rifle butt out of panic more than orders. Wood and metal meeting bone. Jun went down. Blood on the crate corner that wasn't all from his mouth. I got between them the old way, with my body, knuckles first, and there was blood that wasn't all mine and a shout from the far end of the pier that meant more boots were coming.

We ran.

I carried Jun because his feet had stopped believing in floors. He was lighter than he should have been and heavier than I wanted. Every step bounced a sound out of him that I filed under *later, hate yourself later*. Behind us cones clattered. Someone yelled *halt* like the word still had manners. We cut between sheds that smelled like dried fish and wet cardboard, past a hose still running pink for nobody, up the shrine path where the salt wind couldn't chase as hard.

At the shrine a widow packed his temple with cloth and did not ask my name. She looked at my face the way people look at weather they can't afford to hate. Jun's aunt came with sunken eyes and hands that knew bandage work; she didn't thank me. She didn't curse me. She just took over the cloth and made a small, practical sound when she saw the ribs.

Mira found us after, looked at Jun, looked at me, and said, very mild:

"Clear beats loud. Finish every clause. You're aiming with your feelings."

"I was aiming with *him*," I said, ruined voice cracking on the seam.

"Same mistake," she said, and there was no cruelty in it, which made it worse. She crouched, checked Jun's pupils with two fingers, nodded once to the widow. "He'll sit. He won't run for a week if he has sense." Then, to me, quiet enough that the widow could pretend not to hear: "On the roof you were loud. Loud is a crowd tool. Precision is a person tool. Decide which world you're saving."

I wanted to tell her I wasn't saving a world. I was trying not to drop another person. The sentence wouldn't come out clean. Maybe that was the whole problem.

Outside, gray-green moved along the lower road and then moved on. Temporary, they called it. Temporary is a word people use when they want you to wait politely for the permanent thing.

---

That night I sat on the shrine steps with my hands shaking and the maybe sitting in my chest like a second heartbeat.

Maybe if I'm careful.

Maybe if I say it cleaner.

Maybe the roof only happened because I was weak — because the words took the surface and not what I meant — and maybe strength is the only apology that counts.

Haru would call that a skill problem. Mira calls it becoming useful. I call it the only thought that doesn't make me want to tear my own tongue out.

The widows argued soft about salt prices behind the screens. Lantern light made the path look warmer than it was. Jun slept or pretended to; every few minutes his breath hitched and my whole body flinched like I'd been the one hit. I kept replaying the half-open hand. The muddy *stop*. The way his trust had hit me harder than the rifle butt — that grin, that *backup's here*, and then my words making everything worse by being almost enough.

I walked home along the tide line. Wet sand sucking at my shoes. Somewhere a radio muttered FRONT STABLE in a town that had never believed that font. Foam drew and erased and drew again. I thought about telling Haru everything — the half-open hand, the muddy stop, the crate corner, the way Mira's mildness felt like a hand on the back of my neck steering. I imagined his face doing the ledger. I imagined him saying *luck* again with his eyes while his mouth said something kinder.

I didn't tell him the clean way.

In the dark entryway of Sugi's house I wiped the blood off my knuckle on the inside of my jacket and practiced the story until it had edges that wouldn't cut him immediately. When he looked up from the shopping bag he was still packing into the cupboard — rice, tea, the small domestic competence that makes him look older than both of us — I gave him the dull version.

"Jun fell," I said. "There was a fight at the pier. He's at the shrine. Widow's on it."

Haru's eyes did the math and didn't like the remainder. "You okay?"

"I'm standing," I said, which is not an answer, and he let it be one because he is soft in the places I'm sandpaper.

Later, alone, under my breath in the kitchen dark, I practiced sentences with edges:

*Walk three steps back.*

*Sit on the bench.*

*Drop it.*

My nose bled. Thin line on my wrist when I wiped. My throat burned like I'd swallowed the breakwater. In the corner the cat watched with its one milky eye. I thought of Ryn's eyes on the way down, and I thought *never again*, and I meant *never lose*, and those are not the same vow even when they share a mouth.

Outside, the tide kept rewriting the flats. I stood there until the burning eased enough to swallow, and then I rinsed the sink so Haru wouldn't see pink in the morning and start a new column.

---

In the morning Jun could sit without crying when he breathed.

Temple bandage. Ribs wrapped. He looked at me like I was still someone who saved people. The shrine smelled like salt and old incense and the boiled barley the widows were sharing out in chipped bowls. His aunt watched me over his head with a face that had already spent its thank-yous on harder years.

I almost corrected him.

Instead I brought him water in a metal cup that tasted faintly of tin and sat on the shrine floor until my legs went numb, listening to widows argue about salt prices, and every time someone said *let it go* about a small thing — a debt, a rumor, a bad catch — my body flinched like the words still had a ledge under them.

Haru came by with rice from Sugi and didn't ask the questions his eyes were writing. Mira did not come. Her absence was another shape left on the floor for me to trip on. I drank bad tea. I watched Jun trust me. I filed the trust under *debt* and the pier under *lesson* and the maybe under *tomorrow*, and tried not to notice that all three were starting to sound like Mira's homework.
