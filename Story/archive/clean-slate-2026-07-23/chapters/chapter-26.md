# Chapter 26 — Transfer
*POV: Haruki*

Morning in a hold is just night that learned paperwork.

The bulb still hums. The vent still carries gray-green weather in pieces — asset secure, Remnant bird lost west, verification breeding manners into teeth. Riku has not forgiven the rifle butt; the swelling around his eye has turned the color of a bruise that intends to invoice someone. I have not slept clean. Vigil is a soft job. Soft jobs are still jobs. Somewhere above, koi circle a garden that filed a lieutenant into obedience and then into silence. Somewhere west, rotors already became Remnant dark and a flatter voice and a humming grandmother. I file the pad under panic again, hard enough that the filing almost sticks. Almost is the only grammar I trust.

Boots in the hall. Not Mira's punctuation. Federation. Asset posture — the kind of hands that want boys alive and catalogued, manners peeled off by rotor-wash and a city that shuddered like two radios fighting for one song. The door grows a handle on their side. A captain looks at us the way reports look at ink that learned to bleed.

"Transfer," he says. "On your feet."

Riku stands like the floor owes him interest. I stand like ledgers stand — because pages do not get to refuse transport without becoming ash. Soft voice rises in my throat out of habit, sticky, apologetic, the talent that opened mansion doors for a kitchen story Machi invented. It dies unfinished when the captain's eyes do not soften. Belief fails. Fear fails. The hill has stopped renting doors to boys with soft mouths. Good. Or not good. The ledger refuses to price it yet.

They cuff us polite. Not theater-chain. Practical. Riku's crooked tooth shows once — not a grin, a receipt. "Bill's growing," he mutters. I almost apologize for the bill. The apology dies the way last night's apology died. Riku snorts, which in his language means *filed*.

---

The van smells like sealed rubber and someone else's fear that never got a window.

Salt dies as Takamatsu falls behind — harbor rumor, arcade lanterns, Minato's stubborn brown eye, pines that hold an old man who walked. Farm dust replaces it, then inland dust that pretends geography is innocent. Riku maps exits with his eyes even when the exits are a locked panel and a driver's neck. Garden corpse, mid-lunge stall, chase, roof, two frequencies, lift, lock — and now transfer language wearing a seatbelt.

Through the driver's radio — thin, official, the weather of people who invent calm — a voice mentions Kanzaki without flinching. Hunt continues. Loud one still active. Asset transfer confirmed to Quiet Field. Two Voices appears once as a question someone else will answer later, mild as a stamp. My stomach files the entry before my heart can refuse it. Kanzaki's watch on a birthday roof. Smile with none of his mouth. Ryn held like a receipt. I do not let the radio become a reunion with that hour. Reunions are how soft boys invent rescues in vans that do not open from inside.

Riku's shoulder touches mine once — not comfort, alignment. "Survive a Tuesday," he says, low.

"That's my line," I try, and it comes out thinner than joke, thin as the coin a still man left when he asked *whom* and I said *yet*.

Outside, the road invents hours. Inside, my nose remembers bleeding. The pad clash sits under my tongue like metal I refuse to call inheritance. Mira said two Voices. I said panic. I am still saying panic. Panic is cheaper than owning a sky.

---

Shizuhara Detention Complex does not look like a farm. That is the first lie it refuses to tell.

Glass lobby. Fluorescent honesty. CCTV domes like polite pupils. Soft-voice PA murmuring corridor codes that sound like kindness if you do not speak inventory. Biometrics. Clipboards. The smell of disinfectant trying to erase the smell of boys who arrived before us and did not leave as stories. Gray-green here wears indoor manners — boots that learned linoleum, rifles that learned to be furniture until they aren't. Asset posture wakes the second our cuffs catch the light. Alive and catalogued. The phrase fits the air like it was measured for it.

They line us with others — not many, enough. A girl with volunteer-cut hair staring at her own hands. A boy whose paperwork says irregular and whose eyes say alley. War-prisoner stamps on a sleeve I do not stare at long enough to invent a name for. Intake is a choreography of tablets and soft instructions. Sit here. Look there. Answer when asked. Do not invent personality. Personality is how inventory becomes a problem.

Colonel Habu meets the line in the glass like weather that learned to hold a tablet.

Fifties, soft voice, the kind of calm that makes rooms decide shouting would be rude. He does not raise his volume for children. He does not lower it for corpses he has not made yet. He scrolls. He smiles with none of the cruelty people expect from smiles, which is worse. When a small intake — twelve, maybe thirteen, volunteer armband ghost still in the way he stands — stumbles on a question about ward of origin, Habu does not sharpen. He taps the tablet once, mild as filing, and says, without changing tone:

"Mark him inventory until medical clears usefulness."

Inventory.

The word does not arrive like a slap. That is the horror. It arrives like a weather report. Soft. Precise. A child filed as stock that might spoil. Someone behind me exhales wrong. Riku's jaw ticks once — the exit-price look, except the exit just became a philosophy. Habu's eyes pass over us the way folders pass over names. He does not linger on my face. He does not need to. Soft boys and road dogs are still inventory if the building decides the category fits.

"Welcome to Quiet Field," Habu says to the line, soft. "Relocation is a kindness. Processing is a kindness. Compliance keeps kindness from needing other tools."

I almost laugh. The laugh dies unfinished, which feels like dignity wearing panic's coat. Machi would price this man in one pass and refuse him broth. Gorō would call the tablet a fact and the fact a muzzle. Kaito would schedule something. I file Habu under kitchen — the hand on the spoon, smiling, calling the meal inventory without changing his voice.

Medic Yano flashes past the edge of intake like a bright small weather system — cheerful posture, clipboard, the school-nurse energy of someone who will later decide who is still useful. I do not get a camera long enough to keep him. Habu is the lens today. Habu and the word that did not flinch.

---

Cellblock assignment is C before it is anything gentler.

Irregulars. The word fits Riku like a jacket he already owns. It fits me like a ledger stamped wrong on purpose. East wing gets mentioned once in a guard's mouth — specials, medical adjacency, not your corridor — and then the mouth moves on as if specials are a rumor that pays rent in another building. Volunteers A and B breathe somewhere through vents that carry other people's coughs. We walk fluorescent geometry: left, left, a stair that smells like bleach and fear's second cousin, a door that opens only from the polite side.

Our cell is two bunks, one sink, a slit window that invents sky without offering it. Riku takes the bunk with the better angle on the door without discussion. I take the other because soft boys still perform fairness even when fairness is theater. The cuff marks on my wrists look like punctuation. Outside the bars — not bars exactly, reinforced glass with manners — corridor count practice drifts past: numbers, names, the soft violence of being tallied.

Riku clicks his tongue once. "Exits: vent too small, glass too honest, guard rotation every nine if the boots don't lie. Soft boy?"

"Still here," I say.

"Good. Don't sticky-voice the colonel. Men who say inventory don't rent doors."

I nod. Stickiness failed on the hill. Stickiness will fail harder here. I keep the page open anyway — habit, ledger, the soft job that isn't soft: refuse the version where we become only stock. Somewhere in the building a girl I have not met yet may already be correcting a number. Somewhere west of a pad that is no longer ours, a louder boy may already be aiming a flatter mouth at this stack. I do not know that. Knowing would be chasing. I am transferred. Transfer is a kind of holding that learned wheels.

Night in the complex is not the mansion bulb.

It is PA weather. Soft codes. A laugh cut short three corridors away. The smell of rice that arrived as kindness and left as compliance. Riku sits against the wall the way he sat in the hold — exits banking rest without surrender. His eye looks worse under fluorescent honesty. He does not sleep yet. I do not ask him to. First night is for learning the building's breath, not for pretending breath is rest.

I recount the day into the open page: van, dust, glass lobby, Habu's tablet, *inventory* spoken like rain, Kanzaki's name seed on a radio that thinks hunt is climate, cellblock C, Riku's crooked tooth promising a bill the Federation has not priced. The pad still sits under panic in my filing system. Two Voices still sits under refusal. Owning would mean accepting that the stall in the hallway and the skid on the roof are dialects of the same mouth that killed a lieutenant with a calendar. I will not accept that cleanly. Not on a first night. Not while Quiet Field calls children inventory without changing tone.

"Haru," Riku says, not soft. Alignment. "Tomorrow we count. Counting is how they own you. We count back."

"I know," I say, and almost mean the kitchen ideal again — spoon, hand, stir the other way — except the kitchen is a detention stack and the spoon is a tablet and Habu smiles while he stirs.

I keep my eyes open on the glass that invents a corridor. The story does not get to end on a closed eye and call that peace — not while inventory is a soft word and a hunt continues somewhere under Kanzaki's watch and a quieter boy refuses to become a number without writing the number down first.

First night holds. I hold with it. Quiet Field has only started speaking.
