# Chapter 17 — Free the City
*POV: Kaito*

Suzumori names him over cold rice.

The room behind the arcade still smells like dust and old sugar — the ghost of carnival prizes, sticky paper, a decade of quarters pressed into machines that no longer hum. Mira's map is pinned to the wall with nails that used to hold carnival posters. The bicycle bell sits on a crate between us like a small god with one job. Suzumori eats the way she hums — carefully, as if each grain has a name and she is filing them in order. Outside, Takamatsu's soft lockdown keeps the streets thin. Inside, the rice has gone sticky and I have not touched mine. My stomach knows hunger. My mouth knows schedules. Hunger loses when schedules win.

"Lieutenant Aihara," she says. "Filing hand. Quiet floors knew his signature before they knew his face. He was at Kōgetsu Municipal High the night the wards broke. Not chance. Dragnet. He wants carriers alive. He runs Takamatsu from the mansion like a spreadsheet with gardens."

The name lands the way Echo lands — behind the nose, metallic, already half a schedule. Kōgetsu. School night. Roof. A soldier's hand opening because I asked it to. I have spent weeks turning that night into a map of guilt. Suzumori turns it into a filing cabinet with a man inside it.

Mira's eyes shine — not joy, alignment. The blunt cut of her hair catches the weak lamp and throws a shadow on the map that looks like a route. "Then we hear him out."

"We make him talk," I say, and hear how scheduled it sounds. Not *ask*. Not *find*. Talk as verb with teeth. The word sits in my mouth like something I have already practiced on a cloth. Mira does not correct me. Correction would imply I said the wrong thing. She looks like I said the useful thing. Useful is a temperature I am learning to live inside. It is warmer than grief and colder than Haru.

Suzumori rings the bicycle bell once. The sound is small and clean and older than the war app's ugly tones — that chirp that tells you which ward is on fire and calls it contained. "Talk first. Killing is a clause you don't get to unsay. You already know that."

Jun. The soldier. The plum tree. The unfinished loop of *stop* that taught me what unfinished does — how a sentence can leave only one clean clause when the mud gets in. I nod because nodding is cheaper than speaking. The nod is not agreement. It is acknowledgment that she can still hear the difference between a boy grieving and a boy shopping for a throat. I am not sure how long that difference will survive me. The map on the wall does not care. Maps never care. They only ask for ink.

---

We watch the hill for two days.

Not from the mansion's front approach — that is theater, gray-green politeness arranged like furniture, captains standing too straight at gates that were built for photographs. We watch from a laundry roof two wards over, sheets drying on lines that pretend ordinary weather still exists. From a tram stop where the line pretends it still runs on time, the schedule board frozen mid-departure like a city holding its breath on purpose. From the shadow of a shuttered flower shop that sells nothing and still opens its awning out of habit, because habits survive lockdowns the way filing hands survive faces — by becoming invisible.

Servant rotations. Delivery vans that are not vans — wrong logos, wrong weight in the suspension, drivers who do not look at the hill directly because looking is a report. A garden path where a mild man feeds koi while a captain stands too straight, as if posture can substitute for certainty. Aihara looks like someone's uncle if someone's uncle filed cities. Civilian cardigan. Soft shoes. Eyes that count without looking like they count. He moves through his own garden the way a clerk moves through a drawer — familiar, unhurried, already knowing where everything sits.

Mira names the pieces for me in her low voice, the voice that does not ask permission and does not offer warmth. "Captain changes at the east gate every four hours. Kitchen staff leave by the service road after the second meal. Gardeners are local — fear first, loyalty second. Fear is easier to borrow."

"Borrow," I repeat. The word tastes like Mira's philosophy — not theft, rental. Rent the people who open doors. Return them when the door closes. Or don't return them. Doors don't keep receipts.

"You don't steal a city," Mira says. "You rent the people who open its doors."

Suzumori sits with us once, wrapped in a coat that looks borrowed from a thinner year. She watches the hill the way she watches air — as if pitch is a fact, not a feeling. The mansion lights at dusk look almost domestic from this distance. Almost is a lie I have stopped trusting. "He will feel curiosity before he feels threat," she murmurs. "Filing hands like that. They prefer questions that fit in boxes."

"Good," Mira says. "We have questions."

I have questions that do not fit in boxes. Who signed the school night. Who called two boys assets before they were old enough to vote. Who decided Ryn was leverage that could fail upward. Who taught a city that lockdown could arrive without screaming and still be a fist. Who looked at a roof and a birthday and wrote *weather* on a form. I keep those in my chest where the sentences are getting louder. Louder is not louder volume. Louder is cleaner grammar pressing against the ribs.

Between watches, Mira drills sentences with me until the scrape in my throat feels like a second tongue — one that knows what the first one is for.

Who. What. Bounds. No spare words. No mud. She makes me say a gardener's name and a clean errand and then stop, mouth closed, before the Word reaches for more than I meant. She makes me practice *tell me* the way other people practice aiming — breath, sight line, release. She makes me hold a sentence unfinished until my nose bleeds and I do not wipe it, because wiping is a tell and tells get filed. She does not say *Kill yourself*. She doesn't have to. The shape sits in the room anyway, patient as a tool on a cloth. Jun taught me the clause. The plum tree taught me the cost. Aihara, if I am honest in the dark part of myself, is already a calendar square waiting for ink.

"After him," Mira says, during a stretch when the mansion lights look almost ordinary — warm windows, garden lanterns, the lie of a house that is only a house — "the squeeze loosens. The bar families breathe. You wanted to stop losing people."

I did. I do. That is the jacket I put on every morning now — stop losing people — and it used to be Haru's kindness, the soft refusal to let anyone fall through a ledger. Somewhere between Nagihama and this arcade room it got tailored wrong. Shorter in the sleeves. Tighter at the throat. Useful.

"Free the city of him," I say, trying the phrase on. It fits. It fits too well. Free as in release. Free as in erase. The grammar sits between those meanings like a blade that hasn't decided which edge is up. The Word will not ask which I mean. The Word will hear whichever one I feed it when the moment comes.

Suzumori watches me. Her hum is absent tonight — a silence that weighs more than sound. "When you say *free*, does the Word hear *release* or *erase*?"

I don't answer. Answering would be honesty, and honesty is becoming expensive. Mira's mildness does not fill the silence. She lets it hang, because silence is also a kind of permission. Outside, a tram bell rings for a tram that isn't coming. The soft lockdown language on my phone refreshes: stay in, stay quiet, verification in progress. Harbor noise stays thin. The hill stays bright. Bright is not safety. Bright is inventory with windows.

On the second night we eat again — hot this time, rice that steams, fish Mira's runners found without my mouth. The luxury of not having to ask. Suzumori asks for tea and drinks it like ceremony — small sips, eyes on the map, the hill reduced to ink and pins. She tells me, without looking at me, that the quiet floors taught her the sound of a city being inventoried. "Boys in rooms. Names on papers. The ones who hummed were kept longer. Humming is hard to file."

"Is that why they kept you?" I ask.

"They kept me because I am a kept thing," she says. "You freed a kept thing. Filing hands notice shapes."

Mira's gaze flicks to me. "He'll expect Remnant. He won't expect how young the mouth is."

"He knew the school," I say. "He knew ages."

"He knew thresholds," Suzumori corrects. "Awakening is a weather pattern to men like him. He aims nets at storms."

I wipe my nose before Echo can announce itself. Habit. Mira hands me a cloth without looking. The cloth comes away pink. Somewhere under this breath of a city, if ledgers still run, Haru is calling the quiet weather and trying not to chase a handwriting he already recognized in an alley witness. I do not go looking. Looking would be unfinished. Unfinished is how people die in my mouth. I file Haru under *location* and then I file the file closed and I hate myself with the efficient hatred of a boy who is learning to schedule even that.

That night the mansion lights look like a birthday cake for someone who collects boys. I hate the comparison the moment I make it and keep it anyway. Candles. Roof. A wish that never finished. The hill wears celebration the way Aihara wears a cardigan — soft cover on hard inventory.

I sleep facing the map. Approaches. Service roads. Garden wing. H-pad sketched in Mira's neat hand like a future argument. In the dark I almost say Haru's name as a location — not *Haru*, the boy who laughed on a Rail Loop bench, but *Haru*, the coordinate where softness still lives and might become a liability. I swallow it. Locations get missions. People get ledgers. I am trying to become the first and forget the second, because forgetting is what the Word seems to want from me — less softness, cleaner grammar, a voice that does not flinch when Mira says *useful*.

Tomorrow we go up the hill. Talk first. Schedule second. The order is a promise I make to a humming old woman and break already in the quiet of my own chest. The break is not audible. The break is a hairline fracture that only I can feel when I breathe.

I lie there tasting metal and cold rice and the word *free*, and I practice saying Aihara's name without heat. Heat is for roofs. Heat is for boys who still think unfinished sentences are accidents. Cold is for calendars. Cold is for filing hands in cardigans who offered tea before they offered truth.

Suzumori's bell sits on the crate. I do not ring it. Ringing would be asking for a blessing I have already decided not to deserve. The hill stays bright through the shutter crack. The city holds its breath. I hold mine longer.
