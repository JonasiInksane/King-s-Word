# Chapter 4 — Two Weeks
*POV: Haruki*

Two weeks in Nagihama tastes like salt and borrowed futons.

Not the clean restaurant salt. The kind that lives in wood grain and window frames and the soft place behind your teeth after you've been breathing harbor air too long. Sugi's house keeps its own weather — kettle steam fogging the glass above the sink, umeboshi jars lined like little red judgments, an empty stretch of shelf where the arcade cat used to sit before the tide took it. Outside, the port does port things. Nets slapped against concrete. Diesel coughing awake before the sun clears the ridge. Widows who check the eastern horizon every dawn the way other people check weather apps, and who do not ask us for a third set of footprints in the entryway sand.

The war app still buzzes sometimes. Here the tones arrive late and soft, like gossip worn thin by other mouths. The sea does the lying for them: gray-green water that looks calm until you watch it long enough to see how hard it is working.

Kaito's voice is a seam that hasn't finished closing. He speaks soft now, which is wrong on him, like seeing a knife used as a spoon. When he laughs — rare, half-powered — it catches on the roof still living in his throat. He touches the place afterward every time, checking. I pretend not to count how often.

Mira comes and goes. Not every day. Enough that I start counting absences the way I used to count moods. She never takes him far — a walk to the breakwater, a conversation under the shrine's eaves where the salt wind can't steal the words, once a stretch of road inland that she returns him from before the lamps come on — and she always brings him back before dark, as if returning a tool to its hook. She does not train him in front of me. She does not name anything. She leaves permission in the air like incense.

I hate that I can smell it.

On the third morning I found him at the sink practicing silence. Not meditation. Testing. He'd fill a glass, set it down, open his mouth on a word, and shut it again before the word could choose a shape. Water ran. Stopped. Ran. His shoulders were set the way they used to be before a fight he planned to win with his knuckles. When he noticed me he grinned the old grin at half power, which somehow hurt worse than no grin at all.

"Just thirsty," he said.

"Just lying," I said.

He drank the water. His hand shook once against the glass and then decided not to. Outside, a gull screamed like it had opinions about honesty. I wanted to ask what word he'd been holding. I didn't. Incomplete entries itch, but some itches are the only thing keeping the skin attached.

---

Sugi put us to work because grief left alone rearranges furniture.

We mended nets in the lean-to behind her house until my fingers smelled permanently of tar and fish-scale. We carried rice sacks that had been stamped by three different offices and still might not be enough. We sat through a neighborhood meeting in the shrine annex where a man with a stamp and a tired face explained which papers would get you rice and which papers would get you noticed. The room smelled like old tatami and wet coats. Someone's baby cried once and was hushed with the efficiency of people who have learned that noise draws eyes.

Jun was there — Hanae's brother, Haiga dust still ghosting his shoe seams — grinning at a dish of umeboshi like it was a festival stall. He asked Kaito if the east was as bad as the screens said. Kaito said, "Worse," and then, quieter, "Don't go looking for proof."

Jun nodded as if he'd been given a map. Boys like that treat warnings as invitations. I filed him under *keep an eye on* and hated that my ledger had started including strangers. His sister wasn't in the room; a widow with sunken eyes tugged his sleeve when he laughed too loud. He shook her off gently. Festival energy. Wrong season.

Mira watched from the doorway of that meeting for exactly forty seconds. I timed it because I am who I am. She caught Kaito's eye. She left. Forty seconds is long enough to move a decimal. After she was gone, Kaito's attention stayed pointed at the empty doorway for three more breaths, then came back to the stamp-man's voice as if nothing had happened. Something had. I filed that too.

---

On the eleventh evening we sat on the sea wall with our legs hanging over nothing useful.

The tide was wrong for a rite and right for talking. Below us the flats were half-covered, water the color of a bruise deciding whether to yellow. Wind came off the harbor in wet pushes. Somewhere a radio muttered about containment in a town that had never believed that word. Kaito had a bruise on his knuckle I hadn't seen him earn. He kept flexing the hand like he was checking whether it still belonged to him.

"You're going to ask," he said.

"I'm going to ask," I said.

"If I leave with her."

"If you leave with her."

He laughed once, no humor. "You sound like a ledger."

"I am a ledger." I picked at the concrete. Salt grit under my nail. "You're writing in red."

He was quiet long enough that a fishing boat's light crawled across the dark, a small yellow stubbornness. Then: "On the roof. The hand. It opened so *clean*, Haru. Like it wanted to. Like I—" He stopped. Touched his throat. The seam. "I keep thinking if I'd said it different. If I'd been stronger. Mira says—"

"Don't," I said, too fast. The absolution still wouldn't come. Something meaner did. "Don't make it into a skill problem."

He looked at me sideways. Hurt, and grateful for the hurt, which is a terrible combination. The nick through his left eyebrow caught what little light there was. "Mira says it wasn't my fault."

"Mira says what you need to hear."

"And you don't?"

I watched the water where we'd given the cat to the tide — where the milky eye had become a rumor and then nothing. "I say what I can stand to say. That night under the machines I had a sentence. I didn't spend it. That's on me. It doesn't make her right."

"She's the only one who isn't looking at me like I broke a window."

"You broke a world," I said, and immediately wanted to take it back, and didn't, because taking it back would have been another kindness I wasn't sure he could afford.

That sat between us like a third person. The sea kept breathing. Finally he bumped his shoulder into mine — old gesture, smaller now, like a joke told at half volume. "Not tonight," he said. "Inland. Mira's list. Hiding places. Whatever. Not tonight."

"Okay," I said, and hated how relieved I sounded. Relief is a tripwire you can catch a foot on later.

Above us on the road, a plain coat stood too still against the salt wind. Not walking. Not smoking. Just occupying space the way furniture occupies a room. When I squinted, there was no one. Grief does that. So do hunters. I filed both options and kept my eyes on Kaito's hands, which were open on his knees for once, as if he'd forgotten to fist them.

---

The next afternoon we walked the docks because Sugi needed something from a fishmonger who owed her a favor from before favors needed papers.

The quay smelled like ice and blood and honest work. Men shouted weights. A hose ran pink water into a drain that didn't care. Gulls argued over guts. A gray-green checkpoint had grown at the foot of the pier overnight — two Federation, bored in the particular way of men who have been told boredom is vigilance, one refugee kid with a cart that wouldn't clear the cone line. The kid's hands were up. The cart was full of somebody's entire remaining life in plastic bags: clothes, a dented kettle, a photo frame face-down like it knew better than to look.

"Move it back," one soldier said.

The kid's mouth opened. Nothing useful came out. The other soldier's hand settled on the strap of his rifle with the lazy confidence of men who have never lost an argument with a cart. Behind them the pier stretched into water that didn't take sides.

Kaito stepped forward before I could ledger a better option.

"Let him through," he said. Not loud. Not the roof. Just Kaito, chest-first, the old volume trying to remember itself.

The soldier's hands opened.

Both of them. Fingers uncurling off the strap, off the cone, the same even nobody-home release I had watched kill a birthday. No struggle. No argument. Just the world deciding the sentence was finished and the body was furniture that could be rearranged. The cart rattled through. Plastic bags whispered against each other. The kid didn't look back. The soldier blinked at his own empty hands like they'd betrayed a joke.

Kaito wiped under his nose. A pinprick. Dark on his knuckle for a second before the wind took the shine off it. He looked at me, and for once he didn't grin.

"Luck," he said.

"Luck," I agreed, because the alternative was a word I wasn't ready to put in my mouth, and because we were still — for one more night — on the same side of the water.

On the far end of the pier, a man in a plain coat too warm for the season stood very still and watched the empty hands the way other people watch the tide. Not surprised. Not afraid. Interested, maybe, in the arithmetic. When I looked again, he was gone — only cones, only gulls, only the kid's cart disappearing into the maze of sheds like a small escape that wouldn't stay small.

We got Sugi her fish. The monger didn't ask why our faces looked like that. Coast people know when not to inventory other people's weather.

---

That night Mira brought tea she hadn't been offered and sat across from Kaito like a tutor waiting for homework.

I stayed in the room on purpose. Sat on the floor with my back against the wall and my cup cooling untouched between my hands. She smiled at me with perfect mildness — the kind that files you under *present but not relevant* — and spoke only about weather and roads. Rain inland by Thursday. A ridge town with too many shutters. Which paths still had checkpoints that stamped and which ones only watched. Kaito listened the way he used to listen to fight plans: body forward, eyes bright, guilt tucked somewhere it couldn't interrupt.

When she left, Kaito's shoulders had dropped a fraction — relief or surrender, same silhouette from where I was standing. The cat on the shelf watched the door after her like it was keeping a second ledger.

"You don't have to babysit," he said, not looking at me.

"I'm not," I said. "I'm inventory."

He almost laughed. Almost. Then he went to wash his face, and the water ran a long time, and I stayed where I was until the house settled into its night noises: kettle ticking cool, wind under the eaves, distant diesel like a heartbeat that belonged to someone else's body.

I slept facing the door. Habit. Ledger. The numbers tipping.
