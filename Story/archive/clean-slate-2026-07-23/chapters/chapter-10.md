# Chapter 10 — Soft Boy
*POV: Haruki*

The inland road teaches you how many kinds of dust there are.

Salt dust that still pretends it remembers the sea. Ridge dust that gets into your teeth and makes every apology taste like grit. Checkpoint dust that settles on your shoes while a clipboard man decides whether you are a person or a form. Sato's stamps get me through one of those — leftover paper machinery laid on Sugi's table like a dare, one day and one name cleared if I don't invent a speech. The next checkpoint wants a story. I give them the soft voice and a half-truth about looking for family west of the ridge, and somehow they believe me harder than the lie deserves.

I tell myself it's manners. I do not tell myself it's the same math as a hand opening on a roof. I do not tell myself my voice has always been a little too useful in rooms that should have argued.

Sugi's onigiri lasts until the second switchback. Plum and charcoal cling to the wrapping after the rice is gone. Her voice comes with it — *come back as yourself if you can* — and then Jun under the plum tree, and then Kaito's shoulder turning inland, and then the arcade cat on the shelf with one milky eye, staying where the rite can still find it. The ledger does not need me to open it. It runs itself.

I walk.

Kuroishi announces itself with paper before it announces itself with people. Clipboard gray-green at a bend. Bus depot with a schedule board someone still updates in tidy characters, as if the buses have not learned to lie. One long main street of shuttered tool shops and a croquette cart that has never moved in its life. Above the street, a stone shrine gate black with old soot. The air smells like fried batter and old smoke and the particular quiet of towns that have decided fear is more efficient than panic.

I almost walk into a dried-goods shop because a part of me still thinks Kaito leaves heat signatures on doorframes. He isn't there. A woman sweeping the step looks at me too long — not unkind, just accurate — and says, "You're late for the loud one," which is how I learn I'm chasing a rumor that already has a nickname.

"Which way," I ask.

She points with her chin, further from salt. "Harbor city. Hill. Or that's what people say when they don't want to say names." She studies my shoes. "You don't want him for the usual reasons, do you."

"No," I say, and that is true enough to hurt.

I buy nothing. I keep walking. Ledger: one day behind, maybe two. The rumors are not rumors of a monster. That is the problem. Monsters you can hate cleanly. What Kuroishi has is a set of small helpful wrongnesses — a gate that opened because a tall boy asked nicely, a stamp that fell because a clerk suddenly remembered her break, a drunk private who forgot a face and slept like weather. People say *lucky* the way people say *lucky* when they mean *stolen*.

I file every scrap. I do not make anyone tell it twice. Confirmation, not a sermon. Cleaner sentence. Shorter loophole. At the depot a porter tells me a checkpoint line parted — not because a sergeant waved anyone through, but because a tall boy at the back said something mild about *moving*, and the men with rifles stepped aside like the air had been rearranged. "Lucky," the porter says. Then, after a beat: "Didn't feel lucky. Felt like someone borrowed my feet." Outside a boarding house with a cracked noren, a landlord smokes and will not meet my eyes. "I said no to a room. Then I said yes. I don't remember deciding. Charged them less than the paint is worth." He searches for a word that is not *stolen*. "I feel like I agreed to something I didn't agree to."

The shape is Kaito's even when the town refuses to give him a name. Not catastrophe — the soft theft of other people's *no*. Mira will be beside him, teaching him how not to leave loopholes. Somewhere my best friend is learning that finishing a clause feels like relief, and I am late for both.

I buy water near the soot-gate and almost apologize to the bottle when I fumble the change. Habit. The woman at the cart watches me the way people watch weather they can't afford to hate. "Loud one went west," she says, unasked. "Girl with calm eyes. If you're the soft problem behind them, you're already a day of dust too slow."

"I know," I say, and keep walking anyway.

---

The shakedown happens on a cut-through between a scrapyard and a canal that has given up being water.

Afternoon heat. Metal glare. The canal is a groove of green-black stillness with a shopping cart half-sunk in it like a joke nobody finished. Three men. Local, not gray-green — the kind of arithmetic that eats refugees between official hungers. One of them puts a hand on my bag. Another smiles with too many teeth. The third just watches, which is worse.

I say, "Please don't," which is not a strategy, and he laughs, which is.

My hands come up. Habit. Soft. The apology is already shaping itself behind my teeth — *sorry, I didn't mean, I can give you* — and then a fourth person arrives as a collar-grab from behind. Not the attackers. Me. Yanked out of the circle like a misfiled form. My bag swings. My apology dies as a squeak. The canal smell rushes up, wet iron and failure.

The new boy clicks his tongue once, crooked front tooth catching the light when he grins.

"Soft boy," he says. "You walk like the road owes you manners."

Seventeen, lean-hard, messy dark hair tied with a scrap of cloth, boots that have opinions. Work jacket over layers that have walked more than they've been cleaned. He doesn't ask if I want help. He prices the three men with his eyes, decides the math is boring, and says, "Leave."

They leave. Not because of magic — because of the way he stands, like exits are a language he speaks fluently. Or because of me. Or both. I am getting tired of not knowing which. The shopping cart in the canal does not clap. The scrapyard does not award points. My collar still feels the shape of his fist.

"I can pay," I say, automatic.

"Of course you can." He looks at my hands, my bag, my face — the whole soft inventory. "Name."

"Haruki."

"Riku." He clicks his tongue again. "Nakahara, if you're collecting. You hunting someone, or running?"

"Both."

"Stupid. Honest, though." He starts walking. After three steps he glances back, annoyed that I'm still a problem in his peripheral vision. "You coming, soft boy? Or you going to apologize to the canal next?"

I almost do. The canal looks like it could use one. Instead I pick up my bag, wipe grit off my palms, and follow. Not partnership. Not yet. Pay, boredom, spite at the kind of men who put hands on bags — those are currencies I understand. Riku doesn't ask for my story. He takes a croquette from the never-moving cart with money that appears from nowhere honest, splits it without ceremony, and talks in short cuts about cut-throughs and which bends remember FDC and which only remember hunger.

"You apologize before people are mad," he says once, not looking at me. "That's a hobby that gets you owned."

"It's a life skill."

"It's a personality disorder." He grins when I flinch, like he's won a small stupid game. "Someone already told you that."

"Someone did," I say, and the someone is tall and loud and inland of me by a day, maybe two, and I do not give Riku the name for free.

We walk until the ridge light goes copper. Riku talks in short cuts — which bends remember FDC, which only remember hunger, which scrapyards will sell you a rumor if you look like you can pay in silence. He does not ask why I flinch when gray-green passes. I do not ask why his mouth flattens for half a second whenever he sees an armband, even a child's festival one. Some ledgers stay closed on the first day.

He knows a place to sleep that is not a boarding house — a shed behind a bicycle graveyard where the wind argues with corrugated metal and loses. Chain grease. Rust. A tarp that has opinions about rain. He sleeps like a person who has decided nightmares are a luxury. I sleep like a person who still hears *Kill yourself* in a voice that used to say *let her win*, and when I wake with my own hand over my mouth I find Riku already awake, watching the shed door the way you watch a door you have already priced for exits.

"Dreams," he says. Not a question.

"Weather," I say.

He snorts. "Soft boy lying to ledgers. Fine. Don't make it a speech."

Morning is more dust and a shared bottle of water that tastes like plastic and stubbornness. Riku prices the road west with his eyes and decides the math is boring enough to keep me in it. "Harbor city," he says. "Hill. Work, maybe. If your loud one is there, he'll make weather. Weather's easier to hold than chase."

"I wasn't going to ask you to come."

"Good. Asking makes me itchy." He ties his hair back with the scrap of cloth, crooked tooth flashing once. "You coming, soft boy? Or apologizing to the shed for leaving?"

I follow. Not partnership. Not yet. Pay, boredom, spite at the kind of men who put hands on bags — those are currencies I understand. Somewhere ahead, Kuroishi's rumor points toward a harbor city with a hill. Riku knows the cut-throughs. I know how to keep a ledger of moods. Between us, the road keeps unrolling, and for the first time since the plum tree I am not counting footsteps alone.

I do not call it hope. Hope is a word that gets people killed when they schedule it too early. I call it company with boots that have opinions, and a crooked tooth that grins when I apologize to dust, and a direction that still smells faintly, stubbornly, like the boy I am late for.
