# Chapter 12 — Survive a Tuesday
*POV: Haruki*

Riku teaches with his boot.

East yards lot. Dust. A shipping container with a dent shaped like someone's bad decision. Afternoon heat that makes the metal smell like coins left in a pocket. He kicks my ankle into a stance and clicks his tongue once.

"Good," he says. "You look like someone who might survive a Tuesday."

"I was surviving," I say, breathing hard. Sweat in my eyes. The ledger of bruises already opening a new column on my shin.

"You were apologizing at fists. Different sport." He demonstrates a guard that doesn't care about manners — elbows in, chin boring, weight ready to leave. "Again. And stop looking past my shoulder like your boyfriend's going to rescue you from footwork."

"He's not—" I start, then shut up, because explaining Kaito requires a roof and a plum tree and a birthday cake and I am not giving Riku either for free. Not yet. Currencies matter.

We train until my hands sting and my soft instincts start arriving late enough to be useful. Riku doesn't praise. He corrects by moving my body like a broken tool he has decided, against advice, to keep. When I say sorry after catching his wrist too hard, he snorts and says, "Save it for someone who didn't ask you to hit."

"Reflex," I say.

"Then break it," he says. "Or someone else will rent it."

He demonstrates again — guard, step, the ugly practical shove that ends an argument before manners can invent one. Dust lifts around our shoes. The dented container watches like a bored god. I learn the difference between flinching and moving; flinching is what soft boys do when the world swings, moving is what keeps the swing from owning you. My ledger of bruises grows honest columns: shin, forearm, pride.

He doesn't ask for my story. He takes cash when I have it and noodles when I don't. At a konbini with a dying freezer hum we eat standing up like people who have not earned chairs. Riku talks about exits the way other boys talk about girls or games — casually, constantly, as if the world were a building he refused to get trapped in. I talk about weather and wards and the way gray-green politeness changes when a city thinks it is safe. He listens with his whole face when he lets himself, then clicks his tongue and calls me soft for noticing.

At night he sleeps like a person who has decided nightmares are a luxury. I sleep like a person who still hears *Kill yourself* in a voice that used to say *let her win*, and when the dream puts me under the plum tree again I wake with my mouth already shaping an apology to a boy who is no longer there to receive it. Corrugated dark. Ridge cold. The ledger runs without my permission: roof, dock, lane, inland dust, a crooked tooth that will not call itself partner.

Riku, eyes open in the dark: "If you say sorry to the ceiling I'm charging rent."

"I wasn't—"

"You were." He rolls over. "Sleep, soft boy. Tuesdays are undefeated."

---

Between training days we walk for work that isn't chasing. A crate shift behind the yards. A message run for a woman who pays in noodles and does not ask origin wards. Riku prices every job for exits; I price every face for mood. Once, on a cut-through that smells like wet rope, gray-green stops us for papers. I give them the soft voice and a half-truth. They believe me harder than the lie deserves. Riku watches my mouth afterward like he is filing a new fact he does not like.

"You do that too easy," he says.

"Manners."

"Weather," he corrects, not unkind. "Don't get used to borrowing people's yes. Makes you soft in the wrong direction."

I almost tell him about a roof. About a hand. About a plum tree. I give him silence instead, which he accepts as currency.

---

Takamatsu arrives as harbor smell and tram lines and a hill with a mansion that watches the wards like a polite landlord.

We come in on a farmer's truck bed between cabbage and a dog that has opinions about refugees. The city unrolls itself — Station Plaza with requisition stamps on crates, karaoke murder somewhere inland of salt, a borrowed hospital stack on the western skyline collecting its own weather. Gray-green isn't dramatic here. It's administrative. Convoys idle. Uniforms hold corners the way furniture holds a room. The Federation doesn't occupy Takamatsu so much as own it — the way a landlord owns a building he never visits: through paper, and through men paid to carry the paper.

Riku knows a flophouse above a bicycle repair. Chain grease. Oil smell. A window that looks toward the hill if you lean wrong. The repairman downstairs pretends we are inventory he hasn't counted yet. I know how to listen. Between us we learn the city's new weather: protection squeeze on the tram lines, rumors of a hospital stack that went quiet in a way hospitals shouldn't, mansion lights that brighten when the wards get nervous. Working boys talk about verification like tide tables. A granny selling persimmons says the hill always blinks before the wards get hungry.

I stop asking every stranger about a loud boy with a nick through his eyebrow.

It hurts in the specific way unpaid debts hurt — not sharp, just always. The trail isn't cold so much as irrelevant. If he's here, he's in the weather. Chasing weather is how you drown. Kuroishi taught me the shape of his small helpful wrongnesses; Takamatsu will teach me whether I can live in a city that contains them without sprinting myself into a grave with only one name on it.

"You're giving up," Riku says, not unkind, when I say it out loud over convenience-store coffee. Bitter. Thin. The cup warms my hands like a temporary absolution. Outside the glass, a tram sighs and a gray-green checks papers with the bored menace of furniture that has learned to bite.

"I'm holding the city," I say. "If he's in it, the city will spit him up. If he isn't, running inland until my shoes die won't resurrect anyone. Kuroishi already taught me the shape of his helpful wrongnesses. Chasing the shape through every ward is how you become a rumor with blisters."

Riku studies me. Crooked tooth. Exit-eyes. Dust in his hair from the yards. "Soft boy found a spine. Gross. Don't make it a speech."

"I'm not making a speech."

"You're making the face that precedes a speech." He drinks. Wipes his mouth with the back of his hand. "Fine. Hold. But if holding means standing still while gray-green eats someone in front of you, I'm going to kick your other ankle."

"That's fair," I say.

"Don't agree so fast. Makes me itchy." He fist-knocks my shoulder instead of the word *partner*. Light. Claimed without claiming. "Same time tomorrow. Feet. Then we find work that isn't chasing ghosts. Ghosts don't pay. Ghosts don't feed you. Ghosts make soft boys invent rescues and buildings invent poetry."

I don't ask what the knock costs him. Some currencies you don't count out loud. Holding tastes like unfinished coffee and the decision not to ask every persimmon granny whether she has seen a nick through an eyebrow. The decision hurts. The decision is still the only one that leaves me with feet.

---

That evening I walk the tram line alone for an hour because holding a city requires knowing what it sounds like when it pretends to breathe.

Harbor air. Diesel. Someone frying something that wants to be squid and isn't. A granny arguing genially about a persimmon tree. Gray-green at a Plaza edge checking papers with bored menace. Three streets off, the city simply goes on being itself, loud and ordinary, and I catch myself cataloguing exits out of habit and then cataloguing smells instead. Riku would call that applause if he said anything. He doesn't. He's somewhere pricing a cut-through behind the yards.

On a tram I catch my reflection in the black glass and almost apologize to it. Soft face. Dust. The pale patch behind my ear that I still do not name even to myself. Outside, mansion lights wait on the hill — polite, brightening by a fraction as if the building were a pupil. Somewhere under them, I tell myself, is only architecture.

I am very good at lying to ledgers when the numbers hurt.

A woman across the aisle softens when I give up my seat without being asked — the old useless charm, manners as weather. I file it and do not spend it. Spending it feels too close to the helpful wrongnesses Kuroishi whispered about. If Kaito is learning to finish clauses, I am learning which of my own soft tricks I refuse to aim. The refusal is small. The refusal is mine.

Somewhere in this geometry a loud boy may be wiping blood off a mouth that used to apologize for nothing. Somewhere a girl with calm eyes may be teaching him to finish clauses. I hold the cup of the city's noise in both hands and do not chase. Holding is not giving up. Holding is refusing to become another unfinished sentence in someone else's mouth.

Riku finds me at the flophouse stairs with two onigiri and no speech. Chain grease on his knuckles. Exit-eyes tired in a way he will not admit.

"Eat," he says. "Tomorrow we survive another Tuesday. Try not to apologize to it first."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were making the face." He fist-knocks my shoulder. Light. Claimed without claiming. "There's a bar two ridges from salt that feeds working boys on principle. Thursdays especially. When your ghost stops paying rent in your head, we go. Until then — feet, work, hold."

I eat. The rice tastes like charcoal and stubbornness. Outside, Takamatsu keeps being a harbor with a hill. I keep being a soft boy with a ledger and a bruise-map and a friend who will not say the word partner.

For now, that is enough spine to stand on.
