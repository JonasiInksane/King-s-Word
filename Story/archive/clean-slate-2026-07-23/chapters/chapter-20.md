# Chapter 20 — Across the Market
*POV: Haruki*

I see him across the night market and the world becomes a held breath.

Kaito. Nick through the eyebrow. Dark jacket. Blood-bright under the nose he wipes like a habit — the same wipe the alley witness described, the same handwriting I have been trying not to chase through lockdown. Not rumor anymore. Not ghost. Here. Uphill from me. Walking out of a mansion that smells like polished wood and quiet money, the smell I will later walk into without a map that loves us. Mira at his shoulder like punctuation: blunt dark hair, mild face, the stillness that fills doorways without warmth. The anti-warmth that once said *it wasn't your fault* in a story I only half-believe and cannot stop filing. Lantern light between us — paper skins, oil, a skewer vendor shouting into a night that has decided soft lockdown is optional if you sell food loud enough. Patrol gray-green cutting the service road into obedient lanes. Two of them. Verification theater. Asset posture. The city's polite fist. Riku's fist in my sleeve, already reading exits, already saying with bone what his mouth will say in a second.

Neither of us can move. That is the mercy and the cruelty. No chase. No reunion. Just two boys who used to share a train bench looking at each other like opposite shores of a river that learned to be a border. The market noise goes strange and distant, like sound underwater. Skewers sizzle. Paper lanterns rustle. Someone argues about change. All of it far away. The held breath is the only real thing.

He looks older. Or I do. Or the thing in his mouth does — the thing that makes witnesses open gates and go home, the thing that left a humming old woman in a story I keep filing under weather and failing. Echo. Clean sentences. The helpful wrongness that is not weather no matter how many screens say contained. His eyes find mine and for a fraction of a second the fraction is a Rail Loop morning: wet hair, scraped knuckles, *personality disorder, Haru*, the car warming because he laughed. Then the fraction dies. What remains is blood and a mission face and Mira's hand near his elbow like a compass that only points to useful. Useful. The word sits in my chest like Machi's verdict on ideals — they decide who starves.

I open my mouth. Nothing useful lives there. *Are you eating. Are you sleeping. Did you mean to become a sentence.* All of it is unfinished. Unfinished is how people around him die. I learned that from an alley witness and from Nagihama and from every night I washed dishes instead of chasing. I close my mouth. He closes his. The symmetry is obscene — two closed mouths, two boys, one held breath, zero reunion. The mansion behind him holds its pupil-bright stare. The hill keeps the peace, someone said in Minato. Peace is a quieter kind of owning. I feel the owning from here like a draft under a door.

Then the crowd closes and he is gone uphill — jacket, nick, Mira's mild punctuation swallowed by lanterns and gray-green — and Riku says, "Don't," before I can invent a verb.

"I wasn't," I say.

"You were," Riku says. "I felt your sleeve inventing. Soft boy, listen: if you cross that road tonight you become a report. Reports don't get bowls. Reports get rooms."

I know. Knowing does not make the held breath exhale. The breath stays stuck — lungful of market oil and almost and blood under a nose that used to apologize for nothing. We stand in the spill of the market until standing becomes ridiculous, then we walk back toward Minato like errands still exist. My hands shake once, privately, inside my pockets. Riku pretends not to see. That is his kindness: exits and pretending. Present tense. Better than partner. I follow him because following is a finished job and finished jobs are all I trust tonight.

The brown lantern is still lit when we reach the bar. Machi does not ask where we went. Machi prices faces. She sets barley tea without speaking. Gorō's corner stool is occupied. For now. For tonight. The ordinary mercy of a man with one beer taxing the room for facts.

---

The hill takes Gorō the next afternoon.

Not loud. Verification. Protection squeeze with manners — the kind of gray-green that knocks like a neighbor and leaves with a person like inventory. No screaming. That's how you know it's expensive. Minato's Thursday weather collapses into a flat Tuesday. Machi's face goes the way faces go when they have already counted coffins in another harbor and refuse to count this one early. Her forearms still. Her chopstick bun still. The stillness is not surrender. It is funeral math held back by stubbornness. Gorō's corner stool sits empty with the insult of emptiness. His one beer is not poured. His ginger-root hands are not taxing anyone under thirty for facts. The bar keeps breathing because Machi refuses to feed trouble a closed door, but the breath is thinner now. A stool is a small thing until the hill reaches for it. Then it is a wound.

"They don't get to keep him," Machi tells us, which is not permission and not a plan — it's a temperature. She says it while wiping a counter that does not need wiping, the same way I wash glasses that do not need washing. Jobs that finish. Jobs that lie. The lie is the only honest thing in the room — we are pretending ordinary work still exists while the mansion runs Takamatsu like a spreadsheet with gardens.

Riku loads his bag. Knife. Tape. The dry-fire lessons living in his shoulders. Strategy priced in alleys and bleach-lot exits and the curriculum of stance and breath he never quite taught me because soft boys invent verbs at the wrong moments. "Strategy can eat my boot now."

I nod. The kitchen ideal hardens into a local job so fast it scares me. Reshape means nothing if you won't stand for the people in the room when the hill reaches for them. Last night I stood for the whole world and the bar laughed and a still man asked *whom* and I said *I don't know yet*. Today the world is one stool. One man. One beer unpoured. Kuroda's coin — *reshaped by whom?* — rattles in the chest-pocket where I keep expensive questions. I do not know his name. I know the coin. I know the question. I know Gorō's gearbox eyes and tram-memory cut-throughs matter more than my unfinished subject line.

Last night the answer was *I don't know yet*. Tonight the answer is smaller and sharper: by whoever walks into a mansion for an old man with one beer and too many facts. Not the world. The stool. The cardigan buttoned wrong in a side parlor I have not seen and can already picture because Gorō's tram-memory has been drawing maps in my ear for weeks — service corridors, laundry stairs, depot logic from a driver's window, the arithmetic of a city that still believes in stamps.

We don't have a map that loves us. We have Gorō's cut-throughs. We have Machi's voucher-voice on a kitchen phone, calling a contact who owes the bar a favor and will pretend a delivery needs two boys who look like trouble and sound like errands. We have my soft talent for being believed when I shouldn't be — stickiness Riku hates, stickiness I hate, stickiness that opens doors and feels like borrowing someone else's hands. I don't have a word for it yet. I won't own it tonight. Tonight it is a tool. Tools are allowed if they free a stool's rightful occupant. Riku has fists and exits. Somewhere inside the hill, Kaito has grammar. I tell myself those facts can share a ledger if I don't shake the page. I tell myself I am not hunting Kaito. I tell myself until the telling becomes a breath I can hold.

"You saw him," Riku says while we check the bag a second time. Knife. Tape. Rice balls Machi will press into our hands at the door. Not a question.

"Yes."

"He see you?"

"Yes."

"And you still walking up."

"For Gorō," I say. "Not for him."

Riku's crooked tooth shows in something that is not a smile. "Keep saying that. Maybe the hill will believe you."

Machi stops us at the door with two rice balls and a look that prices funerals. "If you free him, he walks. He is not a package. If you can't free him, you come back breathing. I don't collect heroes. I collect living idiots who wash dishes."

"Tell him—" I start. Tell him what. Tell him the stool is waiting. Tell him Thursday weather collapsed into Tuesday and we noticed. Tell him soft boys finally learned strategy and strategy tastes like boot leather.

"Tell him yourself," Machi says. "That's the point."

At dusk the mansion lights look like teeth. Not birthday cake. Teeth. I apologize once, privately, to nobody — to Ryn, maybe, or to a Rail Loop bench, or to the soft boy who thought holding a city meant not chasing ghosts. Tonight the ghost is uphill and the living man is in a parlor, and ledgers can hold two truths if you don't shake them: I am not hunting Kaito. I am retrieving a stool's rightful occupant. If the truths collide in a hallway, that is the hill's grammar, not mine. Collisions happen. I have been holding my breath since the market. Another held breath won't kill me. Letting Gorō become inventory might.

Riku clicks his tongue once. "Survive a Tuesday, soft boy."

"That's my line," I say, and it comes out thinner than joke.

We start walking uphill. The air tastes like rain and paper and the aftertaste of a held breath that never fully left. Service road. Verification language on phones we don't check. Mansion pupil bright ahead. Behind us Minato keeps its door open because Machi refuses to feed trouble a closed one. The brown lantern fades at our backs. Gorō's tram-memory leads in my ear — left at the laundry damp, stair the captains don't watch, garden wing where koi pretend politics don't exist. Belief opens doors. Fear opens them too. I have been borrowing both without naming either. Naming comes later. If later survives Tuesday.

Somewhere ahead, polished wood and quiet money and a filing hand who collects boys. Somewhere in the same house, maybe, blood under a nose and a calendar square darkening and a boy who used to laugh on a Rail Loop bench learning to answer with grammar. I am not hunting him. I am walking through a kitchen door I have been stuck on since Thursday — the door between *I don't know yet* and the hand that holds the spoon. I still don't know who holds it. I know who needs walking out of a parlor. I know who needs to tell Machi he walked. That is enough grammar for one dusk.

Between those somewheres, my best friend's blood under a nose, and a coin I still can't spend, and a kitchen door I am finally walking through without knowing who holds the spoon.

The hill gets closer. The teeth get brighter. I do not invent a verb. I walk.
