# Chapter 6 — Say It Clearer
*POV: Haruki*

I found out the truth in an alley that smelled like wet cardboard and old fish ice.

The morning had been ordinary in the cruel way: Sugi's rice steamed into the kitchen light, Kaito's quiet sitting at the table like a third plate, Mira's absence stretching long enough to make me hopeful and then stupid. Hope is a trap with nice manners. I keep forgetting that. Outside, the port did port things — nets, diesel, widows checking the east as if the horizon might apologize. Inside, Kaito practiced not opening his mouth, and I practiced not watching him practice, and we both failed politely.

Sato came to the kitchen door with a satchel and a split lip healing wrong and asked for "the loud one" like she was picking up a delivery. Mid-teens, Mira's runner energy worn thin into something sharper. She didn't look at me long enough to rank me until later. Kaito stood. I stood. Sugi's glance said *don't*, and my feet said *ledger*, and the ledger won.

"You don't have to," Kaito said, already at the door.

"I know," I said, and followed anyway, because incomplete entries itch worse than honesty.

We found her two lanes over, cornered between a shed wall and stacked crates that had once held something living. Gray-green with a gun on her chest. Not theatrical. Work. The satchel was full of name-crates — Mira's paper machinery, the kind that moves people between being counted and being disappeared, stamps and lists and the soft violence of bureaucracy with a nicer smell than rifles. Sato's eyes flicked to Kaito, then to me, then back to the gun, ranking us in order of usefulness. I came in third. Fair. The soldier's face was bored in the way that means dangerous: a man filling a form with a body.

"Drop the gun," Kaito said, and then — catching himself, building it the way Mira's absences had been teaching him — "Drop the gun and step back three steps."

The soldier did both. Even. Clean. Nobody home in the eyes. Fingers uncurling. Boots reversing. Three steps exactly, like the world had become a stage and the script was short. The gun hit cardboard with a stupid soft sound — not dramatic, not cinematic, just metal kissing damp pulp. Sato's breath came back in a sob she tried to turn into a laugh. Somewhere a gull screamed. Somewhere a hose ran. The alley kept being an alley.

"Good," she started. "Okay. Okay, we—"

Another shadow at the alley's mouth — second soldier, late to the joke, rifle half-raised, face already deciding we were the problem — and Kaito's head whipped and his mouth started a sentence that didn't finish:

"Don't—"

Sato froze.

Not metaphor. Body locked, eyes wide, mid-flinch, mid-word, a statue the world had made out of a girl who had been mid-escape. The unfinished clause hung in the air like a blade with no handle. The second soldier's shot went wild in the panic of a man watching a person become furniture, and the wild shot found her upper arm, ugly and non-fatal and loud. Blood bright against dark cloth. She bit a rag while a widow from nowhere — coast widows always from nowhere when you need them — packed the wound and swore in a dialect that made Sugi's kitchen sound polite. Kaito stood with blood under his nose and his hands open like he didn't trust them. The first soldier still stood three steps back, blinking, empty-handed, a joke that had stopped being funny.

I picked up the satchel because someone had to. The name-crates inside were ordinary paper until you understood what ordinary paper could do. Names. Routes. Stamps that turned a body into a file that could walk. Mira's mildness made into ink.

"Get her to the shrine path," the widow snapped at me, not at Kaito. Ranking again. I helped Sato walk. She hissed every third step and refused to lean on Kaito, which was its own kind of ledger entry.

---

Later, on the path above the port, with the east still a bruise on the horizon, he said it without looking at me.

Salt wind. Fishing boats like small stubborn commas on the water. Below us Nagihama pretended it was only fish. Sato was somewhere learning how to hate saints with lists; Jun was at the shrine, sitting without crying, still trusting the wrong boy for the right reasons. The path grit under our shoes sounded too loud.

"It's me," Kaito said. "When I say it."

The sea kept being the sea. I had known. Knowing and hearing are different ledgers. Hearing puts a date on the column and closes the polite fiction that *luck* was doing the work.

"Since the roof," I said.

"Since before the roof, maybe. I don't — I don't know when it started being real." He laughed, short, the ruined radio voice cracking. "Mira says clarity. Who. What. No spare words. Finish every clause." He glanced at my face and flinched from whatever he saw. "Don't look at me like that."

"How am I looking at you?"

"Like I'm a problem you're trying to solve without hurting my feelings."

"You are," I said. "And I am."

A fishing boat's light crawled. Somewhere a radio muttered containment in a font nobody trusted. Kaito's hands flexed open and closed, open and closed, like he was practicing not making fists or practicing not making sentences. The nick through his eyebrow caught the late light. He looked fifteen and ancient in the same breath.

"I'm not going to hurt you," Kaito said.

"That's not the promise that scares me," I said.

He didn't ask which one did. The quiet between us had started speaking in commands of its own. I wanted to tell him about the birthmark — same pale patch behind the left ear, same place I'd seen on the train when his collar tore, the match that had gone onto my private list next to a soldier's grip opening and a lieutenant saying *do not listen to a single word he says* — and put it on the table like evidence. I didn't. Evidence wants a verdict. We weren't ready for court. Soft boys keep secrets the way knives keep edges: quietly, until someone grabs wrong.

"If you keep using it," I said carefully, "it will use you back. I don't have a proof. I have a throat that sounds like yours and a hand I watched open."

"Then what do I do when they put a gun on a girl?" he asked. Not angry. Hungry. The hunger Mira had been feeding with absences and clean little sentences. "Apologize at it?"

I didn't have an answer that wasn't a joke. Jokes were how I survived answers. Tonight they wouldn't land. The alley still smelled like cardboard in my nose. Sato's blood was still a color I could see with my eyes closed. The unfinished *Don't—* sat between us like a third boy neither of us could carry home.

"I don't know," I said, which was the most honest sentence I'd given him since the vending bank. "I know unfinished is worse. I know loud is worse. I don't know what better looks like when the other option is a gun."

He nodded once, like I'd handed him a tool that didn't fit his hand. "Mira does."

"Mira has a list," I said. "Lists aren't the same as better."

Wind pushed grit into my teeth. Below, a fishing boat's wake drew a temporary white line and erased it. Kaito wiped under his nose even though there was nothing there yet — habit learning its rent early.

Behind us, in the alley we'd left to widows and cardboard, a man in a coat too warm for the season picked up the dropped gun with two fingers, looked at it as if reading a sentence, and set it down again exactly where it had fallen. Not stealing. Not helping. Cataloguing. When I turned fully, only the cardboard remained, and a wet dark spot that might have been water or might have been the afternoon deciding what to keep.

We walked home on opposite sides of the path. Friendship strained thin enough to see light through. Not broken. Not yet. Gulls argued over something that had been living an hour ago. Diesel coughed awake for the evening boats. The shrine roof caught a last scrap of sun and threw it away. Below us, widows moved between doorways with the practical speed of people who have learned that curiosity is a luxury stamped papers don't cover. I kept wanting to ask him about the pier — Jun's temple, the story that had arrived already dull — and didn't, because today's alley had used up the day's honesty budget.

Mira waited on the sea wall with her mild face and her open palm nowhere visible — just a woman in dark clothes looking inland and seaward at once, like both directions were already owned. Wind pushed her sleeve up a fraction; the pale scar flashed and vanished. She did not wave. She did not need to. Kaito's shoulders turned toward her half a degree before he caught himself and turned back toward Sugi's light.

Half a degree is how roads begin.

I counted his steps. He counted something else — clauses, maybe, or the exact distance at which a gun becomes a sentence. The cat would be waiting on the shelf with its one milky eye, and Sugi would have rice, and none of that would make the alley unhappen. In the kitchen doorway I almost said *stay*. The word sat on my tongue like a soft command I didn't trust myself to spend. Soft commands are how I move rooms. I wasn't ready to find out if they moved him.

I swallowed it. He went inside first. Water ran in the sink a long time — practice or washing, same sound from the hall. Mira's silhouette on the wall stayed until the lamps came on, then dissolved into road dark the way hunters do when they've finished taking inventory. I stood in the entryway sand until my shoes remembered they were mine, and then I went in and sat where I could see both the door and his hands, ledger open, numbers tipping, still pretending tipping and falling were different verbs.
