# Chapter 2 — Let Her Go
*POV: Haruki*

I met Kaito under the dead 'K' at six with a cake box balanced on both hands like it was a sleeping animal.

Saturday gold had already dipped the west windows in fire — June humidity turning the air amber, the whole city looking tea-stained and almost soft. The eyebrow nick, the scraped knuckles, the jacket he never washed: he looked at me and said, "You look like a butler," and I said, "You look like you slept in a batting cage," and we were both right. Best evening of my life. That first. Before the white. Before the hand.

We did it in the wrong order on purpose. Ramen first — six seats, a cook who never spoke, broth that tasted like someone's grandmother had been simmering it since before the war. Steam fogged my glasses. Ryn arrived late and pretending she wasn't, hair down, cowlick free, no apron, silver stud catching every lamp. She slid onto the stool between us and stole a slice of menma off Kaito's bowl.

"Birthday tax," she said.

"Extortion," Kaito said, delighted.

"Economics," she corrected, and the cook set down three waters without being asked.

Then the arcade, where the carpet smelled of coin metal and sugar and old victories. Game-flash painted Ryn's face in strobes of blue and red. She destroyed us both at the light-gun, then destroyed a stranger who challenged the high score, then bought him a drink from the machine to apologize, because that was Ryn: take your dignity, then worry about you. Claw machines lined the back wall like a jury of soft animals. Kaito spent nine hundred yen failing at a hideous cat plush — orange, lopsided, one plastic eye already milky — while I stood behind him trying not to laugh loud enough to jinx it. I won it on the second try by accident and had to pretend I'd let him "warm the machine up." He threw an arm around my neck and called me a traitor and a hero in the same breath. Ryn flicked its ear.

"That thing is a war crime," she said. "I love it. Give it here."

"You planned that," Kaito accused me. "You *let* me lose."

"I let you warm up the machine," I said. Let. Just a word. We threw it around all night — *let me try, let it go, let's go, let her win, no let HIM win* — the small loose imperatives of three people who trusted each other completely, language worn so smooth none of it had any weight at all. On the street between games Ryn walked between us and linked elbows like we were a single organism with six legs.

The war app buzzed twice on the walk. Both times I turned it face-down without looking. On the Daigo screen the lying font said EASTERN LINE HOLDING, and for the first evening in a long time I let the names pass unread, because it was Ryn's birthday and I had decided — the way the whole city decided every morning — that nothing was wrong.

One moment I should not have forgotten.

On the Rail Loop up to the school, emptying evening car, a man by the far door who did not belong to the evening. Still in a way people aren't still — not waiting, not tired. Watching. Mid-thirties or forties; short unremarkable hair; a face designed to be forgotten; plain dark coat too warm for June. No phone, which alone marked him strange. When Kaito laughed too loud and the car softened around him the way rooms did, the still man did not soften. He looked at Kaito. Then at me. Then he took out a phone after all and turned the screen toward us for a second — not filming, just *showing* — text too far to read — and the doors opened at the hill station and we tumbled out laughing, and I forgot him completely.

I would give a great deal to have not forgotten about him.

---

The rooftop chain was already broken. We climbed into open air and the city was *there* — all of it at once — Rail Loop a glowing ring, Sakaba-dōri a smear of pink and gold, the river holding the sky upside down. Out east, past where the bright stopped, a low uneven orange along the horizon we all understood and none of us named. Height makes everything look like it isn't happening to anyone. That's the terrible part.

Cake on an upturned crate. Candles. Kaito produced a lighter from somewhere, because Kaito could always produce a lighter from somewhere. Small flames leaned in the warm wind. Wax dripped onto frosting my mother had made in a kitchen that still believed in birthdays. Ryn looked at us over sixteen little fires with an expression I'd never seen — unguarded, almost frightened by how much she was allowed to have. The hideous cat sat beside the cake like a guest.

"Make a wish," Kaito said.

"I know how birthdays work, idiot."

She closed her eyes. The city breathed under us. Candle-hiss. Distant traffic. "I wish nothing ever—"

The sky went white.

Not lightning. Not fireworks. A clean, wrong white that ate the candles and the pink neon and the lying font on the Daigo screen and left a sound behind it like a building learning how to scream. My phone shrieked in three pockets at once — J-Alert, the low third tone we all pretended we'd never hear this close — LINE BROKEN. RUN WEST. Heat slapped us a half-second later, dry and enormous. Out east the orange became a shape, or the absence of a shape, Anomalous Materiel written in light the eye refused to finish.

The rooftop door exploded inward.

Federation gray-green spilled across the concrete like a net that had practiced this building. Boots. Gunlights. A man in the middle with a watch he checked as if the calamity were late — Lieutenant Kanzaki, though I didn't know the name then, only the stillness. He looked at Kaito. He looked at me. He smiled with none of his mouth.

"There you are," he said. Mild. Almost fond. "We've been looking for you two."

One of his men took Ryn — wrist, collar, practiced haul — while Kaito was still stepping forward to put himself between gray-green and the rest of us. The soldier walked her to the low lip of the roof and stood with his boots planted and his arm locked straight, holding her out over the lit-up bowl of the city like a receipt he was willing to tear. The cake crate tipped. Frosting hit concrete with a soft stupid sound.

"Both of you," the soldier said, mint and boredom. "You come quiet, or I drop her. That's the whole conversation."

Ryn's sneakers hung in thirty meters of birthday air. Her free hand clamped around his forearm because there was nowhere else to put it. Wind pulled her hair across her mouth. "Haru," she said. Very quietly. Very reasonably. "Don't. Don't do anything stupid. He's—"

"Hands where I can see them," the soldier repeated. "I said *quiet*."

Ryn's eyes flicked past me — to Kaito — then back. "Okay," she breathed. "Okay. Just — don't—"

Kaito went for the edge. Heat of him. The way the air always leaned. A gray-green reached for his collar; Kaito put an elbow into the man's throat without looking and didn't slow — and then he was beside me at the lip, face-to-face with the soldier who already owned it. Close enough to grab. Too late to grab. Ryn's sneakers still kicking empty air past the soldier's hip: one arm locked out over the drop, one girl as leverage, the whole city waiting under her.

He didn't think. Thinking was my job. He lunged for the grip with one hand and fisted the other in the back of my shirt for an anchor he didn't ask permission for — my ribs hit concrete; my breath left — and he put every raw terrified furious ounce of himself into his voice—

"**LET HER GO!**"

I have heard those three words my whole life. *Let it go, Haru. Let her win. Let me try.* Nothing words. Soft idle grammar. *Let her go.* Means *release your grip.* Means *stop holding on.* Means *be gentle.*

The soldier's hand opened.

It was not violent. That is the horror I have never been able to put down. Not a wrench or a throw. His fingers simply uncurled — all at once, evenly — and his face didn't change. Nobody home. A command, and a hand obeying before the man could even be surprised that the drop he'd promised had arrived on someone else's schedule.

He let her go.

Ryn's free hand scraped concrete. Fingernails. A sound like chalk on stone. Her eyes were still on me. She didn't scream. In the quarter-second she had, with the city lights streaming up past her, she looked at me, and her mouth moved, and the kindest lie I let myself believe on the worst nights is that it was shaped like *okay*.

Then the space where she'd been was just city lights.

Kaito made a sound that wasn't a word. I held him by the jacket so he wouldn't follow her with his body. Below us the Daigo screen stuttered FRONT STABLE into static. Kanzaki's radio crackled, calm as a weather report:

"Confirmed. It's the Word. The loud one is active. One casualty — instructive. Do not listen to a single word he says. Cover your ears if you have to. Both boys. Alive."

The east stairwell chose that moment to become fire and concrete rain. The net broke the way practiced things break when the building disagrees. We ran — not clever, not brave, just the animal math of *west* — down through smoke and a vending bank and a service corridor my mother had shown me once for a drill nobody believed in, and by the time we hit street level Kaito's voice was already a ruined radio and there was blood under his nose that wouldn't stop for a polite reason, and I had a sentence sitting on my tongue — *it wasn't your fault* — and I did not say it.

That is the first crack. Not the roof. My silence.

"Okay," I said instead, useless, steering him by the sleeve into the river of people already flowing toward the coast road. Ash was falling like gray snow. "We go west. She said west."

Behind us Kōgetsu learned how to die in stages. Ahead of us the road kept unrolling. Between us, three words that used to mean nothing, and a hand that had opened like it was being helpful.
