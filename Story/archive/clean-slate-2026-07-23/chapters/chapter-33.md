# Chapter 33 — Zero
*POV: Kaito*

Mei's door opens on empty air.

Not empty — wrong. A specials bunk with the geometry of recent departure: blanket thrown, water cup still sweating, the particular absence of a person who chose a different map sixty seconds before your schedule arrived. Suzumori's hum spikes sharp — recognition without blessing. Mira's mildness cracks into recalculation so fast it almost sounds like grief if you do not know her.

"She's not here," Mira says. "Corridor. The flood. Move—"

I am already moving. The Word wants a clean extract clause — *come, stay, walk* — and finds no living target with that name in the room. Living minds only. Absence does not obey. I taste Echo and wax and the childish sweetness of A/B still pouring through hatches I cleared by asking their owners to become weather. Parallel plans. Same locks. Suzumori warned me. Warning is not the same as stopping.

We hit the gallery slit overlooking Level Two as Habu's voice finds the PA like a soft hand finding a throat.

"Inventory zero," Habu says, as if reminding a class of a due date. "Completed forms prefer clean floors. Seal and vent. Prioritize the corridor column. Do not waste the east anomaly — contain. Gallery crews, stand ready. Protocol is active."

The night tears indoors.

Metal shutters slam both ends of the volunteer flood like eyelids deciding the dream is over. Recycled air turns thick, chemical, wrong — purge vents exhaling something that burns the nose before it burns anything else. Not my sentence. Not my grammar. Not the scrape in my throat answering like a second tongue. The building's binder finishing its math in a sealed box. Habu's contingency. Inventory that can be zeroed. I know this the way I knew disposal through glass: as a film I cannot stop and a schedule I did not write.

Children cough.

Then children fold.

The sound is worse than a roof, worse than a pad, worse than a plum tree teaching unfinished *stop* what unfinished costs. Not one command. Not one hand opening over a city bowl. A system clearing its throat with gas and seals and the polite patience of fluorescents that keep working while contents stop. Concrete dust jumps. Armbands scatter like dropped receipts. A kid from B reaches the core hatch, fingers on the cold lip, and the lip does not save him. Someone screams. Someone else tries to make the scream into a name and loses both. The HVAC skips once, then resumes as if embarrassed.

I watch a cough-laughing boy lift halfway up and stop being a person mid-gesture.

Something in me that STAGE 4 was supposed to have filed — hallway boy, loud, raw, the flinch that used to mean *proof* — comes back wrong. Not a flinch. A hole. The kind of hole that opens under your feet when the world shows you what people are capable of and does not bother dressing it as accident. Habu's soft voice. Soft. The same soft that called a child inventory without changing tone. I thought soft was Haru's weather. Tonight soft is how adults erase rooms.

The Word offers clauses — *stop, open, breathe, live* — muddy, huge, aimed at a corridor full of dying and at vents that have no will to override. Doors never listen. Machines never listen. Weather never listens. Only living minds, and the living minds that own this protocol are in galleries with ear protection and soft voices and the religion of completed forms. The sentences stack behind my teeth like guns arguing with their own safeties. Mira's hand finds my wrist — hard, brakes, equation.

"Don't shout," she says. "Habu wants a vocal signature in the column. You paint this as your weather and the feeds write you as the wipe. Extract. Mei. Now."

Suzumori's eyes are open. Bright. Old. "The children—"

"Are inventory he decided to finish," Mira says, and the cruelty is that she is correct about Habu and wrong about what correctness costs. "Your mouth cannot unvent a building. It can still take a girl."

I look at the corridor.

I will never forget the light. Lockdown amber. Floor wax shine. Bodies becoming less like sentences I can finish. A girl tries to go back for someone already gone and is redirected by scarred knuckles — Mei, in the dust, choosing cells over the empty bunk we opened for her, hauling living first because dead don't need an escort. Riku uses his body like a door. Somewhere in that river a soft face I left as a speck under rotors may be drowning in protocol. I do not find him cleanly. Finding would be a clause. Clauses are failing the only test that mattered.

My last decency does not die quietly.

It dies loud.

The hole in me fills with something that is not Mira's clean justice and not STAGE 4's flat schedule. Despair first — thick, stupid, childhood-sized — the kind that makes you understand, too late, that people will gas a hallway of kids and call it a completed form. Then anger arrives on top of it like a second weather system colliding with the first. Not clean. Not useful. The old loud boy standing up inside the tool and refusing to be filed.

Mira's grip tightens. "Kaito—"

I tear free.

I put every raw terrified furious ounce of myself into my voice the way I once put it into *let her go* on a birthday roof — except this time I am not aiming at one soldier's hand. I am aiming at a building. At adults. At the soft voice that made children into a stamp. At the whole world that keeps calling this weather.

"**If I could—**"

The Word hears the picture behind the sentence. Heavier hand. Not muddy collision. Not a bar confession laughed into toast. A schedule wearing desire like a glove and slamming the glove into every living skull that still has ears.

"**I'd reshape this whole world to free it of this warfare.**"

It leaves my mouth like a roof breaking.

Not metaphor. Volume. Grammar. Intent leaning so hard the speakers in the gallery stutter and the PA eats itself for half a second and Habu's soft manners get overwritten by a ruined radio that has remembered how to be loud. The clause hits the complex the way Echo hits a nose — metal taste, pressure, a frequency that does not ask permission. Gallery crews flinch even through ear protection. Shutters hum. Somewhere a soft captain drops a tablet. Somewhere in a maintenance niche a boy who once roared those same words at contractors in Minato while they laughed will hear them come back wrong — angrier, heavier, finished by a throat that has stopped asking the world for manners.

I hear myself keep going. Not Haru's careful kitchen. Mine. The heavier hand.

"Not your screens. Not your soft fonts. Not your inventory and your clean floors and your peace that knocks like a neighbor and leaves with a child. I am tired of calling it weather. Weather doesn't seal a corridor. Weather doesn't vent kids into a stamp. If the world can be stirred—" My voice cracks on the seam and comes back uglier. "**I will stir it. Free of you. Free of this.**"

Echo runs freely. Blood under my nose like dialect learning a new font. Mira's face does something I have never seen on her — not mild recalculation, not equation hunger. Alarm. The cost she priced and I paid without asking.

Suzumori does not hum.

Habu's feeds just got their vocal signature. I know. I do not care. Caring died on the wax with the cough-laugh and the hatch lip and the hymn that lost its throat.

Rage stays.

Cleaner than grief only in the way a fire is cleaner than a damp room: it burns what was left. The kind of clean Mira has been teaching me to mistake for justice — except tonight I am not mistaking. Tonight I am choosing the burn. Habu does not need my weakness. He has protocol. He has seals. He has a soft voice and a tablet. What he does not have is a monopoly on finishing.

"Mei," Suzumori says, pointing with her chin through the slit — scarred knuckles, practical hair, still moving, still counting. "There. Mid-reach. We can still—"

We push into the spur.

Gray-green tries to become a wall; I do not ask them to sleep. Sleep is a mercy word and mercy is a language that just failed a corridor of children. "Drop. Kneel. Stay." Bodies hit concrete hard enough to sound like punctuation. Not the children. The children are past my verbs. Folded soldiers are a cleared path toward a girl who is dragging a skinny wrist toward the core while vents keep exhaling the end of a world. Mira recalculates strides like a woman rewriting an equation that just learned it can explode. Suzumori hums through chemical wrongness as if humming could braid a path through zero. For three seconds I think the extract can still complete — who, what, finished — Mei's name as a destination, my hand on her arm, Remnant dark waiting like a bird that has not learned this building.

A shutter drops between us and the hatch lip.

Not metaphor. Steel. The building choosing which column gets to finish. Mei's face flashes once through the narrowing gap — blunt, warm, furious at freeze as a concept — and then the gap is a line and then the line is a wall. Mid-reach. Mid-extract. The mission holding a door for nobody. Suzumori's hum tries to make the sealed steel into a season and fails the way keepers fail when the target has already chosen a different map and the map has been taxed by gas.

"Open it," I tell the nearest living mind with a key — clean, hard, finished, still loud with the reshape still ringing in the vents — and he opens what he can. Not enough. Secondary seals. Purge logic. Habu's binder outrunning my sentences by the width of a contingency page. Mira pulls me back from a vent wash that would make my lungs into inventory. Suzumori's small hand is dry in mine for one beat; then she is turning, Remnant runners signaling extract-fail weather, the kind of retreat that keeps a Bellkeeper out of FDC hands even when the number walks away into dust.

"We leave," Mira says. "Now. Recalculate outside. If you stay in the vent plume you become a body Habu can stamp as anomalous actor — and you just painted your name on the wipe."

"The kids—"

"Are zero," Mira says, and does not soften it. "Mei is not. Haruki may not be. Your mouth already spent the speech. Spend the next one on the people who own the knobs. Move."

I do not move like a boy. I move like a schedule that just watched itself become irrelevant and answered by screaming the world into a different shape. Through a final slit before runners drag the angle blind, I see the volunteer corridor settle into a quiet that is not peace — completed quiet, floor-wax quiet, the quiet of forms that prefer clean floors. My last decency does not come back. Something else stands up in its place: not flat yet, not hollow yet — angry, loud, a voice that has learned the building's dialect and decided to answer in kind. Not at children. At the adults who own knobs and rifles and soft PA microphones. The Word purrs at the thought. STAGE 4's hallway lengthens. STAGE 5's door shows its edge like a tooth.

Suzumori looks at me and for once does not hum.

"Do not become his weather," she says quietly. "Habu erases. You are still supposed to—"

"Supposed to," I repeat, and the reshape is still in my throat like a second tongue that tasted blood and liked it. "Keep moving."

We clear into a service stair that tastes like bleach and failure. Behind us the purge completes its manners — vents cycling down, shutters holding, gallery crews reporting columns the way clerks report rain, and somewhere a recording of my voice already learning how to become a lie. Mei is not secured. The soft boy is a rumor in dust who may have just heard his own confession come back with a heavier hand. The children of A and B are a stamp. My nose bleeds freely; I do not wipe it. Blood is a dialect. The stair learns it.

Mira watches my face the way she watched me on a bird leaving Takamatsu — equation-bright, hungrier now that the tool learned volume. "When we regroup—"

"We don't regroup for extract," I say. The clause builds itself without permission. Who. What. Finished. Aimed at a complex full of gray-green that still has pulses. "We finish the people who finished them."

Mira does not argue. Arguments are for missions that still have a girl in hand. Suzumori's silence is older than argument. Somewhere above, Habu's soft voice may still be speaking; somewhere the feeds are already clearing their throats for a lie that now has my exact words as a signature. I walk down steps that feel like a calendar. The last decency stays dead on Level Two where the vents taught me what inventory zero means when it is not a metaphor — and where I answered by promising the world a reshape it will not survive cleanly.

Purge complete.

Mei still not secured.

The Word awake, pleased, offering clean clauses for a massacre I have not yet named — and will name soon, because naming is how schedules become weather, and weather is all I have left that answers when children do not.
