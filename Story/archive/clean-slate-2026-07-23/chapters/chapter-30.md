# Chapter 30 — Inventory
*POV: Haruki*

Rumors arrive through vents before they arrive through mouths.

Relocation weather. Processing weather. Three volunteers down on a wall display and no corresponding three on a transfer sheet Mei makes a junior guard show us by smiling until the tablet becomes public. The junior looks sick. Mei looks steady. Riku looks like a door that has decided to become a fist. I look like a ledger whose columns are tipping and cannot pretend tipping is panic this time.

"Numbers left," Mei said yesterday. Today the building answers: numbers do not relocate. Numbers get zeroed.

Confirmation comes in pieces that refuse to stay rumor. A guard in the crossing laughs wrong about medical adjacency — *adjustment night*, *usefulness cleared*, *inventory loss before dawn*. Soft voice, Habu's dialect secondhand. Another guard tells him to shut up and looks at us like irregulars have ears that invent trouble. Through the vent above our bunk, a cleaner whispers to another cleaner that the gowns went down and did not come up, that Yano marked charts cheerful as a school nurse, that Habu's tablet did not change tone when the total dropped. Inventory loss. The phrase fits the air the way it fit the glass lobby when a child stumbled and Habu filed him as stock.

Riku goes still in a way that is not stillness. Stillness is the coat at Minato. This is a wound standing up inside a boy and finding the room too small.

"Say it," he tells me, crooked tooth bare, voice low enough to cut. "Don't soft it. Say what the kitchen is."

I say it. Because ledgers that lie become Habu's lullaby.

"They're disposing volunteers. Not transferring. Zeroing. Kids who saw sweeps. Kids with inconclusive flags. Kids who were never in the books, so the books don't have to bury them — only erase them."

Riku laughs once. Ugly. Human. Cleaner than grammar. Then the laugh breaks into the roof-sake night he already paid me in pieces: inland ridge smaller than Kuroishi, sewn armband, youth civil-defense, FDC's cheapest meat. Rifles that month. A boy fourteen, flagged asset-adjacent, running his stretch. A soldier shouting *stop him*. Riku firing because runners were the story they sold him about neighbors' kids and perimeter duty. The flag coming back inconclusive — one afternoon of theirs, years of his. Armband folded on a bunk. *They don't chase volunteers — you were never here.*

He does not say the dead boy's name. He said he would say it sober or not at all. Tonight is sober in the worst way. The name stays his. I do not invent it for you. Inventing would be another kind of filing.

"I was their gun," Riku says to the glass, to me, to the vent that carries gown-weather. "And this place is where the guns go when the Federation finishes needing the story. Volunteer. Never in the books. Disposable by design." His hands flex. Exit-price eyes gone red at the edges. "I taught you so nobody gets to use you. Not paper. Not causes. Not friends with beautiful reasons. And I was paper. I was the cause. I was the beautiful reason with a rifle."

"You were a kid they pointed," I say. Not absolution. Not judgment. The same line I gave him when the empty bottle got poured as thank-you: the boy got carried, not filed — the worst possible way for it to be something. "This building doesn't get to make that the only story left."

Riku's mouth twists. "Your kitchen speech. Soft boy still thinks spoons rewrite stacks."

"I think Habu calls children inventory without changing tone," I say, and my voice surprises me by holding. "I think that means someone is stirring. I think we don't get to call it weather anymore — not from inside the pot."

Ideals bruise. Of course they bruise. Bruises are how ideals prove they had blood in them. The Minato roar — reshape, free, kitchen, hand on the spoon — sits in my chest beside Habu's tablet and the vent's gown-whisper and Kaito's flatter voice somewhere in the same war, scheduling deaths and calling them freedom. *Reshaped by whom?* The still man's coin still won't spend. Tonight *whom* might be a colonel with soft manners. Tonight *whom* might be a louder boy with Echo on his face. Tonight *whom* might be me if I keep refusing to own the stall on the pad and still want the spoon. Yet remains unfinished. Yet remains the softest blade I own. Yet is getting sharper against Quiet Field's glass.

---

Mei does not give a speech.

She appears in our corridor with two volunteers trailing her like correctable errors and a stolen marker for a wall display that has been lying for an hour. She updates the number herself — not to pretend the zeros returned, to refuse the display the right to look complete. Then she sits on the edge of Riku's bunk without asking, scarred knuckles loose, smile gone but not replaced by theater-grief.

"Road dog," she says. "You're loud inside. Make it useful or it becomes Habu's soundtrack."

Riku bristles, wounded into mean. "Don't manage me, war stamp."

"I'm organizing," Mei says. "Management is what they do with charts. Organizing is what we do with exits." She glances at me — warm, blunt, a soft romance seed so light it could be only attention if I were less of a ledger. "Soft boy. You still counting faces?"

"Always," I say.

"Good. Count who can walk, who can fight, who freezes, who sells. Don't count ghosts until we can carry them." Her eyes hold mine a beat longer than chore requires — not a confession, a check: are you still here, still useful to your own tally. My ears heat. Riku clicks his tongue once, filing the heat as billable later. Mei's mouth almost grins. Almost is enough. "Fear is boring," she adds, lighter. "Revenge is boring too if it doesn't open a door. We open doors. Speeches after."

She maps without paper: service core rumor, loading bay geometry stolen from a cleaner's complaint, PA codes that mean lockdown versus codes that mean count, the east wing specials route that brushes C when rotations get lazy. No banners. No recruit pitch. Just geometry placed in our hands like oden bowls — eat, or starve on pride.

"Breakout," Riku says, tasting the word like blood. "Not cute. Not soft. Out."

"Out," Mei agrees. "With as many of the volunteer arithmetic as we can steal back from the zeros. I'm not dying as legitimacy for anybody's story and I'm not watching gowns go down while I practice smile-math."

She does not say Remnant. She does not say heir. She does not say a birthday name that would turn this cell into a shrine. She says out, and the word fits the bruised ideal in my chest better than reshape did in a bar full of contractors who laughed.

I look at Riku. Partner-in-practice, fist-knock instead of the word. His volunteer wound is open and wet and somehow cleaner than Habu's soft voice. "I'm in," I say. "Kitchen door. Other stir. I don't know whom yet. I know this stack doesn't get the last count."

Riku fist-knocks my shoulder hard enough to invoice. "Don't apologize for the decision after. I'll bill you if you do."

"I won't," I say, and almost mean it.

Mei's gaze flicks between us, gravity steady. "Dawn after next if the bay rumor holds. Soft boy keeps ledger of who freezes. Road dog prices the fists. I take the displays and the lazy rotations. If a louder problem arrives from outside — Remnant weather, Word weather, pad weather — we use the noise. We do not wait for saints."

Pad weather. My stomach files the entry. Two Voices under panic. Kaito west of a lift, flatter, colder, maybe already tasting this building's arithmetic through glass I cannot see. If he is coming for a name, if Mira is rewriting equations mid-corridor, if a Bellkeeper hums through Quiet Field — then locks may open from two sides without either side admitting the lesson. I do not own that hope cleanly. Hope is muddy. Mud is how boys die under plum trees. Still — the ledger underlines coincidence as a tool.

Night does not take us as sleep.

It takes us as planning breath: Riku naming boot intervals, Mei correcting his intervals with a smile that is almost kind, my penless columns filling with faces who might walk. Through the vent, medical processing invents another soft sentence. Somewhere Habu's tablet stays calm. Somewhere Kanzaki's hunt continues under a watch that once timed a calamity. Somewhere a still coat may pass a lobby without sticking — optional edge, unread, unpaid. I do not chase coats. I chase a door with a handle on our side.

Ideals bruise and stay alive. That is the only victory Quiet Field has offered: bruise means blood, blood means the kitchen speech was not only theater, theater is what Habu wants when he calls murder inventory.

"Haru," Mei says at the glass, quieter. My name in her mouth is a soft seed, nothing blooming yet, just soil. "When we run, you look at me if the ledger tips. Not at the zeros. Zeros want watchers. Watchers become next."

"I'll look," I say, and the promise is small and dangerous and mine.

Riku watches the promise land and does not mock it. Progress. Or trauma. Or both, stuck together with spite the way we have always been.

Resolve leans toward breakout the way plants lean toward any light that isn't fluorescent mercy. I keep the page open. I tell you the vent-truth, the wound, the girl who organizes without speeches, the soft heat of a name said quietly in a cell that invents sky without offering it. I still file the pad under panic. I still refuse to call myself a Voice. I no longer refuse to call this place a kitchen that cooks children.

We are going to steal the spoon.

Not tomorrow's dawn — the dawn after, if rumors hold — but the lean has started, and leaning is how soft boys become doors other people can walk through without becoming inventory first.

Quiet Field has begun its count. We have begun ours. Volume is not ending. The next page is an exit.
