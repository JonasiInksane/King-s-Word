# Chapter 35 — Massacre

Concrete stairs bit down through the service core's blind side, and Riku cut the path like exits were a language the building had forgotten to seal.

Pipe sweat. Rust smell. Ozone. The hatch Habu's contingency had forgotten to reseal because contingency assumed zero left no witnesses worth fencing. Mei had the keys and the floor's geometry; she corrected our path the way she'd corrected a count — chalkless, absolute, no speeches for the dark.

I was weight between them, shame with blisters, the boy who had opened a door into math and still didn't know how to close anything that mattered.

Gray-green recovered in patches — men blinking out of kneel, radios screaming, Kanzaki's soft fury somewhere behind the amber like a watch that refused to be late even to ruin. Boots turned toward our sector. Toward the core. Toward the last irregulars who had not yet become a line on Yano's stamp.

"Don't look back," Riku said.

I looked back anyway, through a landing slit, and heard what looking couldn't hold.

Habu's voice on the PA first — soft, broken, forced into honesty the way a locked jaw is forced open.

"—Shizuhara is not relocation. It is disposal. We zeroed the volunteers. We—"

The sentence frayed. Spent. Habu's confession emptied itself into fluorescent air and then stopped being useful, because a confession is not a resurrection and Kaito had already taken what he needed from the living mind behind it. Whether Habu fell after, or fled, or hid in a binder's shadow — I couldn't swear. The amber made liars of distance. The PA clicked once and went to static.

Then Mira's clean grammar, carried in Kaito's mouth, aimed not at children — the children were already gone — but at the armed staff who had run the wipe.

"Gallery crews who fired on the corridor — turn your weapons on each other. Fire until none of you can stand."

A tower of wet cracks rolled down through concrete. A radio became a scream that cut itself short. Level Two ate its own hands.

"Processing staff who sealed the vents — walk into the open corridor. Kneel. Do not rise."

Boots. Knees. Another chord of obedience, colder than the first, wider, the kind of weather that doesn't ask which face deserves it — only which living mind had held the purge.

"Admin who called the contingency — drop your papers. Face the glass. Stay."

Paper fluttered. Glass reflected nothing useful. Nosebleed bright on a boy who used to argue soccer. Voice flatter than any hallway I'd known him in. Mira at his side like a drawn conclusion, watching the stove finally burn at the temperature she'd wanted.

Not the roof-voice. Not *let her go* aimed at leverage. This was grammar with nowhere left to mishear.

That is what the feeds would call it, later, when they needed a name big enough to hide a disposal building: the Shizuhara Massacre.

We hit the loading bay.

Diesel. Cold air from a half-open shutter. Tire marks on sealed concrete. Mei shoved me under a truck's shadow while Riku checked the service road with his stolen sidearm and a curriculum that had learned paranoia as survival. Behind the bay doors, through concrete thick enough to make murder sound like weather, I heard the progressive clearing continue — floor by floor, command by command, Mira's clean sentences wearing Kaito's spent throat.

Gallery. Processing. Admin. Stair posts that had aimed down. Men who had sealed shutters and called it manners.

Riku yanked me harder. Mei's scarred knuckles stayed locked on my sleeve until the bay light died behind us and only amber ghosts remained in the shutter gap.

"Keep moving," she said. "Road. Now."

I heard myself say something about fact-tolls and unpaid coins — nonsense from another life, Gorō's stool, a bar that still believed in mornings — and then I was only running.

The service road took us into inland dark. Gravel under feet. No neon. No pastoral fiction. The sound of Quiet Field eating its staff followed like a second HVAC — wet, human, scheduled — then thinned with distance until it became something you could almost pretend was wind.

Branches of scrub whipped my face where the ditch met the road. Roots tried to invent a fall. Mei hissed left; Riku shoved right; somewhere a recovering boot crashed brush and then lost us to deeper dark. We did not stop to invent heroism. Heroism had already been stamped zero.

A radio somewhere in Riku's pocket — stolen gray-green habit — screamed orders that no longer belonged to living mouths. Another answered in a voice that was already wrong. The road took the rest and made it weather.

Mei's breath came sharp beside me. Riku's did not. Curriculum had taught him to spend air like money.

"Cut," he said. "Then we stop inventing."

---

Dawn found us in a drainage ditch two ridges from anywhere Habu had bothered to map. The cut held cold water and last night's dust and three living bodies that hadn't earned morning. No armbands. No kids' cough-laugh. Just breath, and the taste of iron that wouldn't wash, and a sky trying on ordinary blue like an insult.

Birds started. That was the insult's second verse.

I washed my hands in the cold cut until the water ran less red and my fingers still remembered lifting. Mei watched without commenting. Riku watched the ridgeline like a curriculum that had learned paranoia as a survival skill.

Riku sat with his head back against dirt, eyes shut, curriculum smashed and somehow still breathing. A scrape along his forearm had already decided to bruise.

Mei cleaned a cut on my cheek with bottled water like the scrape mattered — practical, unsentimental, devastating in the way care is devastating when you feel undeserving of it. Neither of them asked me to rebuild the world before breakfast.

"Drink," she said, and shoved the bottle at me.

I drank. It tasted like plastic and not being dead.

The stolen radio crackled in the weeds. Civilian band overlay. Government calm. The kind of voice that makes disasters sound like weather reports.

*—confirmed Remnant terrorist action at the Shizuhara Youth Annex—*

*—vocational processing site—*

*—casualties include relocated youth volunteers murdered by anomalous actors—*

*—public advised the situation is contained—*

Contained. Managed. Loss, translated into chyron.

Mei laughed once, sharp enough to cut the broadcast.

"There it is. They zeroed the proof and rented your jacket the credit."

Riku opened his eyes.

"Of course they did. Cheapest meat never gets a truthful funeral."

I listened until the radio became weather again. Somewhere Kaito was still in that story — louder now, flatter, a Voice the feeds could blame while Habu's binder burned or hid. Somewhere Mira stood confident in the blood. Somewhere Suzumori hummed toward a Remnant that had failed to collect what it came for, because the girl they'd named had chosen chalk and cells instead of their map.

The whom-question sat unpaid in my chest, heavier, bloodier, no longer a bar coin.

*Reshaped by whom?*

The ridges did not answer. The radio lied for a living. My hands still remembered lifting a child who hadn't made the bay. My chest still remembered a roof where *let her go* had opened a hand on leverage and killed the girl it was meant to free.

Mei bumped my shoulder, gentler than last night's slap.

"You're not useless. You're late to a uglier job. There's a difference."

I didn't believe her yet. Belief would take miles and food and a morning that didn't taste like a completed form. But I stood when they stood, and I walked when they walked, and shame walked with me without getting to choose the road.

Riku checked the radio once more, then killed the volume with a thumb like closing a ledger he refused to keep.

"Inland," he said. "No neon. No heroics. We eat when we find food. We move when the road allows."

Mei nodded. "And we don't let the feeds write our names for free."

I looked once more toward the direction of Quiet Field, though the ridges had already stolen the amber. Whatever Habu had become — dead, hiding, filing — the building had already taught its lesson.

Behind us, Shizuhara would wear a massacre's name forever.

Ahead, the inland road had no neon and no promise — only the three of us, a media lie already hardening on the chyron, and a best friend who had stayed in the fire until the fire learned his voice.

Volume closed on gravel and static: children erased, truth scrubbed, one boy dragged toward living, one boy crowned in someone else's atrocity, and Shizuhara pretending — for the cameras — that the kitchen had never been a building.
