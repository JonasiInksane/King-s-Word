# Chapter 25 — Lift

The last flight smelled like tar and pine and money that had decided to leave town.

Riku took the stair two at a time. A gray-green grabbed for him from a landing niche; he broke the grip the way he'd broken mine in three towns, planted, and put the man on the steps without wasting a bullet. "Exits above are the sky," he said. "Don't invent poetry."

I didn't. I climbed.

We spilled onto the mansion roof into floodlight and rotor-wash. The H-pad was a pale rectangle that had never been on any heritage plaque. A craft squatted on it, engines warming, Remnant-ugly, practical. Mira was already at the hatch. Beside her — small, calm, an old woman in a dark wrap — stood as if the night were a bell she had heard before. She hummed under the noise, a thin thread of sound that didn't care about floodlights. When the air went wrong later, she would be the only person on the roof who did not flinch.

Suzumori, though I didn't know the name then. Only that she looked at the boys like kept things, and at the sky like a schedule.

Kaito stood between us and the hatch.

Jacket. Blood dried under his nose. The scheduled face from the study. When he saw me finish the stair, he didn't say my name like a bridge. He measured distance.

"Haruki," Riku warned, low. "He's going to talk."

"I know."

Gray-green boiled up the stair behind us — three, then five. Riku turned, fired once into the concrete lip to make them duck, not to collect bodies. "Soft boy. Whatever you're going to do, do it knowing the door closes."

Kaito lifted his chin.

"You with the gun," he told Riku, exact, Mira-clean. "Drop it. Step back three steps. Sit."

Riku's fingers opened. The sidearm hit the pad. He stepped. He sat. His jaw clenched so hard I heard teeth. Eyes furious and awake — he knew what had him. Knowing didn't unlock his knees.

Then Kaito looked at me.

"Stop," he said. "Stay where you are. Do not follow. Do not touch the craft."

The Word landed like a hand on my sternum.

For a blink my feet agreed. Soft boy. Obedient weather. The old shelf where I filed luck and charisma shuddered.

And something in me — panic, refusal, the kitchen thought, Gorō running, Ryn's unfinished wish on a roof where leverage had opened the wrong hand, the mild man calling us wells — shoved back without asking my permission.

"No," I heard myself say, not loud, not scheduled, a soft blade. "Don't. You don't get to—"

The pad *answered*.

Not the craft — living minds, the soldiers on the stair, Riku's held body, Kaito's throat, mine. Two pressures met in the air above the landlord's house and the city below flinched like a single animal. Windows along the pine road blew white. Somewhere a tram bell screamed without a tram. Floodlights stuttered. My nose went hot. Kaito's went hotter. The old woman hummed through it, eyes half-lidded, as if she were listening to a chord she had been waiting to hear.

Mira's stillness broke for the first time I had ever seen — not fear. Recalculation. She looked from Kaito to me and back, and the math landed in her face: not one weapon and a soft boy. *Two.*

"On," she snapped, and hauled Kaito toward the hatch even as he staggered. He stared at me over her shoulder — still him, farther, colder, surprised in a way that wasn't soft — and for half a second his mouth shaped something that might have been my name again, or an apology, or another command. The rotor ate it.

They pulled the humming woman in with them. The hatch sealed.

The craft lifted.

I tried to move and found my legs were mine again and useless anyway. Riku tore free of the leftover *sit* with a sound like cloth ripping and scooped his sidearm, too late for sky.

Engines leaned into the dark. Takamatsu's shortened evening lay under us like a map someone had crumpled. Kaito's craft became an uglier star and then a rumor.

The city kept shaking in aftershocks — small, insulted, alive.

---

The gray-green recovered first.

They hit the pad in a wave of polite violence: knees, cuffs, the particular competence of men who'd lost a lieutenant tonight and found two boys on a roof that still hummed wrong. Riku put one down with an empty-handed trick that would have made the lot proud; three more buried him. I swung once, soft and stupid, and ate concrete.

Zip-ties. Shoulder pins. The taste of tar.

"Assets," someone said into a radio, and the word Aihara had loved crawled into my ear like a second cuff.

They dragged us off the H-pad, down a flight that smelled like the house again, into a holding room that had been a wine cellar before it learned locks. Stone. One bulb. No cardigan. No melon soda. Riku spat blood onto the floor and tested the ties with the patience of a man who intended to be annoying tomorrow, if tomorrow was allowed.

Above us — through stone, through pine — the craft's voice thinned to nothing.

"He flew," I said.

"I noticed." Riku leaned his head back against the wall. Dry. Human. "Curriculum didn't cover sky."

"I told him no." My throat hurt. The pad still echoed in my bones. "And the city—"

"File it," he said, not unkind. "Don't name it yet. Naming is how they write you into their ledger."

The bulb hummed. Somewhere in the mansion a door slammed. Somewhere in the pines an old tram driver might still be running free. Somewhere above the wards my best friend carried an old woman and a colder voice toward whatever came next.

I sat in the landlord's leftover dark with my wrists burning and the whom-question still unanswered in my chest, and I did not know what I had done on the pad — only that I had refused, and the world had listened to more than one voice, and listening had a cost.

The lock engaged with the manners of a butler.

Engines gone.

Volume closed on the hill: two boys imprisoned, a craft in the sky, and Takamatsu still pretending evenings ended when the speakers said so.
