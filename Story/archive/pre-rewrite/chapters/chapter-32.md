# Chapter 32 — Open

The first U-bolt shrieked once and gave, and the obscenity was how ordinary the metal was.

No coded seals. No humming wire. Just storage-cage security hung on doors that pretended they kept inventory, not children. Up close it smelled like oil and disinfectant and the particular boredom of something that had never expected to be argued with by hands.

Riku hit it with a stolen rod and a knee and the kind of hate that keeps hands steady. He didn't celebrate. He was already at the next hinge, breathing through his teeth the way he did when a delivery went sideways and the only honest move left was force.

I took the second the way he'd drilled grip escapes into me in pine lots and bleach-building stairwells — angle, weight, don't negotiate with the object. My palms slipped on floor wax. I reset and shoved until the bolt rolled free and my shoulders learned a new bruise.

Somewhere behind us a volunteer whispered *what are you doing* in the tone of a person who had stopped believing doors could open for them.

The latch dropped onto linoleum with a sound too small for what it meant.

"Out," Mei said — not a speech, a favor framed as order. Shorter hair stuck to her temples, scarred knuckles white on a borrowed keyring, eyes that refused to make fear interesting. "Service core. Don't look at the gallery lights. If you fall, the person behind you lifts. Go."

Her keys sang through the inner gates like they'd been waiting for a less polite owner.

Click.

Click.

The sound was almost shy. Around us the volunteers woke into confusion that turned, fast, into the animal understanding of an open door.

They went.

Armbands and bare wrists. Coughs and curses. A kid with the water-cart cough laughed once in disbelief — a bright, cracked bark — and then ran like laughter might be revoked. A girl from B grabbed a smaller boy's sleeve and hauled him without looking back at Habu's glass. Someone tripped on a mop bucket and kept going on bloody knees. A boy from A whispered *thank you* to nobody and then never stopped moving.

A and B poured into the corridor in a dark river of too-young feet, and for one stupid luminous second the hallway looked like it had remembered what hallways were for.

The night smelled like floor wax and ozone and the hot copper of fear that hasn't decided yet whether to become grief. Fluorescent strips threw long shadows that made every runner look taller than they were. Dust rose in soft ghosts under sneakers and bare heels. Somewhere a vent kept humming as if the building still owed it a pastoral.

"Keep moving," I heard myself say, useless and necessary. "Core. Don't invent a line."

A boy with Haiga dust still under his nails stared at me like I'd grown a stamp. Then he ran.

The service-core hatch opened on the cycle — and then kept opening wider than habit allowed.

Gray-green at the stair posts turned, sat, dropped rifles, walked three steps back as if an invisible teacher had finally put an edge on it. One of them smiled while he sat, the polite smile of a man holding a door for packages. Another started counting under his breath, eyes glassy with obedience that didn't know it was obedience. A third reached for his radio, froze mid-reach, and sat anyway, as if sitting had become the only available verb.

I heard Kaito's voice through the stairwell, not loud the way he used to be loud in hallways and karaoke rooms, clean the way Mira had made him:

"You at the hatch — open it fully. Sit. Count to a thousand. Do not shout."

The cordon obeyed.

Living minds. Locks never had a chance because the people who owned them had already lost theirs. The hatch yawned. Level Two's west edge became a mouth — the same grammar that had opened a soldier's hand on a roof, leverage mistaken for rescue, now aimed at doors instead of wrists.

Riku swore, almost admiring. "There's your weather."

"Don't invent poetry," Mei said, already shoving the next cluster through. "Down. Now."

We pushed. Kids into the core. Concrete in teeth. A boy tripped on his own relief and I caught him under the arm the way you catch a crate before it bruises — stupid reflex, soft hands, useful for once.

"Keep going," I told him.

He nodded like I'd given him a stamp that mattered.

Somewhere east, Mira's silhouette cut toward Mei's number; Suzumori's hum threaded the HVAC's skip, a thin wrong music under the fluorescents. East-wing glass threw amber in broken strips. Kaito stood in the corridor light for half a breath where I could see his face — and he saw me.

Not a reunion. A collision of aims.

His mouth shaped something that might have been my name or another command. The rotor of the night ate it. For a second the distance between us was just waxed floor and the old shape of a friendship that had once argued soccer and stayed for each other's worst jokes — before the roof, before the hand opened on leverage, before the mouth learned to aim.

Then Suzumori's hand was on his sleeve, and he turned toward the east wing because that was the extract he'd come for, and I turned toward the service core because a cough-laughing kid had gone down and needed hands that still believed in lifting.

I hauled the kid up. His laugh had become a cough again.

"Don't look at the lights," I said. "Down."

He ran. I ran after the river.

Overlap. Aid. Neither of us had waved.

---

It worked.

That is the sentence I need you to hold. It *worked*. The cells emptied. The hatch stayed polite. Volunteers hit the first landing like a rumor becoming a body. Concrete swallowed sneakers. Someone whooped once and was immediately shushed by someone wiser. A girl from A clapped a hand over her own mouth as if joy were contraband.

Mei shoved a ring of keys into her waistband and grinned with all her teeth, alive in the way people are alive when they refuse to treat fear as interesting.

"Go," she told a cluster of thirteens. "Don't wait for a count. Counts are for people who still think morning is coming."

A girl hesitated, eyes on Mei's scarred knuckles. "Are you coming with us?"

"I'm chalk," Mei said. "You're feet. Use them. We'll be behind you until the bay takes the math."

Riku fired a warning shot into concrete with a sidearm he'd taken off a sitting guard — not to collect bodies, to make poetry stop inventing itself behind us. The crack rolled up the stairwell and came back smaller.

"Exits are the bay. Don't invent heroism. You invent heroism, you become a form."

I ran. Kids ran. For a stretch of corridor measured in heartbeats, Quiet Field was failing at being a building that erased children. The kitchen thought — the roar at Minato, the grin, the whole world redrawn — stood up in my chest bruised and upright, almost believing itself. *Reshape* felt, for once, like a verb with dirt on it.

My lungs burned. My blistered feet found rhythm. Somewhere Riku's shoulder bumped mine — the old non-word — and somewhere Mei's keys clinked once like a joke the complex hadn't approved.

Then the gallery lights went lockdown-amber.

Habu's soft voice came through the PA with office-polite clarity that did not belong over bodies, and every bright second we'd bought turned its head to look at the binder.

"Contingency," Habu said, almost kindly. "Inventory zero. Vocal anomaly confirmed. Seal the building. Initiate purge."

The words landed like a polite knife. Somewhere a radio answered with clipped confirmations. Gallery shutters hard-focused on the river of volunteers between corridor and core. Kanzaki's watch-glint moved on the glass walk — silver, exact, never late. Yano's medical wing lit like a smile that had already chosen its tray.

Mei's grin went flat.

Riku's jaw set.

The cough-laughing kid looked back once toward Habu's glass as if manners might still negotiate.

The loading bay was close enough to smell diesel.

The building was closer.
