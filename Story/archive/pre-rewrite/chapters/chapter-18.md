# Chapter 18 — Volunteer

The forklift's name was Momo, and the foreman loved her more than his children, which we knew because he told us so, twice, before midnight.

"My kids never lifted anything," he explained, walking Momo backward through a canyon of crates with two fingers on the wheel. "Momo lifts. Momo doesn't talk back. Momo doesn't marry a man from *Sakai*." The last word carried twenty years of a grudge nobody had asked about. That was the depot at night: machinery, grievances, and the best coffee I'd had since Kōgetsu, brewed in a pot old enough to have opinions, poured by men who believed sleep was a rumor invented by the day shift.

Night deliveries turned out to mean this: load at the depot by eleven, run the routes till four, know which streets to take wide and which gates opened to a knock instead of paper. The cargo was honest. Mostly. There was a crate every few nights that the manifest described with the poetic vagueness of a horoscope, and Riku's policy on those was the same as the crew's: lift with your legs, not your curiosity.

I liked the work more than I wanted to admit. The city at 2 a.m. was a different animal — soft, emptied out, neon cooling to embers, trams asleep in their sheds like horses. We saw bakers starting and drunks ending. A heron stood in the shallow river by the depot bridge every night at the same bend, and Riku nodded to it going past like a coworker.

"You're getting sentimental about a bird," I said.

"I'm professional about the bird. The bird shows up. That's more than I can say for your stance."

Two weeks into standing still, and my life had a shape again: feet at dawn, sleep till noon, cargo till four, Thursdays at the harbor. If you had drawn it on paper it would have looked almost like peace.

---

The third night, the route took us wrong.

Not lost — *wrong*. We came down the hill road with a van full of restaurant chairs and hit light where light shouldn't be: a residential ward, sleepy, low tile roofs, and at the mouth of it two FDC trucks parked in a wedge with their headlamps crossed like swords. Gray-green moving door to door up the lane. A sweep. Late-shift, quiet-ward, the kind that doesn't make the morning news because the morning news is also a manifest.

Riku slowed the van the correct amount — not guilty-slow, not curious-slow, delivery-slow — and we would have been past it in forty seconds if not for the perimeter.

The perimeter was children.

Not literally — fifteen, sixteen, some maybe my age — standing at arm's length from each other along the ward's edge with batons they didn't know how to hold and armbands that said **市民防衛** in stitched characters. Civil defense. One of them yawned so hard his whole body apologized for it. A soldier walked the line behind them, straightening a shoulder here, an angle there, the way you'd adjust tools on a pegboard.

"Volunteers," the soldier called them, to someone on a radio, bored. "Perimeter's volunteers, copy. They'll hold."

The word went through Riku like current through water.

I'd seen him quiet before. This was different — a stillness with machinery under it. His hands stayed perfect on the wheel. His eyes did the mirror-check rhythm exactly on schedule. He drove us past the wedge, past the yawning boy, past the crossed headlamps, delivery-slow the entire way, and said nothing for eleven minutes, and then finished the shift like a man performing a person doing his job.

I didn't ask. Three towns with him had taught me that much: you don't knock on a door while it's holding.

---

He knocked on it himself, after.

Four-thirty, pay collected, city dark. Instead of turning down our street he bought a bottle of sake from the vending row outside the shuttered liquor shop — the cheap kind, the kind that tastes like a decision — and climbed to the roof of the bleach building, and I followed, because following him was still my whole personality, except now I brought cups.

He drank the first one alone. I let him. The heron was probably asleep. The hospital held down its dark corner of the skyline and the north hill held its pines and between them the city breathed like something that trusted us.

"Sixteen," Riku said, to the skyline. "I was sixteen."

I turned my cup in my hands and shut up in the most supportive way I knew.

"Inland ridge town, smaller than Kuroishi. When the eastern wards started going, every town got a quota. Civil defense. *Youth voluntary units.*" His mouth did something that was not a smile. "Volunteer. You know what that word means, officially? It means they don't owe you anything. A soldier gets paid. A conscript gets a law. A volunteer gets an armband and a speech about the community, and if he ends up in a ditch he was never in the books. Cheapest meat the Federation ever invented, and it invented itself. Towns competed to be patriotic. Mothers sewed the armbands."

"Perimeter work," I said, carefully.

"Perimeter work." He refilled, drank. "They used us for sweeps. Same as tonight — you saw it, that ward, that's the whole design. Gray-green does the doors, and the line of local kids does the *edges*, because a runner won't hurt a neighbor's kid, and if he does, well." He shrugged with one shoulder, a bookkeeping gesture. "Volunteer."

The sake went around again. Below us a first train tested its rails somewhere, once, like a throat clearing.

"There was a boy," Riku said. "Sweep like tonight's. Flagged — *asset-adjacent*, that was the phrase, some cousin of a cousin of a rumor. He ran my stretch of the line. And I want you to hear this part right, because drunk men lie by making themselves worse instead of better, and I'm not doing that." He looked at me for the first time since the roof. "Nobody made me. The soldier behind us was shouting *stop him*, and I had a baton and two empty hands' worth of choices, and what I had instead of a choice was a *rifle*, because our unit had impressed somebody that month. And I did the thing I'd been drilled to do. I stopped him."

The city breathed. I didn't.

"He was fourteen. The flag was reviewed after — you want to know the official finding? *Inconclusive.* Not false. Not true. Inconclusive. They spent one afternoon on it. I've spent—" he counted with a small motion of the cup "—three years."

"What was his name?"

Riku was quiet for a long moment, and when he answered, his voice had the flat care of a man carrying something full to the brim.

"I know it," he said. "I say it sober or not at all."

He poured again; I put my hand over my cup, and to my surprise he nodded like I'd passed something. He drank mine anyway.

"I walked the next week. Left the armband on my bunk, folded, like a good boy. They don't chase volunteers — that's the other thing the word means. You were never here." He tipped the bottle, found it light, set it down with exaggerated gentleness. "So no, I don't wear gray-green even when it's free at the market. And no, I don't do speeches about causes. And when your Federation friends call a line of children *volunteers* on a radio like it's a supply category—" the stillness came back for a second, machinery under water "—I finish my shift. Because the other thing I could do helps nobody and gets a bird's route changed."

I thought about Nagihama, about a rooftop where a soldier had held Ryn out over the lip as leverage and Kaito had shouted *let her go* and the hand had opened too evenly, about the absolution I had once held behind my teeth like a coin I couldn't spend, and how much that silence had cost the person who needed it — and how saying *it wasn't your fault* would have been a lie then and would be a lie now, and how there had to be a third thing, because two wasn't enough.

"You know his name," I said. "Three years, and you won't say it drunk because it deserves better. The men who flagged him spent an afternoon and filed him under *inconclusive*." I set my empty cup upside down, which felt correct in a way I couldn't defend. "I'm not going to tell you it wasn't your fault. You pulled the trigger; you'd throw me off this roof if I pretended otherwise. But there's a version of that night that stays a *file*, and there's a version that gets carried up a roof three towns later by a man who checks on a heron. He got the second one. That's not nothing. That's just the worst possible way for it to be something."

Riku looked at me for a long time. The sky was doing its first gray thinking about morning.

"You talk like a funeral for a living," he said at last, "and yet."

He refilled my cup from the empty bottle — a ceremony of nothing, both of us watching air pour — and we drank the nothing, and that was the thank-you, and I knew better than to want more.

---

Morning was a war crime.

"Up," Riku said, at what my body understood to be four minutes later. He looked appalling and moved like he didn't. "Feet. Then the range."

"You drank more than me."

"I drank *professionally*. You drank like an exchange student." He threw my boots at the bedroll. "Today you get live rounds. All of them."

The lot first, where my hangover and I failed the same grab twice and then, third time, didn't — feet before mouth, hips turning, out — and Riku, instead of the usual grunt, stopped the drill.

"Again. Not because it was wrong. Because it was right, and I want your body to file it under *always* instead of *lucky*."

Then the borrowed range behind the tool shop, sandbags sweating dust in the early heat. He set me up, checked my grip twice, and worked me through a full magazine for the first time — slow fire, two hands, the world narrowing to sight-breath-press until the punch of it stopped being an event and started being information. He corrected everything. He corrected things he'd let slide for two weeks. Sharper, harder, no jokes between rounds, and it took my slow morning brain until the reload to understand that this was the same conversation as the roof, continued by other means.

"Last night you asked why I bother," he said, watching me clear the weapon. I hadn't asked it out loud. He answered it anyway. "Here's the whole curriculum, so you never have to ask again: I teach you so nobody gets to use you. Not paper. Not causes. Not friends with beautiful reasons. *Nobody.* You want to stand in front of a machine — fine. You'll do it as a person who chose it, on his own legs, knowing exactly what his hands can do. The day someone turns you into their tool is the day I failed twice."

He took the sidearm, safed it, and held out his fist. I looked at it.

"This is the part where you don't say anything," he advised.

I knocked my knuckles against his and didn't say anything, and the word *partner*, which he had refused in three towns and I had stopped offering, sat between us on the warm morning air, unspoken and, for the first time, unnecessary.

"Thursday tomorrow," Riku said, holstering the gun in the crate he called an armory. "Try not to win any more arguments about snow. The tofu people are starting a file on you."

"Everyone in this city has a file."

"Yeah," he said, squinting at the bright ordinary street like it had done something right for once. "But theirs has *broth* in it."
