# Chapter 11 — A Step Behind

The salt died somewhere between the second switchback and the third blister.

Sugi's onigiri had lasted that long — plum and charcoal clinging to the wrapping even after the rice was gone — and then the west ridge road was only dust and hot rubber and my feet filing complaints in a language I couldn't argue with. Below me Nagihama shrank to a gray smear and a wrong calm. Ahead, inland, the Federation still pretended the war was something you could manage with polite announcements.

I ate the last bite standing under a roadside cedar because sitting would have felt like admitting I could rest. Sugi's voice came with it — *come back as yourself if you can* — and then Jun under the plum tree, and then Kaito's soft useless *I'm sorry*, and then the empty jacket hook. The ledger did not need me to open it. It ran itself.

I walked.

---

Kuroishi announced itself with paper before it announced itself with people.

Clipboard men at a bend that was not quite a checkpoint — gray-green without the full theater of trucks, just enough uniform to make refugees flatten against the ditch when they passed. A bus depot with a schedule board someone still updated in tidy characters, as if the buses had not learned to lie. One long main street of shuttered tool shops and a croquette cart that had never moved in its life. Above the street, a stone shrine gate black with old soot. Black stone. The name fit the way names sometimes do when towns are tired of pretending.

I asked for two people without saying their names.

A woman selling tea in paper cups looked at my shoes and decided I was not FDC. "Boy and a girl?" she said. "Girl with the calm eyes. Boy who talks like he's used to being listened to." She poured anyway. "They came through yesterday. Maybe the night before. People get the days wrong when they're scared."

"Which way."

She pointed west with her chin, inland, further from salt. "Toward the bridge town. Ishibashi. Or that's what the croquette man said, and he lies about oil prices, so."

I almost laughed. It came out wrong — too thin — but it was still a laugh, and for half a second the road felt like a road instead of a chase.

Then she added, quieter, "You don't want them for the usual reasons, do you."

"No," I said, and that was true enough to hurt.

---

The rumors were not rumors of a monster.

That was the problem. Monsters you can hate cleanly. What Kuroishi had was a set of small helpful wrongnesses, passed from mouth to mouth like a bad cold.

At the depot, a porter told me a checkpoint line had parted the day before — not because a sergeant waved anyone through, but because a tall boy at the back had said something mild about *moving*, and the men with rifles had stepped aside like the air had been rearranged. "Lucky," the porter said. Then, after a beat: "Didn't feel lucky. Felt like someone borrowed my feet."

Outside a boarding house with a cracked noren, a landlord smoked and would not meet my eyes. "I don't take refugees," he said, automatic. Then his mouth twisted. "Except I did. Night before last. A girl asked if we had a room. I said no. Then I said yes. I don't remember deciding. Charged them less than the paint is worth. My wife says I'm getting soft. I don't feel soft. I feel—" He searched for a word that was not *stolen*. "I feel like I agreed to something I didn't agree to."

The shape was Kaito's even when the town refused to give him a name. Not catastrophe — the soft theft of other people's *no*. Mira would be beside him, teaching him how not to leave loopholes.

---

FDC found me the way paper finds people who look like witnesses: by asking a question that was already a net.

I was buying water near the soot-gate when a clipboard stopped in front of my chest. Young soldier. Mint on his breath that made my stomach turn for reasons that had nothing to do with candy. "Papers," he said. Polite. "Evacuation registry. Origin ward."

I had Sugi's stamp-story still clinging to me from Nagihama — sick grandson, fever, ordinary — and it was already thinning in the inland air. I fumbled. The soldier's eyes did the thing eyes do when they have been trained to look for witnesses.

"Kōgetsu," I said, which was true and also the wrong truth.

His pen paused. "Kōgetsu is sealed."

"I know."

"Then you're either lucky or lying."

I opened my mouth to apologize — reflex, useless — and a hand closed on the back of my collar and yanked me sideways hard enough that the water bottle hit the asphalt and rolled, absurd, toward the croquette cart.

"He's with me," said a voice I did not know. Flat. Bored. "Volunteer runner. Depot list. You want to argue with the depot, argue with the depot. Don't waste ink on kids who can't even hold a bottle."

The soldier looked at the speaker. Then at me. Then at the bottle, which had come to rest against a tire like it was embarrassed. Something in the clipboard man's shoulders loosened — not kindness. Cost-benefit. He clicked his pen shut and moved on to a more promising refugee.

I turned.

The boy who had grabbed me was about seventeen, lean in the way of people who get muscle from not dying rather than from gyms. Dark hair tied with a scrap of cloth; scraped knuckles; boots that had opinions about roads. He looked me up and down and grinned — crooked front tooth, sudden and stupidly bright.

"Soft boy," he said. "You walk like the road owes you manners."

I should have been angry. What came out instead was a laugh that surprised us both — real, ugly, the kind that belongs to konbini nights and not to chase mornings. "I walk like my feet are filing a complaint."

"Yeah. I heard them." He nodded at the bottle. "Pick that up before the croquette man adopts it."

I picked it up. "You didn't have to—"

"Don't thank me. Makes me itchy." He eyed the gray-green disappearing down the street and his mouth flattened for half a second — not fear exactly. Something older. Then the grin came back, thinner. "Name's Riku. Nakahara. You can buy me a croquette or you can keep looking like a witness. Your call."

"Haruki," I said. "Aoyama."

"Aoyama Haruki." He tested the weight of it. "Sounds like someone who apologizes to vending machines."

"I have," I admitted, and he laughed with his whole face, the way Kaito used to before the volume went wrong, and for one dangerous second the inland heat felt almost like Sakaba-dōri.

We ate croquettes on the curb. Oil on our fingers. The cart's owner complained about prices and Riku complained back with the fluency of a boy who had argued with every cart between here and the front. He did not ask why FDC wanted me. I did not ask why he had a volunteer-runner story ready that he clearly hated saying.

When I tried to give him money, he took one of Sugi's remaining onigiri from my bag instead, unwrapped it like contraband, and hummed approval.

"Widow rice," he said. "Better currency."

"How do you know—"

"Smell." He stood, dusted his hands. "You're chasing someone."

It was not a question.

"A friend," I said.

"Friends don't usually leave a trail that makes landlords feel robbed." He watched my face, then decided something without announcing it. "I'm not joining your tragic road movie. I have a job that pays in actual yen sometimes. But if you walk into another clipboard like that, you're going to get loaded into a truck, and trucks are boring."

"I wasn't asking you to join."

"Good. Because I'm expensive." He jerked his chin west. "Ishibashi. Bridge town. Fresher trail that way — diesel pump says a tall kid made a quartermaster *hand over* spare batteries without a form. Same flavor as here." He started walking, then glanced back. "Don't follow me. Follow the rumor. And buy better shoes. Those are funeral footwear."

"They're school shoes."

"Same thing, lately."

He was gone around the soot-gate before I could decide whether to shout after him. Oil on my fingers. Direction I already half had. And one word he had used as cover that made his mouth go flat: *volunteer.*

I did not understand it yet.

---

By late afternoon the blisters had names.

I wrapped my heels with a strip torn from a spare shirt — nurse-mother competence, the domestic skill that survives apocalypse — and kept west. Kuroishi fell behind the way Nagihama had: too calm in the wrong places, too loud about paper. The ridge road climbed again. Heat came off the asphalt in soft waves. Far east, the bruise of the front still glowed under the sky like a bad memory that refused to set.

I did not use my mouth for anything that sounded like an order. I asked. I apologized. A farmer with a truck let me ride in the bed as far as a turnoff because I looked like someone's grandson and because the world still contained that kind of math. He talked about weather and battery prices and a rumor that inland towns were getting *lucky* in ways that made old people nervous.

"Luck that sits you down," he said, and laughed, and then stopped laughing. "My sister in Kuroishi told me. Said it helped a kid get past a line. Said she didn't like how the help felt."

"I know," I said.

He glanced at me in the side mirror. "You chasing the luck, or running from it."

"Yes," I said, and he snorted, which was almost kindness.

At the turnoff he left me with a bottle of barley tea and a warning not to sleep near the drainage ditches where the gray-green sometimes checked for runners. I thanked him too many times. He waved it off. Soft boy reflex: paid in excess gratitude.

I walked until the light went copper.

Ishibashi was not a town yet — only a name on a faded road sign and a rumor with fresher teeth. Somewhere ahead, on a bridge I could not see, Kaito was learning how to build sentences that closed. Mira was teaching him which words left doors open. People were agreeing to things they had not agreed to, and calling it luck, and tasting metal afterward.

I sat on a guardrail and ate nothing and listened to my feet complain and let myself, for three breaths, remember Kaito knocking my shoulder on a sea wall and saying he was not leaving *me*.

He had left anyway.

Not because he stopped loving. Because loving had found a louder tool — the same mouth that had told a soldier on a roof to *let her go* and watched the hand open on leverage, not rescue. I had been a step behind ever since.

I stood. The empty water bottle from the soot-gate was still in my bag, absurd and useful. Ahead, the rumor had fresher teeth. Behind me, Kuroishi held paper and croquette oil and a laugh I was already spending like currency.

I followed the road.
