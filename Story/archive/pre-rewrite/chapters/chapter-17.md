# Chapter 17 — Thursdays

"Left," Riku said.

"Your left or the lantern's left?"

"The ladder's left. The ladder is the only one of us with a future."

We were halfway up the covered arcade with sixty paper lanterns on a handcart, and the wind had found the one gap in the shotengai roof the way wind always finds the exact place you don't want it. The lanterns were for the summer festival — weeks away still, but the merchants' association believed in preparing for joy the way other towns prepared for typhoons — and every one of them was rice paper and bamboo and lighter than a lie, which meant that every gust turned our cargo into a flock of large glowing birds attempting to emigrate.

I caught one out of the air without thinking — pivot, two steps, hand up — and only realized afterward that my feet had done it in the stance Riku had been kicking me into for three weeks.

"Who taught you that," he said, from the top of the ladder.

"You did."

"Correct. Say it louder. The association president is watching and I want a raise."

The association president was a round, sweating man with a clipboard — a *friendly* clipboard, the first one I'd met since Kōgetsu, which took some getting used to — and he did in fact watch me field two more escaping lanterns before declaring, to the street at large, that the tall rude boy and the polite one were now his problem every festival season until further notice.

That was the shape of the week. Gorō had said one word about us to the merchants' association — I never found out which word — and the work stopped being whatever paid and started being *ours*: lanterns, kegs, a piano that had to descend three floors and was carried the whole way like an unexploded bomb, a shrine gate that needed painting before the priest's mother-in-law visited. Takamatsu paid us in yen, in leftovers, in gossip, and once, memorably, in eel.

"City's deciding whether to keep you," Riku said, when I pointed out that we hadn't been threatened by anyone in eleven days. "Don't get sentimental. It keeps whoever shows up twice."

We kept showing up.

---

Thursday arrived the way holidays used to.

The good broth was real. I want that on the record. Mama Machi's Thursday oden was to weekday oden what a live orchestra is to a phone speaker, and she served it with the grim satisfaction of a woman winning an argument nobody had dared to start. My melon soda came in its beer glass — a standing order now, set down with ceremony, toasted by Gorō with complete solemnity — and Riku got his beer and the corner table got its regulars and the little bar filled up with the specific warm noise of people who had decided, together, to be fine for one evening.

Gorō collected his fact-toll at the door. I paid with the water tank on our roof that I had been assured was structural and was fairly sure was empty; he ruled it acceptable and, in exchange, gave the bar forty years of city in one story — the night flood of '02, when the low crossing by the depot went under and he'd driven his tram dead slow through black water with sixty stranded shift workers aboard, headlamp lighting rain, everyone singing to keep the mood up because a young conductor had panicked and someone had to fix the air.

"What did they sing?" I asked.

"Everything," Gorō said. "Badly. That's the trick of it, melon soda. Nobody drowns during a bad chorus. It's not physically possible."

Mama Machi, drying glasses, said the flood was the last time the city had felt like one animal. Then she looked like she'd said more than she'd meant to, and went back down the counter, and the tofu couple by the wall — two small old people who shared one bowl and had never once finished it — started an argument about whether the '02 flood or the '09 snow had been worse, which was apparently a twenty-year-old argument and had rules.

The rules, as far as I could reconstruct them: each party was allowed one new piece of evidence per year; the evidence could not be a thing the other had personally witnessed; and the loser refilled the tea. They appealed to Gorō, who declared himself a flood man with snow sympathies and was accused by both of cowardice. They appealed to Riku, who said he charged for arbitration. Then, to my horror, they appealed to me.

"The snow," I said, and the tofu grandmother sat back with the satisfaction of a lawyer resting her case, and the tofu grandfather demanded to know what a boy with a summer face could possibly know about '09, and I said snow was worse because floods gave you something to do and snow just sat on you and waited, and the whole bar went quiet for a second in the way I was learning meant a point had been scored on Takamatsu's invisible board.

"He stays," the grandmother told Machi, pointing at me with a chopstick.

"He was already staying," Machi said. "Drink your tea. You lost."

I sat in the middle of it with my glass sweating into its coaster and thought: I am learning this city the way Riku said to. Not ransacking it. Living in it. And it was working — I knew the wards now, the tram bells, which konbini clerk saved the unsold karaage for the arcade kids. What I hadn't expected was that the city would learn me back.

---

The door curtain moved at nine.

The man who came through it wore a suit the color of weak tea and carried a ledger book, and behind him, outside, two gray-green stood in the lane with the placid patience of furniture that could arrest you. The bar didn't go silent. It went *careful* — conversations continuing at exactly the same volume with none of the same ease.

"Evening, Machi-san." The suit had a soft voice and a municipal smile. "Association security contribution. Adjusted for the season."

"It was adjusted in spring."

"The season," the suit said pleasantly, "has been adjusted."

Mama Machi took a tin from under the counter and counted notes into his ledger without letting her face spend anything at all. He stamped a receipt, complimented the smell of a broth he made no move to taste, wished the room a safe festival, and left. The gray-green peeled off the wall and followed him up the lane toward the next lit door.

The bar breathed again — one degree flatter.

"Twice since spring," Gorō muttered into his beer. "The hill eats twice since spring, and my line got shortened for *materiel*—"

"Gorō." Machi didn't raise her voice. One word, flat as her counting face.

He drank. The tofu couple resumed the snow of '09. Nobody explained anything to us, which was itself an explanation.

Later — kegs down, chairs up, Machi pressing containers of broth on us "for the walk," which was not a distance that required provisions — Gorō caught Riku's sleeve at the door.

"You two want steady?" he said. "Night deliveries. My old freight boys at the depot lost two loaders to conscription paper and they're drowning. Honest cargo, mostly. Good pay, bad hours. I'll say the word."

Riku looked at him for a second. "That's twice you've vouched for us, old man."

"Third time I'll want a favor." Gorō's gearbox laugh. "That's how a city works, boys. Show up twice, owe once."

We took the job.

---

"So who's the suit," I said, on the walk home, containers warm against my chest. "Who does the season get adjusted *for*?"

Riku walked a few steps before answering, which from him meant the answer had corners.

"Every town has a landlord," he said. "Not the Federation — the Federation's a weather system, it doesn't count coins in bars. Under it there's always one desk where this town's paper actually lands. Here, people look at the north hill when they don't say it. Old governor's mansion up in the pines — 'heritage office' now. You saw Machi. You can grumble about the war all night in that bar. The hill, you don't name."

"Why not?"

"Because grumbling about the war is patriotism, and grumbling about the hill is a *complaint*, and complaints get filed." He shrugged. "Landlords read their mail. That's all I know and all I want to."

"You've been in this town as long as I have. How do you already know things like that?"

"I don't know things. I know shapes." He hefted his broth container like it was evidence. "A bar owner who pays fast and grumbles slow. A tram man whose line got cut and whose mouth gets one word into the reason before he swallows it. A suit that walks *ahead* of its soldiers instead of behind them. You put the shapes next to each other and you get a town with a hand on its shoulder. Every town's got one. This one's just better dressed."

I looked north without meaning to. Above the roofline the pines were a black shoulder against the city glow, and somewhere in them, presumably, a desk, and on it, presumably, a ledger with Mama Machi's tin in it, adjusted for the season.

*The war has a kitchen,* I'd said. Apparently cities had kitchens too. Smaller stoves. Same smell.

I filed it — a thought, not a plan — and walked on.

---

The drunk found us two lanes from home.

Big man, construction jacket, the kind of drunk where the night has stopped going the way he wanted years ago and every stranger is the reason. My shoulder brushed his in the narrow lane, which was all the invitation the evening required. He spun, grabbed my collar, cocked his arm back with the slow ceremony of a man who wanted the moment to be bigger than it was.

I was tired. My feet were flat wrong, my hands were full of broth, and the apology queued up on my tongue the way it always did.

What came out instead was, "Go home. Please."

Quiet. Flat. Certain in a way I didn't feel.

The man blinked. The arm came down. He let go of my collar, smoothed it — actually smoothed it, like a valet — turned around, and walked off down the lane with the peaceable gait of a man who had somewhere to be. A door two streets away opened and closed. Gone.

Riku looked at the space where a fight had been. Then at me. Then he made a sound of profound disgust.

"Manners that *work*," he said. "Do you know how many years I spent learning to duck? And you say *please* at a man and he remembers he has a house."

"He was tired," I said. "It's late. People want to be talked out of it."

"Nobody wants to be talked out of it that much." But he was already walking, already done with it, filing it where he filed the rest of me — under *soft boy, works somehow, don't take it apart*. I laughed and caught up, and I did not think about the flat certain sound my own voice had made, the same way you don't think about a stair that gives half a centimeter under your foot. You just keep climbing. It's probably nothing. The house is old.

---

The room over the bleach smelled like broth and gun oil, which was somehow, by then, the smell of things going well.

Magazine out. Chamber clear. Feet in the morning; the depot next week; a festival's worth of lanterns waiting in a storeroom for a city that intended, against all municipal evidence, to be joyful this summer. On the wall by the window Riku had tacked Gorō's hand-drawn map of the tram lines, corrected twice in pencil where forty years of memory had argued with itself.

"Thursday again in seven days," Riku said from his bedroll, eyes already shut. "Try not to adopt anyone before then."

"No promises."

The city hummed its night hum. Somewhere north the pines held their dark shoulder over everything, and I let them. Not tonight's problem. Tonight had been paid for in broth and a bad chorus, and next Thursday was already saving us seats.
