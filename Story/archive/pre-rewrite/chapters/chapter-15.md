# Chapter 15 — Shed Twelve

Shed twelve still smelled like the second we had missed.

Morning made the warehouse block ordinary again — forklifts that had decided to exist, a vendor selling canned coffee from a cooler as if diesel were a seasoning, gray-green at the far chain-link pretending to count boxes. Riku and I stood where Kaito's jacket had been and where my shout had gone to die, and the gravel under my new boots had nothing left of him but the idea of a gap.

"Poet-staring," Riku said.

"I'm mapping."

"You're bleeding on a map that already moved." He nodded at my lip. "We walk the gap from the other side. Quiet. If paper teeth are nesting there, we leave. If your friend left trash, we read it. Then we eat something that isn't adrenaline."

"Overlapping itinerary," I said.

"Don't brand that either."

We went around. The long way. Past numbered doors, past a loading bay where the broken lamp had given up flickering and simply died. Joy showed up sideways: Riku stole two coffees when the vendor looked at a clipboard, tossed me one, and said, "Payment for not getting us shot yesterday," as if that were a real invoice category. The coffee was terrible. I laughed into it anyway, and he looked briefly offended that terrible coffee could win.

---

The far side of the gap was a service lane pressed against the back of an old municipal building — shuttered clinic on the ground floor, stairs taped off, a faded sign about emergency dental that felt like a threat. Someone had cut the chain-link from this side too. Recent. Wire ends still bright.

Riku went first. I followed because following had become my whole personality.

Under the stairs, in a nest of tarps and battery sleeves and a blanket that had given up, we found Mira's kind of trash: neat even when discarded. A food wrapper folded into thirds like it had been graded. Two water bottles, caps on, labels peeled. And in a rusted can, a curl of burned paper — pages, plural, gone all the way to ash except one corner that had survived out of spite. Blank. Of course it was blank.

"She burns what matters," Riku said, turning the can once with his boot and setting it down exactly where it had been. "Neat girl. Neat fire."

"So there's nothing."

"There's confirmation. They slept here, they left before your yell finished echoing, and she doesn't leave paper behind for anyone to find." He straightened, already done with the nest. "That's not nothing. It's just nothing you can chase."

My pocket held Mira's one surviving scrap from Ishibashi — *cleaner. still say the target.* — and I hated how much I'd been counting on a second page. A schedule. An arrow. Anything with a direction printed on it. The nest under the stairs had already told me the only thing it was ever going to say: *faster than you. tidier than you. gone.*

---

A can rolled in the lane behind us.

We both turned. Not gray-green — a kid, maybe fourteen, Haiga dust on his sneakers, eyes too wide. He had seen us under the stairs. He had also, from the way his mouth worked, seen something earlier he was still trying to unsee.

"The tall one," he whispered, because the lane demanded whispers. "Last night. After you yelled. He came back through here with the girl."

"And," Riku said, mild, the way you talk to a cat you don't want to bolt.

"There's a watchman on the clinic gate at night. Old contract guy, sits with a radio and a heater. The tall one walked up to him — didn't grab him, didn't show a knife, nothing — he just looked at him and said, *Open the gate and go home.*" The kid swallowed. "Cleaner than a person talks. Like reading it off something. And the old man *did*. Opened it, smiled like it was his own idea, walked off and left the heater running. Middle of his shift."

The coffee went cold in my hand. An old man on a night shift, and Kaito had reached into his evening and switched it off like a light — no threat, no bruise, nothing the man would even file a complaint about. He'd walked home *smiling*.

"Then the girl said something I couldn't hear," the kid went on, "and he looked at me. I was behind the crates. He told me to *forget the alley.*" His jaw worked. "I didn't. I couldn't. I ran. My head hurt after. It still hurts."

Literal leftover. Learning fast — clean enough to empty a watchman's shift, not clean enough to wipe a kid's night. Not a god. A boy still leaving seams.

"Which way after," Riku said.

The kid shook his head. "Dark. I stopped watching. Watching was what almost got me the first time."

Riku gave him the second coffee without ceremony. "Smart. You didn't see us either. Go be a person somewhere else."

The kid fled. I stood in the lane with a burned page and a borrowed evening that some old man would never quite get back, and the bruise under my ribs sang the same song it always sang: closer. Harder. Still late.

"He didn't touch him," I said. "The watchman. He didn't even raise his voice."

"That's the part that should scare you," Riku said. "Loud is a weapon. Quiet is a tool. Your friend is being taught the difference." He clicked his tongue once — the sound he made before deciding things — and started walking. "Come on. Feet."

---

We did not wander the wards knocking on rumors. That had been my whole method since Kuroishi, and the method had just been outrun by a neat girl with a lighter.

Instead: feet, in a different empty lot, farther from Plaza paper, closer to the pine hills' shadow. No stick this time — hands, gravel, the ugly repetition of not apologizing when grabbed. I failed. I got free once. I failed again. Riku called my stance "a coat rack in an earthquake" and I snorted blood-taste and coffee and something like hope, which was more dangerous than either.

Between rounds he made me stop, look around the lot, and name every way out of it out loud — alley on the left, gap in the fence, the konbini's back door — until I could recite them without looking. "A fight you can leave is a fight you won," he said. "Know your doors before someone picks one for you." Then: "Again," and he grabbed, and I moved my feet before my mouth this time, and for exactly one round it worked, and he almost smiled about it, which for Riku was a parade.

We ate onigiri on a curb in the shade of a shuttered tool shop, and I did the thing I do — spread the whole problem out in the air between us like laundry.

"Every town, I get there after," I said. "I collect what he broke, I ask the people he emptied, and by the time I can say which way he went, the answer's two days stale. In a village that almost works. Here—" I waved the onigiri at Takamatsu generally, at the trams and the arcades and the hundred thousand evenings a boy could disappear into. "Here it's like tracking one wave through a harbor."

"Mm." Riku chewed. He let me hang there long enough to be annoying. "So stop tracking."

"That's not—"

"I mean it. You've been playing the dog. Sniff, run, arrive, sniff. The dog loses in a city, soft boy. City's too loud for noses." He balled his wrapper and banked it off a drainpipe into a crate, which he pretended not to be pleased about. "They're still here. She burned her paper and moved her nest, but nobody torches a good sleep spot to leave town — you torch it because you're staying and you don't want it read. Whatever they came to this city to do, it isn't done."

"You don't know that."

"I know jobs. That—" he nodded back toward the warehouse block, the lane, the neat ashes "—is housekeeping. You don't housekeep on your way out the door."

I turned that over. It had the ugly shape of true things. "So if I can't chase them—"

"You stand still where they'll have to move." He stood, brushed rice off his knees, and looked out at the city the way he looked at a lot before a lesson — exits first, then everything else. "We're in the same town as your friend and we keep almost dying of hurry. Wrong tool. So: we stop. We work — I'm nearly out of yen and this city doesn't feed spectators. We train. We learn this place until it's ours — which streets talk, which bars feed you, which wards the uniforms own after dark. A city tells you everything eventually if you live in it instead of ransacking it."

"And when something moves—"

"We're already standing where it moves. Fed. Rested. Able to make a fist." He shrugged like it was obvious, like it hadn't just rearranged my entire year. "Or we keep sprinting at echoes until FDC nets you out of sheer pity. Your call, poet."

---

Evening brought the warehouse block back under copper light. We risked the edge of shed twelve once more because I could not help myself and Riku could not stand my pacing. Empty. The gap chain-link had been re-wired, ugly and official. Paper had found our near-miss and put a stamp on it.

On a wall beside the gap, chalk — new, low, where a tall boy might write without thinking of witnesses: a single crooked character and then a smear, as if a calm hand had wiped it. Not a message for me. Not not one either. My throat did the stupid thing again.

"Don't yell," Riku said, mild as weather. "Yelling is how alleys get famous."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were composing it." He bumped my shoulder, not gentle, almost kind. "Come on. Bleach room. We're broke enough to invent work before the city invents us."

We walked back through the evening crowds — shutters rattling down, a tram sighing at its stop, the J-Alert chiming its usual lie that everything was managed. Somewhere in those hundred thousand evenings, Kaito was in a nest I would not find, being taught to speak more and more cleanly into other people's lives, and the trail I had followed across three towns had finally done what trails do in cities.

It had gone quiet.

The strange thing was that it didn't feel like losing. It felt like putting down something I'd been carrying wrong. Tomorrow there would be work, because we were broke. There would be feet, because Tuesdays keep coming. There would be this whole loud, bright, frightened city that Kaito and Mira were living in too — eating somewhere, hiding somewhere, waiting on something — and I was done arriving at places after they'd finished being places.

Riku glanced up once at the pine-black hill line, then at me.

"Try to keep your feet," he said. "The city starts charging rent tomorrow."

I kept them. Sore. Moving. Not after anyone, for once — just home, or the room shaped like one, in the same town as my best friend, both of us learning the same city from opposite ends of the dark.
