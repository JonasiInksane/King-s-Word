# Chapter 30 — Volunteers

Yano chose midmorning for the lesson, and the stamp was already talking before we got close enough to pretend we hadn't heard it.

The medical corridor had the particular cruelty of inland fluorescents — bright without kindness, hot enough to make the mesh smell like warm metal and older wire. We were on crate detail: boxes, tension, the polite fiction that supply rotation kept the wrong things out instead of the right things in. Close enough to processing to hear the stamp. Not close enough to stop it.

Sweat found the small of my back and stayed. A camera dome drew a bored circle over Habu's junction and decided the columns weren't lunch.

They called A-line in groups of five. "Health assessment," smiles first, white sleeves rolled, the cheerful woman from intake humming something that belonged in a school hallway and had no business in a building that zeroed children.

A boy came out without his armband.

Cut neat. Folded. Placed in a tray like a receipt. His wrist looked naked in a way that made the corridor feel colder. He walked back toward A with the empty carefulness of someone who'd been told he was still useful and was trying to believe the stamp.

Another didn't come out.

The processing door stayed shut long enough for the corridor to invent a silence. Then Yano appeared, cheerful, and told Habu the adjustment had been clean. Habu thanked her for her efficiency — lobby-warm, boiled-rice manners, the smile of a man who lightened weight for a living. The PA did not comment. Elevator shafts kept their schedule. Somewhere a gray-green checked a tablet like weather.

Riku's hands whitened on a crate until the wood complained.

I didn't look at him full-on. Looking would have been a speech. Speeches got stamped.

"Breathe," I said, quiet.

"I am," he said. "That's the problem."

"Look," someone said behind us — not a guard.

---

Mira stood in the service alley between shaft and mesh as if she had grown there overnight.

Blunt dark hair. Level gaze. No burn performance, just presence — the hunt-stillness I'd glimpsed in markets and mansion throats, scaled down to concrete and heat shimmer from the loading bay vents. She looked at Riku the way she looked at drowning boys — like tracks. The alley smelled like diesel and warm metal. A fly argued with nothing. Beyond the mesh, Habu's complete-count voice congratulated someone on existing in a column.

"You," she said.

Riku didn't reach for a weapon he didn't have. He angled his body so I was half behind the geometry without making it a speech. "You're the one who teaches mouths."

"I'm the one who tells the truth when soft people won't." Mira's eyes flicked to medical, to the tray of folded cloth catching midmorning like a bad joke. "You were a volunteer. I can smell the desertion on you — the folded band, the unfinished name. Look at what they do with the ones who stay in the books."

"I see it," Riku said. Low. Dangerous. The East Yards voice, the one that named exits twice. "Don't use it as a recruitment song."

"It isn't a song. It's a reason." She stepped closer, voice never rising — which was worse than shouting, because shouting would have belonged to Habu's theater. "The Federation eats children and calls it structure. We take a Voice to the kitchen and we break the stove. You hate being anyone's gun. Fine. Hate them more." A glance that brushed me and moved on, column without warmth. "He wants a world without warfare — this building is warfare in a cardigan. If you free these cells alone, Habu zeros them and files the weather. If you understand why we are here—"

"You're here for the east-wing girl," Riku cut in. "Don't dress an extract as mercy."

Mira almost smiled. Almost. The vent heat made the air between them look solid. "Mercy is a luxury the powerless can't afford. I learned that in a fire your Federation lit." A beat. Dust settled on the shaft casing like punctuation. "Tell Haruki the tall one still flinched at the glass. Tell him flinches don't open locks. Sentences do."

She was gone into the alley's heat shimmer before the nearest gray-green turned, leaving Riku with his scraped knuckles and a rage that had finally found a matching shape. I stood there tasting diesel and the word *stove* and the kitchen thought that refused to share a chair with it.

"I understand why she's doing it," he said to me, not looking. "I don't forgive the sermon. We free them because they're kids. Not because a believer needs a choir."

I nodded. Conversion was a stamp with better manners. We didn't take it.

The gray-green finished his turn of the alley and saw only crate tension and two specials pretending to be useful. Habu's voice floated across the junction, congratulating efficiency. Yano hummed. The tray of folded cloth sat in the medical light like a punchline nobody had asked for.

We went back to the crates because unfinished detail was the kind of incompleteness Habu noticed. Mesh sang under Riku's grip. Dust stuck to sweat. The boy without an armband stood in A-line and tried not to look at his own wrist.

"You okay?" I asked Riku, which was a stupid question wearing soft shoes.

"No," he said. "Keep working."

---

Kanzaki arrived before second count like a returned appointment.

Silver watch. Exact hair. Gray-green without a crease wrong — the kind of uniform that made Habu's cardigan look like a costume rented for kindness. He stood in the intake lobby and made the building rearrange around him without raising his voice. Habu's manners bent. Yano's cheer dimmed half a degree, which for Yano was weather. The volunteers stood straighter, as if posture could become innocence and innocence could become a status line that said *still breathing*.

I watched from the crate line with a box still in my hands and the alley sermon still in my ears. Kanzaki's eyes found Block C, found me, found Riku, and kept us with the patience of a man who had already written the ending on a rooftop and was only waiting for the stamp to dry.

"Asset confirmation," Kanzaki said, soft. "The loud anomaly left Takamatsu. The soft one did not. Keep both breathing. The building's other inventory is secondary."

Habu inclined his head. "Of course. We prefer boring forms."

"Boring forms are a luxury," Kanzaki said, and checked his watch as if Quiet Field were behind schedule and the fluorescents owed him minutes.

A breeze from the loading bay moved the dust and failed to move him. Somewhere in A a kid coughed and was told, gently, to save it for after count.

I thought of a rooftop. Of *cover your ears*. Of Ryn's unfinished *okay*. Of a soldier holding her over the lip as leverage — wrist locked, thirty meters of birthday light under her sneakers — while Kaito screamed at the grabber and the hand opened on grammar, not mercy. My stomach did the old arithmetic and came up bankrupt. Secondary. The cells. The kids with naked wrists and the ones who didn't come out. Secondary — and still standing in rows under lights that didn't care what column you belonged to.

Riku's tongue clicked once, almost too quiet to count as opinion. "He's here for us. The building is furniture."

"Furniture with kids in it," I said.

"Then we move the furniture before he notices it can walk."

---

Near the intake lobby, during the shuffle back toward Block C, I saw a man who did not belong to Habu's lobby story.

Too-warm coat for the air-conditioning. Face designed to be forgotten — the kind of features that slid off attention like soap under water. Stillness that wasn't Mira's hunt-stillness — older, evaluative, the listening of someone at a concert he'd already paid for. He stood where the lobby glass threw a shadow, not quite inspector, not quite visitor, and watched the corridor count the way the plum man had watched a bar: patient, expensive, without a greeting.

I blinked. The face slid. Wet soap.

Two darker shapes waited further back near the parking ramp mouth — coated, wordless, the kind of company that didn't need introductions because introductions left handles. No wave. No hello. No staff badge catching light. Just coats and a lobby and a listening that made my neck itch.

When I looked again, the coat was gone. Only glass. Only dust. Only Habu's complete-count voice congratulating us on existing in columns, and the late light catching Kanzaki's watch like a small hard sun.

I didn't tell Riku. There was nothing to grab — travelers don't have staff; friends say hello — and Shizuhara already had enough named knives. Unnamed coats got left on shelves until they grew handles. That was still the agreement. Mostly.

Mei caught my elbow at the wall board. Cooler frost still ghosting her sleeve. The hinge look. Her mouth shaped two syllables without sound: "Tonight." Then, quieter still, almost lost under Habu's warmth: "Gate cycle. Cells. Your jacket friends will want the same hour for the east wing. Don't wave."

Riku's tongue clicked once. Agreement without partnership theater.

I nodded once, small enough that Habu's glass couldn't invent a form for it. Mei's finger squeaked a correction onto the tablet — a volunteer number, a status, the kind of honesty that still lived in dust even when the folders lied. Her eyes said the rest: same hour, no partnership, no hymns.

Across the junction Yano stamped. Habu smiled. Kanzaki checked his watch again like the night had already been scheduled and we were only late to our own column. Somewhere in the east wing a boy who used to be loud was learning the vocabulary of trays — and deciding, I hoped and feared in the same breath, which words he would spend when the locks moved.

Tonight.

The building exhaled disinfectant and manners. Second count finished clean. The boy with the naked wrist made it into his cell. The one who hadn't come out of processing did not become a rumor; Habu's Quiet Field preferred completed lines to rumors.

We walked to Block C like boys who still believed morning would count them — and like boys who were already rehearsing how to stop that count without waving at anyone's Voice.

A badge beeped behind us at lobby glass. Soft. Patient. Awake.
