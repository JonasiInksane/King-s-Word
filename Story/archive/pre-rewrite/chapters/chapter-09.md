# Chapter 9 — Kill Yourself

Kaito's fingers shaped rice at Sugi's table and whispered when he thought no one could hear: *you in the gray — drop — step back — finish it.*

I watched from the doorway with a cup I wasn't drinking. The morning after the alley had the thin ordinary ugliness of a scab — necessary, something you try not to pick. Sugi fed us. The cat watched from the shelf. I barely spoke to him. He barely asked me to. We moved around each other like people sharing a train platform after a fight.

Mira did not come to the door. That should have reassured me. It didn't.

---

The sweep hit at midday.

Engines filled the lane. Boots. A loudspeaker voice polite as a knife, telling residents to remain indoors while the Federation Defense Command conducted a *security verification* of west-coast refugee concentrations. Gray-green flooded the shrine path, the dried-fish shop, the bend where the plum tree stood. Mira's people scattered. Sato's bandage flashed white between houses.

Jun did not stay inside.

I saw his aunt's hand on his good arm in the shrine doorway — saw her mouth shape *no* — and then he was in the lane anyway, limping on ribs that hadn't finished healing, Haiga dust ghosting the seams of his shoes, shouting about papers, about his sister, about the satchel a soldier had torn off an old net-hauler near the plum tree. Fifteen or sixteen. Too alive for this arithmetic. The kind of boy who grins at umeboshi like it's a festival and then walks straight into a rifle because someone told him to kneel and he still believed the word *no* meant something.

"On the ground," the soldier said. Mint-and-boredom voice. "Last time."

Jun stayed on his feet. "I have papers—"

The rifle came up.

---

Kaito was already moving.

"Stop," he said.

Not a scream. Built — who, what, Mira's lessons stacked behind his teeth. Aimed at the soldier.

The soldier's shoulders hitched. For half a second the barrel dipped, like a man remembering a dream, and then it came back up, clean, and Jun was still standing, and the Word had only *almost*.

"Stop," Kaito said again, louder. "You — stop. Put it down. Stop aiming at him. *Stop.*"

I heard the pier in it. I heard the alley. I heard a boy trying to finish a sentence the world would not accept half-made. The soldier blinked hard, shook his head once like he'd walked through a cobweb, and stepped in on Jun instead of away — the wrong direction, or no direction the command had successfully owned.

"Stop it—!" Kaito's voice cracked. "Don't — don't touch him — you with the rifle, stop moving, stop—"

Jun tried to pull free. Disobedience, the soldier would call it later if anyone asked. A kid with broken ribs refusing the ground.

"Last chance," the soldier said, almost bored, and Kaito was saying *stop* like a prayer now, like a person drowning, the same word over and over because it was the only plank left in the water —

"Stop. Stop. Stop—*please*, stop, I'm telling you to *stop*—"

—and the Word would not hold. Partial flinches. A stumble. A jaw tightening. Not enough. Never enough. His nose had started to bleed and he didn't wipe it. He was past wiping it. He was past careful. He was a boy watching the crate corner happen again in slow motion with better grammar and the same ending already walking toward him.

"Kaito—" I started, from the wall, useless.

Jun looked at him. Just once. Not blame. Something worse — trust.

The soldier shot him.

---

I will not decorate that either.

Jun went down in the lane under the plum tree with his papers still half in his hand. The sound punched a hole in the day that nothing polite from a loudspeaker could fill. For one long second the only moving thing in Nagihama was Kaito's mouth trying to make a word that had already failed.

Then the sound that came out of him was not a command.

It was a tear in the air.

"**No—!**" Raw. Breaking. The old volume back as pure grief. "**Nooooo — not again!!**"

Ryn on the roof — not falling, not rescued, *held*. A soldier's grip on her wrist over the edge, leverage while the gray-green spread across the concrete like a net. Kaito's *let her go* aimed at the grabber. The hand opening evenly, fingers uncurled, nobody home in the soldier's face. Then the crate. Sato's arm. And now Jun on the stones because *stop* would not stop, because careful was a joke, because the world had taken another person while he was still learning the shape of a sentence.

I saw the shift happen in his face.

Not hunger first. Not Mira's precision. Something colder and simpler: the end of asking the world politely. The coal going white. Every unfinished clause and every almost and every *if I'm careful* burning off at once, leaving only a single clean imperative aimed at the man who had just made the lane into a grave.

"**Kill yourself.**"

---

There was no misfire.

No second clause left lying around. No wrong body freezing. No ricochet.

The soldier's face emptied the way the hand on the roof had emptied — nobody home, grammar overriding will — and then his body did exactly what it had been told. When it was finished, the rifle was on the ground, and Jun was still dead, and a man who had been breathing mint and boredom was not, and none of that brought the grin back to the boy with the umeboshi.

Kaito's nose poured blood. His voice, on the next breath, was ruined — thinner than the week after the roof, scraped raw, as if the sentence had charged him rent in his throat. He stared at what his mouth had made. Then at Jun. Then at me.

I was still against the plum tree. Alive. Looking at him.

The friendship as we had known it — Rail Loop benches, melon soda, even the cracked version that had shared a roof and a ruined cat — ended in that look. Not because he had failed. Because he had succeeded too late, and too completely, and I had watched him become the kind of person who could finish a man after the world finished a child.

"What did you do," I said. Or I didn't. Maybe I only thought it. The lane was noise and then the silence that forms around two bodies before anyone has decided what kind of story to tell.

---

Mira was there before the second wave of boots understood what they were seeing.

She did not scream. She did not run from him. She put herself at Kaito's shoulder — ownership, not comfort — and said, low and fast: "You couldn't stop him with *stop*. You ended the one who did it. That's the cost and the proof. They will not let you stay in this town after a thing like this. Come with me. Now. Inland. The lists. People who can hide a voice."

Kaito's ruined voice: "Haru—"

I shook my head. *Don't speak. Don't say my name. Don't ask me to thank you. Don't ask me to go.*

Sugi stood in her doorway with both hands over her mouth. Her eyes found Kaito, then Jun, then me. She was a war widow. She had seen bodies. She had not seen this math. Something in her face closed on him the way a shutter closes — not hatred, not yet, but the end of the kitchen as a safe equation.

Soldiers were shouting. Someone had a radio up.

Mira's hand closed on Kaito's sleeve. "If you stay, they take you. If they take you, they use you or they bury you. Choose."

He looked at me one more time. Blood on his mouth. The boy who had knocked his shoulder into mine on the sea wall and the boy who had just finished a man after screaming *not again* occupying the same skin.

"I'm sorry," he said. To me. To Jun. To Ryn. The words were soft. They were not a command. They did nothing at all.

Then he went with Mira.

Not running at first — walking, fast, into the gap between the fish shop and the shrine path where her people knew the holes in the net. I took one step after him. Sugi's hand found my wrist, hard, the way she had held me on the flats at low tide, and she did not let go.

"No," she said. The only imperative I obeyed that day without flinching.

---

They were gone before the loudspeaker found a new polite sentence.

I sat on Sugi's kitchen floor with my back against the cabinet and the taste of iron in my mouth and the cat staring from the shelf with its one milky eye, and I understood the arithmetic at last, too late, the way I understand everything:

He had tried to stop it.

He had failed until failure broke him into something that could kill.

Jun was dead either way.

And I was still alive to know that we were no longer — not that afternoon, not that night, not in any ordinary way I still had words for — on the same side of the water.
