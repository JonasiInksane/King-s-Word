# Chapter 28 — Inventory

The stamp kissed the form with a wet little sound, and I understood the building before anyone meant to teach us.

Second day. Vocational detail: Block C to medical processing for "supply rotation," which turned out to mean carrying boxes past a room you weren't supposed to understand. The small cheerful woman from intake was there in white, sleeves rolled, humming something that belonged in a school hallway before a sports day. She had a stamp and a smile that arrived before her eyes did. Disinfectant rode the air like a second opinion. Fluorescent light made every tray look like evidence.

"Yano," she said, bright as a nurse on picture day. "If you're useful, you stay useful. If you're not—" the stamp kissed a form again "—we adjust. Don't look so tragic. Adjustment is just math that learned manners."

Through the observation glass behind her I saw the glimpse they hadn't meant to leave open: a volunteer on a table, armband already cut and folded into a tray, a machine humming with the patience of something that had done this before. A child's foot twitched once and stopped. Yano's humming didn't miss a note. Status line on a tablet beside the glass, soft green: *inventory loss — completed.* Date from last week. No grave. No letter home. Just a line that had decided a person was arithmetic.

Riku went still in the way that meant machinery under water.

Yano noticed and liked it. "You're irregular, not A/B. Different column. Don't worry — specials are expensive. We don't zero expensive things without paperwork from upstairs." She winked. "Usually. Upstairs has moods."

I looked away from the glass and still saw it. Armband. Folded cloth. Completed. The stamp's wet sound lived in my ears like a second heartbeat I hadn't asked for.

Habu appeared in the doorway like kindness arriving on schedule. "Doctor, please don't frighten the late inventory. They're still learning our vocabulary." He dimmed the glass with a casual swipe of a wall switch, as gently as if he were removing a sharp knife from a child. "Youth who serve irregularly sometimes see things the Federation prefers to keep uncounted. Quotas. Sweeps. Flags that say *inconclusive* after a boy is already dead in a ditch." Soft smile. Soft cardigan. "Those youths become… heavy. Shizuhara lightens them. Relocates the weight. Vocational closure. You understand."

I understood the way you understand a fall while you're still in the air.

"They're kids," I said, and hated how small it sounded in a room that smelled like alcohol wipes and warm plastic.

"They're *proof*," Habu corrected, almost fond. "Proof has a storage problem. We are the solution with a lobby view. The public prefers its volunteers heroic and absent. We arrange the absence."

Through the half-open inner door I saw a tray: folded armbands, neat as laundry. Yano hummed and stamped another form. Somewhere a volunteer coughed and was told, brightly, to wait his turn. A gray-green in the hall checked his watch like the morning owed him boredom.

Riku laughed once — no humor, just air leaving a body that had been holding a different joke for years. "Volunteer," he said. "Officially it means they don't owe you anything. Cheapest meat. Towns sew the armbands. Mothers compete. And when the meat learns too much about the kitchen—" he looked at Habu, at Yano, at the stamp "—you bring it here and call the hunger *adjustment*."

Habu's face didn't change. "You speak like someone who wore a band."

"I speak like someone who left one folded on a bunk," Riku said. "They don't chase volunteers. You were never here. Turns out *never here* has a forwarding address with fluorescents."

A junior cleared his throat in the hall. Habu made a note. Yano stamped. We were walked out with the supplies we hadn't been allowed to count, past A-line kids scrubbing floors who didn't know their folders were already practicing the word *completed*.

The corridor outside felt like an accusation. Mesh flashed. The cough kid from B sat against a wall and tried to make his breathing quiet enough not to be interesting. A-line scrubbed. B-line hauled. Habu waved from behind glass as if he'd just explained the weather and the weather had agreed to be polite.

Riku walked three steps ahead of me, jaw set, scraped nothing yet — that would come in the service stair — and I followed with boxes we hadn't been allowed to count and a status line still burning behind my eyes: *inventory loss — completed.*

---

In the service core, where the elevator shafts muttered loud enough to hide a voice and the stairwell's draft covered the rest, Riku put his fist into the concrete once and then stood there breathing like he'd run a ward with no exits left.

Knuckles scraped. Blood bright and small. The shafts kept their tired note like they hadn't noticed a boy inventing weather against a wall.

"I finished my shift," he said. Not to me. To the years. "In Takamatsu. Saw the armbands on a sweep and finished my shift like a good deserted boy. Curriculum. Nobody gets to use you." His eyes found mine, bright and wrong. "This is what they do with the ones who don't desert early enough. This is the ditch with a clipboard and a nurse who hums."

I didn't offer comfort. Comfort would have been a lie with soft edges, and he'd earned better than my reflexes. I stood there and let the kitchen thought bruise until it changed shape.

If I could reshape the whole world — I had roared it at Minato, grinning, too big for the room — and the world had a building like this in it, then reshaping wasn't a speech. It was a door. Keys. Bodies. Risk. The whom-question sat up in my chest and asked uglier: *reshaped by whom, if you're too weak to open a cell?* Kuroda's laugh from another night tried to crawl up the ridge road and I shoved it down. Not now. Now was armbands in a tray.

"Haruki," Riku said.

"Yeah."

"If you say *I'm sorry* I'm going to put you in the ditch myself."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were thinking it loud enough." He flexed the scraped hand once, practical. "Feet. Plan. Not apology. Apology is Habu's second language."

Dust lifted off the stair wall. Somewhere a volunteer laughed and the laugh died mid-note, as if the corridor had reminded it of the stamp. I watched Riku's knuckles darken and thought about kitchens that stirred wars, kitchens that stirred buildings, and the stupid full-size promise I'd made in a bar while people laughed like the world was still a joke with good timing.

The bruise in my chest wasn't romance. It was the kitchen ideal learning it had walls. Walls with stamps. Walls with trays of folded bands. Walls that called absence a lobby view.

"We free them," I said, quiet enough for concrete.

Riku didn't smile. "We plan first. Freeing without a plan is how you become a completed line with better handwriting."

---

Mei found us without being invited, which seemed to be her religion. She took one look at Riku's scraped knuckles and snorted like she'd been waiting for the scrape.

"Took you long enough," she said. "Most specials need a week to stop believing in vocational hope. You two did it in two days. Congrats. The building grades on a curve." She crouched, elbows on knees, scarred knuckles forward. Fluorescent light made a small glare on her boots like punctuation. "A and B aren't waiting for graduation. They're waiting for a quiet night and a form. East wing hears the trucks. I've counted the trucks. Last month enough left that the board has to lie, and Habu likes me for correcting the lie because corrected lies look like order."

"How many," Riku said.

"Enough." Mei's smile went sharp. "You want numbers for a speech, invent them. You want a door, listen. You leave alone, the parking ramp eats you — I've watched that movie. You want to do something that isn't manners, you free the cells on a gate cycle and you pray the outer locks forget their job at the same time." She tipped her chin at me. "You collect guilt. Collect a plan instead. I know the east wing keys' habits. You know feet. Tall-and-furious knows how not to die on a Tuesday. That's a crew whether you like the word."

"We're not—" I began.

"Partners," Riku said flatly, and then, because the word had already become unnecessary in another city, he knocked his fist lightly against my shoulder. "Fine. We look. We don't storm on a speech. Speeches get stamped. Plans get people out or they don't — no half measures. Halfway is how kids die in lanes while somebody's still saying *stop*."

Mei stood. Dust on her knees like a choice. "Good. I've heard enough halfway for one war." Her eyes held mine a beat longer than logistics required — not romance, not yet, just the unpaid glance of someone deciding whether you were weight or hinge. "Tomorrow after second count. Water cooler. Bring a brain that isn't apologizing. Leave the apology in the bunk where it can keep the mattress company."

"Keys," I said.

"Habits of keys," Mei corrected. "Keys without habits are just metal. Metal gets counted." She tipped her chin toward the service core's painted timer strip, visible past the stair rail like a joke someone had laminated. "Gate cycle. Same hour the outer locks get bored. Overlap is how you survive a building that calls children columns."

She left toward the east wing's closed sign as if closed were a rumor she'd already corrected on a tablet. A junior shouted about crates somewhere. The elevator shafts muttered. The corridor went on looking innocent.

---

Riku watched the volunteer lines for a long time through the mesh. Kids hauling. Kids scrubbing. Kids still young enough to believe a fluorescent corridor was a kind of future. The cough kid from B sat against a wall and tried to make his breathing quiet enough not to be interesting to Yano. Kenji from Block C walked past with a crate and didn't meet our eyes, which was its own kind of map.

"If we open it," Riku said, "and they die anyway, I'm going to need someone to tell me the curriculum still matters."

"I'll tell you," I said, and heard how thin it was against a stamp's wet sound.

He clicked his tongue once. "Then we don't open it halfway. We open it, or we don't touch it. And if your jacket friends from the roof show up with sentences—" he didn't look at me "—we use the hole. We don't join the choir."

I nodded. Half measures had a body count I already knew by heart — lanes, lips, rooftops. On the roof a soldier had held Ryn over the edge as leverage, wrist locked, city bowl under her sneakers, and Kaito had screamed at the grabber thinking he was naming rescue. The hand had opened. Leverage, not rescue. The building would only add tablets to that arithmetic.

Across the junction, Habu waved at a complete count with lobby warmth. Yano's white coat flashed behind medical glass. Somewhere on a radio that wasn't for us, a patient voice might already be asking for directions to Quiet Field, watch gleaming, schedule intact. The loading bay's timer strip caught late light and pretended to be only paint. East wing stayed closed. A and B stood for evening manners. Specials filed toward Block C with blistered hands and a plan that hadn't learned to apologize yet.

Mei was already at the wall board again, correcting something small, staying useful. Useful lasted. Righteous rode trucks.

Tomorrow: water cooler. Plan. Brain without apology.

I didn't stack a litany. I held one bruise where my ideal had been and one plan where an apology used to live — keys in Mei's tablet habits, feet in Riku's religion, a door that would either open clean or not at all.

And for the first time since the pad, I wanted a door more than I wanted forgiveness.

The PA recited second count complete. A badge beeped at medical. Somewhere behind observation glass a tray of folded armbands waited for the next child who had learned too much about kitchens.
