# Chapter 7 — The Pier

The Federation trucks came on a Thursday.

I only know because Sugi marks the calendar in pencil the way widows mark weather, and because Thursdays were when the dried-fish shop restocked and the whole lane smelled like salt and smoke and something almost like ordinary life.

Three trucks. Gray-green. Not a full company — a detachment, the kind that shows up to "assess refugee flow" and leaves with more people than they arrived with. They blocked the west pier with the casual authority of men who had never once asked permission to stand somewhere. A lieutenant who was not Kanzaki — younger, louder, sweat in the wrong places — read from a clipboard while soldiers peeled refugees out of the shrine grounds by the arm.

Nagihama held its breath harder.

Kaito was already on the pier when I got there. Of course he was. Rope-burned hands, jacket on, the coal in him bright enough that I could feel it from the steps. Mira stood further back under the eaves of the net shed, not intervening, watching him the way you watch a fuse you've already decided not to stamp out.

I saw the foreboding before he did. Or before he would admit he did.

---

It happened at the checkpoint rope. A soldier put his hand on an old man's shoulder — not cruel yet, just herding — and Kaito, three meters off, said, "Hey. Enough," not even loud, the way he'd said *easy* to the fishmonger two days before. The soldier's grip loosened. Just for a second. The old man slipped past like a fish through a tear in a net. The soldier looked at his own empty hand, then at Kaito, then shrugged it off as sun and overtime.

Kaito didn't shrug it off. I watched him go still the same way he had with the rope coil. His fingers found his throat. His eyes did a small ugly arithmetic I recognized, because it was my arithmetic, stolen and worn wrong on his face.

*Luck,* I begged him silently. *Call it luck again.*

He didn't look at me. He looked at the trucks.

---

The one they grabbed was a boy I knew by sight — not by name, which is its own small shame. Fifteen, maybe sixteen. Haiga dust in the seams of his shoes. He'd been hauling crates with us three mornings running and grinning at Sugi's umeboshi like it was a festival. Two soldiers had him by the elbows, walking him toward the middle truck, and he was not grinning now. He was saying *I have papers, I have papers* to men who had not asked for papers.

"That's Hanae's brother," someone whispered behind me — a widow, one of the low-tide singers. "They took the sister last week. He's all she's got left in the west."

Kaito moved.

I caught his sleeve. "Don't."

He looked at me. Not the cold room. Worse — the hot thing under it, the boy who stepped between me and third-years without thinking. "I'm not going to stand here and watch them put him in a truck, Haru."

"Then we find Sugi. We find someone with a stamp. We don't—"

He pulled free. Not hard. Just final. "Stay back."

I did not stay back. I am cursed with following him into the arithmetic even when I can already see the sum.

---

"Let him go," Kaito said.

He said it to the nearer soldier, ten steps away, voice pitched to carry without shouting — and I heard the roof in it, I heard the ledge, I heard *LET HER GO* like a bruise under the words, and I saw him hear it too, saw the flicker of fear and then the worse flicker of *hope*, the first real *maybe* landing in him like a coal he'd been carrying and had finally looked at.

The soldier's left hand opened.

The right one did not.

Hanae's brother jerked half-free, stumbled, and the soldier who still had him yanked sideways to compensate — into the pier rail, into the stack of wet crates the morning boats had left, into gravity. The boy's temple hit the corner of a crate with a sound I will not decorate. He went down. The opened-hand soldier stared at his fingers the way the man on the roof had stared, blank for one second too long, and then the yelling started.

"Stop!" Kaito's voice cracked up into volume — the old volume, raw. "Stop — don't touch him — get *back*—"

*Stop* is a bad word. I know that now. At the time I only knew that three people froze who should not have frozen: a medic half-kneeling, a woman reaching to cushion the boy's head, and for a sick heartbeat Hanae's brother himself, crumpled wrong against the wood, eyes open, body refusing the curl that might have protected him while a soldier's boot, still moving because *get back* had hit the wrong bodies in the wrong order, caught his ribs.

Something broke. Soft. Expensive.

The pier became noise. Mira was suddenly there, between Kurosawa and the lieutenant's raised hand, talking fast and flat — wrong pier, wrong list, clerical error, this one's stamped, she'll take responsibility — the language of people who survive by lying better than soldiers shout. Sugi appeared like weather. Blood on the crate corner. Hanae's brother making a sound into the wood that was not words.

Kaito stood in the middle of it with a nosebleed starting, bright against his skin, and the seam in his voice torn wide open when he tried to say the boy's name and didn't know it.

He looked at his hands.

He looked at me.

I have seen my best friend afraid of third-years and of uncles and of empty apartments. I had never seen him afraid of his own mouth.

---

They let the boy go, in the end. Not kindness. Mira's paperwork and Sugi's widow-voice and the lieutenant's desire not to file a cracked refugee on a pier in front of a crowd. They loaded a different set of names into the trucks and left a silence behind them that smelled like diesel and iron.

Hanae's brother was carried to the shrine. Alive. Hurt. The kind of hurt that would have been a shove and a bruise if a voice had not entered the math.

I found Kaito behind the net shed. Mira was already with him. Of course she was. She held out a cloth for the blood under his nose like she'd been expecting it.

"You almost had it," she said. Quiet. No triumph. Something worse — precision. "The first one listened. The second one didn't get the whole sentence. Next time you say exactly what you mean. Who. What. No spare words for the air to keep."

"Next time," I said, and I did not recognize my own voice, "there isn't a next time. People got—"

"People were already getting hurt," Mira cut in, still mild. "He tried to stop it. That's more than anyone else on that pier did." She glanced at me. Filed. Set aside. "You saw it too. Don't pretend you didn't."

Kaito wiped his face. Missed half the blood. "Haru." His eyes were too bright. "On the roof. The hand. And the monger. And just now — his hand opened. I said *let him go* and it *opened*, and then everything else went wrong because I didn't — because I said *stop* like an idiot and it landed on the wrong—" He stopped. Pressed the heel of his hand into his forehead. "Tell me I'm inventing this. Tell me it's luck. Tell me I'm crazy."

I opened my mouth.

It was all right there. Kanzaki's radio. *It's the Word.* The birthmark. The too-even fingers. The soldier who'd held Ryn over the lip as leverage and opened his grip when Kaito shouted. I could have given him the name. I could have said *you have something and it kills when you're careless and you have to never speak an imperative again.*

What I said, because I am a coward who loves him, was: "I don't think you're crazy."

Not enough. Too much. His face changed around it — fear and relief and a terrible dawning hunger braided together.

"Then I can do it," he whispered. Not to Mira. To the blood on the cloth. To Ryn, somewhere. "Not right. Not yet. But I can. If I say it better. If I'm careful. I could've gotten him out clean. I *almost*—"

"Kaito." I grabbed his wrist. Harder than I meant to. "Listen to me. Whatever this is — if it's anything — it just put a boy into a crate corner. You don't get to practice on people."

He looked at my hand on his wrist. Then at Mira. Then east, always east.

"I'm not practicing," he said. Soft. The coal. "I'm learning how not to lose anyone else."

He pulled free. Not cruel. Final, the way doors are final.

Mira did not smile. She didn't need to. She fell into step with him toward the shrine to check on the boy, and I stood behind the net shed with the smell of iron in my mouth and watched my best friend walk toward the next sentence he would try to build, and I knew — the way I know every sum too late — that the *maybe* had already become a *how*.

On the sea wall above the pier, for half a second, I thought I saw a plain dark coat against the sky.

When I looked again there was only the bruise on the horizon, and the gulls, and the trucks already gone west.
