# Chapter 6 — Two Weeks

Two weeks is a long time when you're fifteen and the world has already ended once.

It is also no time at all. At the time it just felt like weather — gray mornings stacking on gray mornings, the salt working into everything, the eastern glow still there every dawn like a bruise the sky couldn't stop picking at. We slept in the back room of Sugi's house. We ate what she put in front of us. We hauled nets for the old men on the docks when they needed hands, because Nagihama does not have room for people who only sit and grieve, and because doing something with your body is the only way grief sometimes agrees to shut up for an hour.

The cat sat on the shelf above Kaito's futon, one milky eye staring at the ceiling. He never put it away. He never talked about it. It just watched us live the small stupid life that comes after a funeral with no body.

Kaito's voice came back. Not all the way — a seam you could hear if you knew where to listen — but enough that the widows stopped looking at him like he was a wounded animal. The quiet in him stayed. He filled the days with motion: harbor, errands, *helping someone.* He would leave after breakfast and come back smelling of diesel, and when Sugi asked where he'd been he would say, "Around," in a way that now meant *do not ask*. I counted the absences. Of course I did.

---

Near the end of the second week Mira came to the door.

Three soft raps. Sugi went still by the stove. Mira stood in the frame and looked past her to Kaito, who was tying his shoes, and said, "There's a crate at the west pier. Names inside. People who don't want to be found by the ones who send soldiers upstairs."

A job shaped like the vow on the wall — cut down to the size of an afternoon.

"I'll go," Kaito said.

Sugi's hand tightened on the ladle. "Boy," she said, edge under the mercy. "Don't let her take you somewhere you can't walk back from."

Mira said, mild, "I'm not taking him. He's walking."

I went with them as far as the bend. At the corner Kaito said, "Stay with Sugi. I'll be back before dark." He came back before dark, as promised — hands scraped, a new bruise — and those were the last ordinary days we were going to get, though neither of us knew it.

---

The stranger was there two evenings later.

I saw him from the shrine steps, where I'd gone because the house was too quiet and Sugi had started checking the eastern horizon the way the widows did. He stood at the edge of the sea wall in a plain dark coat too warm for the season, short unremarkable hair, a face that refused to stick. The same watching-still as the Rail Loop car on Ryn's birthday. Phone low at his side. Eyes on the pier.

Kaito and Mira were coming up from the west dock with a wooden crate between them. A dockworker ahead of them barked "Wait—" — sharp, automatic — and for a beat, wrong as a skipped frame, the people around him did not wait. They moved as if they'd heard a different word. Then the moment folded up and became ordinary, and I told myself I was tired.

The still man watched Kaito. Then he watched me, just long enough. He turned the phone screen toward the pier for a second — not filming, showing — and walked into the dark between the shrine and the fish shop, and was gone.

I met them at the bottom of the steps. "The man from the train," I said. "The coat. He was here."

Kaito glanced at the empty wall. "Saw who?"

Mira's eyes flicked once to the dark. Something like calculation moved through her face and was put away. "Then we're not as quiet as we thought," she said. She shifted the crate. "Get some sleep, both of you. Tomorrow we talk about next steps."

She left us at Sugi's gate. That mattered. She left us.

---

We didn't go inside right away.

Kaito stood in the lane with his hands in his jacket pockets and looked at the plum tree like it had asked him a question, and I said, "Walk with me," which was the bravest sentence I had managed in two weeks, and he did.

We ended up on the sea wall. Of course we did. The same stretch where we'd sat with the ruined cat the night of the tide. Low water again. The eastern bruise still glowing. Gulls arguing over nothing. The town held its breath the way Nagihama always did after dark, and for a while we just sat, two boys and a lot of unsaid things.

"She's asked me to go inland," Kaito said finally. Not looking at me. Looking east. "Tonight. There's someone a few towns over who keeps lists — real ones. Who was on which roof. Who gives the orders. Mira says if I'm serious about what I said after the tide, this is where it starts." He flexed his scraped hands. "I told her I needed an hour."

"An hour for what?"

"For you," he said, and the thin seam in his voice caught, and he hated that I heard it. "Don't make a thing of it."

I made a thing of it anyway. That is also the problem with me.

"I don't want you to go," I said. Plain. No softener. It felt like stepping off the wall. "I know that isn't fair. I know you made a vow. I know Mira makes sense to you in a way I haven't. But I don't want you to go, and I'm tired of pretending I'm fine with the way you keep walking away from the table before the tea's cold."

Kaito let out a breath that wanted to be a laugh and wasn't. "I'm not walking away from you."

"You are. You have been. Since the roof. Since I—" I stopped. The facts crowded up — the hand opening on his command, the radio, the mark, the silence where *it wasn't your fault* should have been — and what came out instead was the true shape underneath them. "Since I didn't say the right thing in the dark. Under the machines. You asked me why he opened his hand, and I said nothing, and you've been alone with that ever since. I know. I know I did that."

He was quiet for a long time. The sea worked at the stones.

"I thought you blamed me," he said. Soft. The boy under the quiet. "I thought that was why. You saw it. You were right there. And then you wouldn't look at me, and you wouldn't say it wasn't my fault, and I figured — yeah. Of course. Whose fault was it, if it wasn't mine. I said the words. She fell."

"I don't blame you." It came out too fast, too fierce, and I had to steady it. "I don't. I blame the men on the stairs. I blame the war. I blame myself for not getting to the edge faster. I have a whole stupid list. You are not on it." I swallowed. "I was afraid. Not of you. Of how clean it was. How his hand just — and I didn't have a name for it, and I still don't, and instead of staying with you in that I went quiet, and that was cruel, and I'm sorry."

Kaito's jaw worked. He wiped at his face with the heel of his hand like he could pretend it was salt spray. "You sound like her," he said. "Mira. The men on the stairs. The ones who send them."

"Maybe she's right about that part." The admission cost me. "Maybe she's wrong about the rest. I don't know yet. I only know that every time you leave with her, the house gets smaller, and I keep thinking about Ryn on the pavement that night telling us the plan for Saturday, and how she would hate this — us sitting here like the war already won."

He made a broken sound. "Don't."

"I have to. She's the only reason either of us is still trying to be people." I turned to face him fully. The nick in his eyebrow. The scraped knuckles. "If you go tonight, I'm not going to stop you. I can't. But I need you to hear me say this before you do, not after, not in a letter, not never: you are still my best friend. You are still the loudest person I have ever loved. The quiet in you scares me, and Mira scares me, and that man in the coat scares me, and none of that means I'm letting go of you. Even if you can't let go of — of her. Of the vow. Of any of it."

Kaito stared at the water. When he spoke, the volume was almost back — not warm, not yet, but *present*, aimed at me instead of at the east.

"I'm going," he said. "I have to. If there's a list with a name on it that belongs to the man who checked his watch on that roof — in my head it's always while she was falling, even if the second was earlier — I need to see it with my own eyes. I can't sit here hauling nets and pretending the bruise on the horizon is someone else's problem. Ryn would — she would have already gone." He finally looked at me. Eyes bright. Afraid under the quiet. "But I'm coming back. Before morning if I can. Before the day after if I can't. I'm not — Haru, I'm not leaving *you*. I'm leaving the version of me that only sits on this wall and hates himself. Can you live with that. For now."

I wanted to say no. I wanted to say *stay*. I wanted to put every withheld fact in his hands and make the world make sense.

I said, "For now."

He nodded once. Then — and this is the part I keep in a separate drawer from all the other parts — he leaned sideways and knocked his shoulder into mine, hard, the old way, the Rail Loop way, the way that used to mean *you're stuck with me*, and he left it there against my shoulder until we were both breathing like idiots.

"If that coat-man shows up again," he said into the space between us, "you tell Sugi. You don't go looking. You're bad at looking. You're good at — the other thing."

"Apologizing to rectangles."

"Yeah." A real almost-laugh. Small. Gone quick. "That."

We sat until his hour was almost used up. Then we walked back to the house together, shoulder to shoulder in the dark, and for a little while that was enough.

---

Mira was waiting at the corner of the lane, not at the door — she knew better than to make Sugi watch this part. She read Kaito's face before he opened his mouth. Something in her stillness shifted by a millimeter. Not surprise. Recalculation.

"Not tonight," Kaito said. The seam in his voice. "I'll go. Just — not tonight. There's things here I have to—" He glanced at me, quick, and didn't finish. "Tomorrow. Or the day after. I'm not walking away from this. I'm asking for time."

Mira studied him. Then me. Then him again. "Time is a luxury the people on those lists don't have," she said. Mild. No knife in it — which somehow made it worse. "But I can wait a little. You're no use to anyone if you split yourself in half before we start." She tipped her chin west. "When you're ready. I'll be at the pier."

She left without argument. That scared me more than if she'd fought.

Inside, Sugi took one look at both of us still in the doorway — jackets on, shoes dusty, neither of us gone — and the set of her shoulders loosened a fraction she would deny until the day she died. She fed us. She always fed us. The cat watched from the shelf with its one milky eye, and Kaito touched two fingers to its ruined head on the way to his futon, and I lay in the dark listening to him breathe on the other side of the room, present, *that*, and I told myself the heart-to-heart had held.

It had. For now.

---

The next afternoon, on the docks, something small happened that I filed and he pretended not to.

A boy — refugee, Haiga dust on his cuffs, maybe twelve — was underfoot of a fishmonger's cart. The monger, tired and loud, snapped "Move—" and the boy froze wrong, the way scared kids do. Kaito, passing with a coil of rope over his shoulder, said it without thinking, sharp, the old volume flickering for half a second: "Hey. Easy. Let him through."

The monger's hands opened on the cart handles. Just — opened. He stepped aside like he'd decided to be polite in a town that had forgotten how. Then he blinked, confused at himself, and scratched his neck, and went on shouting about prices as if nothing had happened.

Kaito stood very still with the rope. He touched his throat once, the way you touch a tooth that might be cracked. Looked at his own hands. Looked at the boy already gone between the crates.

"Luck," he muttered, when he caught me watching. Too quick. "He's just — tired. Everyone's tired."

"Yeah," I said.

I did not believe him. I don't think he believed himself. For the first time since Low Tide I was counting something that had not finished happening yet.

It was about what might happen next.

We went home to Sugi's table. We slept under the same roof. For one more night, that was still true in the only way that mattered.

I have learned not to trust that sentence the way I used to.
