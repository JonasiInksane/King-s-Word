# Chapter 1 — The Loud Quiet

The salaryman's pass was already falling when my hand closed on it.

Plastic, warm from his pocket, a hand's width above the Rail Loop tiles. I hadn't meant to move. I straightened, held it out, and said, "Sorry," which made no sense — I hadn't done anything wrong — and he blinked at me like I'd apologized for the weather. Heat was already coming up off the platform before the sun had cleared the buildings. The vending machine by the gates jingled its four notes for nobody. Neon over Sakaba-dōri still buzzed pink in daylight, useless; the karaoke tower with the dead 'K' still promised ARAOKE to the morning commute. My phone buzzed against my palm: the war app, two ugly tones, Haiga on fire again three prefectures east. I turned the screen face-down against my thigh like that would help.

"You apologized to a pass," said a voice behind me. "You apologized to an inanimate rectangle."

"I apologized to the *man*," I said, not turning around, because I'd know that voice at the bottom of a river.

"You said sorry before he could even be mad. You defused a fight that didn't exist." Kurosawa Kaito dropped onto the bench beside me with the boneless confidence of someone who had never once worried a seat wouldn't hold him — half a head taller even sitting, wet hair from a shower he'd clearly lost a fight with, the thin white nick through his left eyebrow catching platform light when he grinned. Yesterday's dark jacket over today's uniform. Knuckles scraped, as usual. "One day you're going to apologize to a guy who's actually swinging at you and he's going to be so confused he'll just leave."

"That's the plan," I said. "De-escalation. It's a life skill."

"It's a *personality disorder*, Haru." He said my name the way he said everything — too loud, a little too fond, like the volume was the point.

The train doors chimed. We got on just ahead of them. Two women across the car glanced over and then, oddly, softened, the way people always seemed to soften around Kaito right when you'd expect them to bristle. Dark eyes, hard-bright edge, and somehow you wanted to be annoyed at him and then you weren't. He had that. He'd always had that.

The train pushed off. Through the window the city unrolled itself — the river the color of weak tea, the elevated highway, the enormous municipal screen on the side of the Daigo Building that was supposed to show weather and increasingly showed the war instead. FRONT STABLE, it said this morning, in the specific font the government used for things that were not true. EASTERN WARDS: CONTAINED. A ribbon of smaller text underneath scrolled the names of the newly missing and I did what everyone in the car did, which was become very interested in my own knees.

"Ryn's off today," Kaito said, already changing the subject, which he did whenever the screen came up. "Grandma's stall. She's doing the pickle thing."

"She hates the pickle thing."

"She loves the pickle thing. She just hates that she loves it." He stretched, and his sleeve rode up, and I caught the edge of the bruise on his forearm — greenish, three days old, the shape of someone's grip. I didn't ask. Kaito collected bruises the way I collected apologies; it was how each of us tried to keep the people around us from getting hurt, and neither method worked, and we both knew it, and we never said so. "You bringing your mom's thing tonight?"

"Her *thing* is a homemade cake and you will show it respect."

"I will show it *reverence*," he said, and put his hand over his heart, and a third-year two seats down snorted into his phone, and just like that the whole car was a fraction warmer, and none of us could have told you why.

---

We came up the school hill late enough that the track already smelled baked and the vending machine that ate coins was already mid-theft. Kōgetsu Municipal High sat above the Rail Loop like it was trying to see over the city's shoulder to the east and had decided, wisely, not to look. The rooftop door still wore its chain and its padlock that had been broken so many times the school had given up replacing the lock and just replaced the chain — a polite fiction between us and the administration, like FRONT STABLE.

First period was Doi-sensei, who taught modern history and looked, every single morning, like a man who had been informed his deadline was yesterday. Soft midsection, thinning hair combed forward in a losing battle, wire glasses already askew. He shoved through the door clutching a stack of papers to his chest the way a smaller man might clutch a life preserver, ink on his fingers, tie loosened before the day had earned it.

"Sit down," he said to the room, to no one, to the general concept of teenagers. "Sit down, sit down, I have — please — I have handouts, and I have exactly enough, which means if any of you have multiplied since Friday we are in trouble."

Kaito was still on his feet in the aisle, arguing a point with a third-year who'd wandered in from the hall — something about a soccer match, the eternal war of the practice fields. The third-year had half a head and a year on him and was doing the chin-up thing older boys do when they want a room to watch. Kaito's grin had gone bright and hard. His hands were already talking.

"Hey," Kaito said, not loud, just absolute, the way he'd said everything on the train. "Back off. Go back to your class."

And the third-year did.

At the time it was nothing. The kid's weight shifted off his forward foot *before* his face caught up — shoulders dropping, chin losing its angle, body answering a beat ahead of whatever argument he'd meant to finish. He blinked once, looked briefly confused at his own feet, and walked out into the hall like the hall had always been his idea. Nobody noticed. There was nothing to notice. A loud boy told a louder boy to leave and the louder boy left; that is the most ordinary sentence in a Japanese high school.

But I was watching, the way I always watched, and I saw the third-year's body decide before he did. And I saw Kaito, still standing, touch two fingers under his nose like he'd caught a sneeze — and come away with a bright pinprick of red on the knuckle. He stared at it the way he'd stare at anything that interrupted him, wiped it on yesterday's jacket, and shrugged, already bored of winning.

Dry AC. Summer. Puberty. That was the easy story, and I let myself keep it.

Doi-sensei looked over his papers at the suddenly quieter room, at Kaito still upright in the aisle, and put the day's last fray into his voice. "Kurosawa. Sit *down*."

Kaito sat — late, with a roll of the eyes, like the sitting was his idea — and Doi-sensei exhaled the specific relief of a man who had won one (1) round against teenagers before first period had properly begun. Ordinary. The bleed and the early step stayed with me anyway, unnamed, the way the war app stayed face-down in my bag.

---

We found Ryn at Fullmoon Mart after last bell, which was where we found Ryn approximately always, because Fullmoon Mart had air conditioning, a fried-chicken case under a heat lamp that glowed like the last good light in the world, and Amano Ryn behind the register.

She was shorter than both of us — compact, athletic, built like someone who'd decided the counter was a suggestion. Dark brown hair clipped back for the shift, stubborn cowlick fighting free at the crown; a small silver stud in one ear catching every time she turned her head. Sleeves shoved up, apron strings double-knotted, ink from price tags on her fingers, and murder in her face before either of us had spoken.

"Don't," she said.

"We haven't—" I started.

"You're both wearing the face. The 'let's do something stupid' face. Kaito's wearing it because he's Kaito and you're wearing it because you'll agree to anything Kaito wants and then feel responsible when it goes wrong." She rang up a salaryman's canned coffee without looking at it. "It's her birthday Saturday, isn't it. That's the face. You're planning something."

"It's *your* birthday Saturday," Kaito said, delighted, leaning both elbows on the counter directly under the NO LEANING sign. "You can't plan your own surprise, it's against the rules."

"I can absolutely plan my own surprise, I plan everything, this store would burn down without me—" and then the door chimed and a kid in a too-big hoodie slid in fast along the drinks wall, and I felt the shift in her before I saw the reason for it. Ryn's whole body changed. She'd clocked the kid's hands, the sandwich already half up the front of the hoodie, the eyes on the door.

"Hey," she said.

The kid ran.

What I remember is that Ryn was over the counter before Kaito or I had processed the word *shoplifter*. She didn't shout for help. She didn't freeze. She vaulted the counter — one hand flat on the surface, a clean gymnast's pivot, sending a display of gum skittering — and went out the door after him into the wet neon evening while the automatic chime was still finishing its cheerful little *irasshaimase*.

We caught up half a block later. She had the kid by nothing — he'd dropped the sandwich and kept running, which was the correct choice — and she was standing in the middle of the pavement, hands on her knees, chest heaving, absolutely furious and starting, helplessly, to laugh. The pale scar on her right knee showed where her skirt had ridden up from the vault — childhood bike crash, she'd told us once, no romance attached.

"An egg sandwich," she wheezed, holding it up. It was crushed on one corner. "He risked a criminal record for a *crushed egg sandwich*. He didn't even want it. He just — he panicked, did you see his face, he didn't want to steal, he just walked in already scared—" She straightened up, and the laugh went out of her as fast as it had come, and something older moved behind her eyes. "He had the Haiga dust on his shoes," she said quietly. "The gray. You can't wash it out."

None of us said anything for a second. The war app buzzed in three pockets at once, its two tones, and none of us reached for it.

Then Kaito took the sandwich out of her hand, examined the crushed corner with a surgeon's gravity, and ate it in two bites. "Evidence," he said around it. "I'm disposing of it."

Ryn shoved him hard enough to move him a full step and he let her, grinning, and she was laughing again, and the evening was pink and loud and warm, and I stood there on the pavement of the only city in the world that mattered to me and I loved them both so much I had to look at the ground.

"So," Ryn said, when we were walking back, the three of us taking up the whole width of the pavement the way you can only do at fifteen. "Saturday. Since you're both incapable of surprising me. What's the plan?"

"Sakaba-dōri," Kaito said immediately. "Whole night. Arcade, the good ramen place, that karaoke tower—"

"The 'K' is dead."

"The 'K' has *always* been dead, Ryn, the dead 'K' is *tradition*—"

"Rooftop," I said.

They both looked at me. I don't know why I said it. It just came up, the way the apology had come up on the platform, ahead of the deciding.

"The school rooftop," I said. "Midnight. You can see the whole city from up there. The lights. All the way to—" I stopped before I said *the east*. "All the lights. We bring the cake up. We watch the city. You turn sixteen where you can see the whole thing at once."

Ryn was quiet for a second. The neon moved on her face — across the cowlick, the stud, the eyes that were suddenly older than sixteen.

"Yeah," she said softly. "Okay. The roof." And she smiled — the rare sincere one, the one she rationed — and beside me Kaito went quiet for half a second like someone had turned his volume down, which was how you knew it had landed somewhere he couldn't joke about yet.

"The roof," Kaito agreed, throwing an arm around each of our shoulders, hauling us in, too loud, too warm, the volume the point. "Midnight. All three of us. I'm telling you right now, nothing is going to ruin it. *Nothing.*"

He said it like a command. He said everything like a command.

And for exactly four more days, the world obeyed.
