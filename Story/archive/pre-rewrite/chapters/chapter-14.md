# Chapter 14 — Paper Teeth

"North Market," I said, before the kettle finished thinking about boiling. "That's where the auntie put him. Dawn. We go there."

Riku was already dressed. Scrap of cloth in his hair. Knuckles the color of old arguments. He had slept like someone who kept one ear on the door and called it rest. The cheap arcade-ward room smelled like fried oil from the claw parlor below and the bruise under my ribs had opinions about sitting up.

"We go where the teeth are," he said. "Yesterday the teeth looked at me. Not at your tall friend."

"The Plaza guy."

"The Plaza guy who knew my drop contact's face." He tossed a pair of boots at my feet — scuffed, not a eulogy, bought with half of what was left of the envelope. No joke. Inventory completed. "Put those on. Your funeral is cancelled. Temporary."

I put them on. Mira's scrap stayed in my pocket. *Cleaner. still say the target.* Kaito was still in this city. Ward-late. Every minute I spent not walking toward North Market felt like another door closing ahead of me.

"If we lose the morning—"

"If we walk into North Market with a civilian and two gray-greens already sniffing the Yards, we lose more than a morning." Riku checked the window reflection the way he had checked the pharmacy sleeve. "I have one more pickup. Small. Should be quiet. Then we hunt your mouth. In that order. Because dead boys don't stop anyone."

I wanted to argue. Soft. Reasonable. The kind of argument that sounded like *please*.

"If he's still in North Market—"

"Then he's still in Takamatsu," Riku said. "Which means he can wait twenty minutes while we don't get loaded into a van. Priority of operations. I invented it just now."

I shut up and followed him into the neon that hadn't gone to sleep yet. Takoyaki smoke. A salaryman asleep upright at a bus stop. Somewhere a karaoke tower murdered a love song with commitment. The city kept being loud on purpose, the way Kōgetsu used to, and I hated how much of me still wanted to believe loud meant safe.

---

The East Yards in morning light looked almost honest. Chain-link. Cardboard. Diesel. A forklift that had not moved since the war learned new vocabulary. Riku's pickup was supposed to be a handshake in a shed: three stamped sleeves, no names spoken, yen that pretended to be boring.

The bored-envelope man from Station Plaza was already inside.

Not a tablet this time. Hands in his pockets. Two gray-green behind him — young, polite, the kind of polite that has cuffs in it. The shed door stayed open like a mouth that had decided not to smile.

"Nakahara," the civilian said. Flat. Same energy as the envelope. "And friend. Good. Saves me walking."

Riku's weight shifted half a step toward the side door without looking like a shift. I felt my own feet plant wrong — school-body habit, even in new boots.

"Wrong shed," Riku said. "We're lost tourists."

"You're a drop runner with a burned contact and a soft-looking companion from a sealed ward." The civilian's eyes flicked to me. "Kōgetsu dust takes a while to wash. We have questions about crates. Names. Who's buying light without forms."

My hands came up before I told them to. "Sorry — we don't want trouble. We can leave. We didn't—"

One of the soldiers moved. Not a swing. A grab. Fingers on my wrist, the same shape as Tuesday school, and my mouth kept going because that was the only tool I had trusted for fifteen years. "Please. There's a mistake. We're just—"

The soft approach failed the way soft approaches fail when the other person has already finished the sentence. He shoved me into the shed wall. Breath left. The bruise under my ribs woke up screaming. Something metal kissed my other wrist — not locked, not yet, the promise of a cuff.

"Names," the civilian said, to Riku. "Or he rides."

Riku did not apologize. He moved.

Boot heel to a knee. Elbow. The side door became an idea with a body attached. "Feet," he snapped, and it was not a lesson tone — it was the tone you use when a fuse is already lit.

The soldier's grip tightened. My head filled with *sorry* and I stepped off the line instead — ugly, late, the way Riku had kicked into me in the pine lot — twisted the thumb-side, and my arm came free with a sound like fabric and skin disagreeing. I did not fall. I ran because running was suddenly the only grammar left.

We hit gravel. Chain-link. A shout. Something caught my jacket and tore. Riku yanked me sideways behind a stacked pallet as a gray-green shoulder went past where my head had been. He laughed once — short, stupid, adrenaline — and I laughed too, because my lungs had forgotten the difference between terror and a joke.

"Don't gift-wrap," he hissed, grinning like a cracked tooth. "Again."

We cut through the yards the way rumors cut through towns: low, fast, between things that pretended not to see. A loader turned his back with professional blindness. A dog barked once and chose a different career. Behind us paper teeth closed on an empty shed — polite voices rising, the civilian swearing without raising volume, which somehow made it worse.

My new boots slipped on gravel. I did not apologize to the gravel. Progress.

---

Cost arrived in small ugly pieces.

Riku's next drop was ash before it happened — the civilian had the contact's name, which meant the contact had the civilian's boot on his throat, which meant the quiet yen route through Takamatsu East was done. The room above the claw parlor was burned too; Riku would not say how he knew, only that we were not going back for the kettle. What little money we had left after the boots went to a different lock that smelled like bleach and someone else's cigarettes, three wards closer to the station than I wanted and farther from North Market than the chase wanted.

Mira's scrap stayed in my pocket. I checked twice. The corner still cut my thumb. Chase engine intact. Body less so: ribs, wrist, a split in my lip where the wall had introduced itself.

Riku sat on the new room's floor and counted what we had with his eyes closed. "Annoying," he said.

"I'm sorry—"

"If you apologize for surviving I will throw you out the window." He opened one eye. "You got free. Ugly. Counts. Tuesday school is not a religion yet, but it's not nothing."

I touched my lip and tasted iron. "They said sealed ward. Kōgetsu. They looked at me like I was already a form."

"Paper likes forms." He tossed me a cloth. "Wipe. You look like a rumor that lost a fight."

I wiped. It hurt. I laughed again, quieter, because the alternative was shaking and I was saving the shaking for later.

---

Station Plaza by afternoon was a throat full of announcements.

We did not walk through the middle. Riku took us along the service edge where luggage carts nested and gray-green liked to smoke when they thought they were off-stage. A radio crackled from a parked van — clipped, bored, official.

"—tall juvenile, female handler, last seen moving toward warehouse block west of Plaza after the Yards disturbance—"

My body stopped without asking. Riku's hand found my collar and kept me moving, soft enough not to look like a grab.

"They're using our mess as a broom," he muttered. "Your friend swept himself into the next corner while paper chewed on us."

Warehouse block west of Plaza. Not North Market. A ward closer. A step that had been a ward and was now a block.

"Tonight," I said.

"Tonight we don't poet-stare at a radio." He glanced at my boots, then at the van, then at the pink claw light far down the arcade like a fake star. "Tonight we look. If you freeze, I drag you. If I freeze—" He snorted. "I don't freeze. Bad habit."

We cut west before the announcements found a new polite way to say *hunt*.

---

The warehouse block smelled like dust and old rain trapped under tin.

Long sheds. Numbered doors. A loading bay with a broken lamp that flickered like it was negotiating. Somewhere a forklift beeped once and thought better of it. The sky over the pine hills went copper. My lip throbbed in time with my steps.

We were halfway down the second alley when I heard him.

Not a command. Not yet. Just a voice I would know in a fire — volume banked, still him — saying something short to someone I could not see, around the corner of shed twelve. Mira's calm answer under it, a correction, soft as a pen stroke.

I moved.

Riku swore and moved with me. We hit the corner too fast and too loud and the alley ahead was already emptying — two figures at the far end, tall and calm, cutting between sheds toward a chain-link gap. Kaito's jacket. The nick of his shoulder. Gone into the gap before my new boots learned the gravel.

"Kaito—!"

It left me as a shout, not a sentence with a target. Useless. Ordinary. The gap held only a swing of chain-link and the smell of diesel.

Riku caught my arm before I tore myself through. "Not that way. They'll have ears on the gap in ten seconds if they don't already." His breath was hard. Mine was worse. "You saw him. That's more than rumor. Don't waste it dying in a fence."

I stood in the alley with paper teeth behind us and a near-miss in front of us and the Mira scrap cutting my thumb like a pulse. Same city. Closer. Still late — but late by a corner now, not a dawn. The shout sat in my throat afterward like a stone I had thrown and watched miss.

For a second I almost told Riku the true shape — roof, radio, a soldier holding Ryn out over the lip as leverage, Kaito's *let her go* opening the hand too evenly — and then I didn't, because naming it in an alley with gray-green weather behind us felt like inviting another shed. And because the soft shelf was still there, stubborn: *him, not me; luck; people being kind.*

"We keep hunting," I said.

Riku looked at me, bruise to bruise, and nodded once.

"Overlapping inconvenience," he said. "Let's go be inconvenient."

We turned back into Takamatsu's loud ordinary evening — takoyaki smoke, J-Alert silence for once, patrols tightening somewhere behind it all — and I walked on sore feet toward the next block, carrying a shout that had not reached him and a body that had, for one ugly second, remembered how to get free.
