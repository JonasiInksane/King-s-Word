# Chapter 12 — Bridge Town

The stone bridge showed itself at the bottom of a long inland dip — old arches over a riverbed that had forgotten how to be wet — and Ishibashi arrived behind it the way rumors do: first as a shape, then as a smell, then as a problem with a name.

Under the arches, stalls were already shouting. Grilled squid that had no business this far from salt. A bus stop selling lies. At the near end, an FDC table that was not a checkpoint, only paper theater.

My blisters had opinions about the downhill. The farmer's barley tea from yesterday was a ghost in my throat. I wrapped my heels again and walked into the market noise like a boy who still believed arriving counted as catching up.

It did not. People said *this morning* the way people say *lucky* when they mean *wrong*. The diesel-pump tip from Kuroishi checked out in one line at the depot — tall kid, girl with him, batteries gone without a form, west toward Takamatsu at first light — and I did not make the quartermaster tell it twice. Confirmation, not a sermon. Cleaner sentence. Shorter loophole.

---

Under the middle arch, a stall auntie was shouting prices at grilled squid and losing the argument with her own voice. Beside her railing, a boy about twelve sat on the stone with his knees hugged up, blinking like someone waking from a nap he had not chosen.

"He told me to stay," the boy said, when the auntie noticed me noticing. Not to me at first — to the air, to the bridge, to anyone who would make the sentence smaller. "There was a scuffle. Someone grabbed my cousin's crate. The tall one said *stay.* I stayed." He laughed once, thin. "Sat here an hour. Couldn't get up. People walked around me like I was a wet sign. My legs work fine now. That's the joke."

The auntie clicked her tongs. "Luck," she said, and did not sound like she believed in luck. "Helped the cousin keep the crate. Left this one stuck like a nail. Don't look at me like that. I don't do magic. I do squid."

I almost said *I know the shape*. What I said was, "I'm sorry," which helped nobody and made the boy snort.

"You sound like my mom," he said.

Good. I would rather sound like someone's mom than like the mouth that had opened a soldier's hand on a roof and called it letting go.

---

The third piece came quieter, from a woman selling stamped junk and secondhand stamps under the last dry arch. She did not want to talk. She talked anyway, the way people do when a wrongness needs a witness more than it needs a customer.

"Girl with him corrected his wording," she said. Eyes on her trays. "Once. Soft. *Say who. Say what.* He listened. Repeated it cleaner. Then the hands at the depot moved." She finally looked at me. "She didn't thank anyone. Didn't apologize either. Just… taught. Like the bridge was a classroom."

Mira as coach, not sermon. Grammar in the residue — and still literal enough to nail a kid to a railing for an hour. Comfort and horror in the same breath. First light. Takamatsu. Hours ahead again.

---

I found Riku by almost ruining his job.

Under the bridge, past the squid smoke, the arches narrowed into a working dark where crates moved without price tags. Batteries. Stamped sleeves. Junk that had been paperwork yesterday and contraband today. A lean shape with hair tied in a scrap of cloth was shifting a crate from shadow to shadow like the stone itself had hired him.

I stepped wrong. My school shoe scuffed loud. A buyer at the far end looked up. Somewhere above, the gray-green at the paper table turned his head a fraction.

Riku's hand closed on my collar for the second time in two towns and yanked me into the shadow hard enough that my apologetic *sorry—* died as a squeak.

"You again," he hissed, then saw my face properly and the anger broke into a crooked-tooth grin that was almost worse. "Do you get paid to walk into other people's problems, or is it a hobby."

"I was following a rumor—"

"Congratulations. You found a better one. Me, almost getting my cargo taxed by a clipboard because you clap your feet like a festival." He shoved me behind the crate. "Don't breathe like a confession. And if you apologize to the air again I'm selling you to the squid auntie."

I stayed. Not because a voice made me. Because I still had legs that listened to fear the ordinary way.

The buyer argued. A local tough with a cut eyebrow wanted a cut of whatever hummed inside the plastic sleeves. Riku handled it the way he handled roads — short sentences, a joke that landed like a shove. When the tough's hand reached for my sleeve instead, I did the usual useless thing: hands up, mouth already shaping *sorry, I didn't—*

Riku yanked me again. Sideways. My shoulder hit stone. The tough's fingers closed on nothing. Riku's boot found the man's shin with casual precision, and then we were moving — crate, shadow, river-mud smell, up a side stair that smelled like wet rope — until the bridge noise became a ceiling instead of a throat.

Out on the warehouse-road shoulder, Riku set the crate down, exhaled, and laughed. Whole face. Stupid and bright.

"You apologized to a guy trying to rob us," he said. "I need that framed."

"Reflex," I said, and hated how true it was. "I apologize to vending machines."

"I know. You told me. Kuroishi. Widow rice." He squinted at my bag. "Any left."

"No."

"Tragic." He tore a squid skewer he'd somehow acquired mid-escape and held out half. Oil on his fingers. "Payment for almost getting me arrested. Eat. You look like a rumor that forgot to sleep."

I ate. It was too far inland and perfect. For three breaths the chase was just two idiots on a curb with squid, and my laugh came out real enough that a passing auntie glared like we'd stolen her morning.

Riku watched the west road.

"I've got a drop toward Takamatsu," he said. "Same direction as your tall friend and his classroom girl. You walk near my cargo, you keep your mouth shut around gray-green, and when someone grabs you—" He looked at my shoulder, at the place the tough had almost owned. "Don't say sorry. Move your feet."

"That's it? The whole lesson."

"That's the free sample. Full course costs more than widow rice." The grin flashed. "Try not to die before I invoice you."

---

We left Ishibashi before the paper table decided we were interesting.

The warehouse road climbed out of the riverbed in a long pale ribbon. Riku walked like exits were a language. I walked like blisters were. Same kilometers. Different math.

Near a kilometer marker someone had dropped a scrap of paper that did not belong to the wind. Torn list. Map tick. A neat hand that was not Kaito's — Mira's calm even in ink — with a line struck through *Ishibashi* and a circle around *Takamatsu* and a smaller note I almost wished I couldn't read: *cleaner. still say the target.*

I held it until my fingers hurt, then folded it into my pocket.

"Walk like it matters," Riku said. "And if your friend's luck sits anyone else down, don't stare like a poet. Staring gets you noticed."

"I'm trying to stop him."

Something colder moved under his grin — a flinch at the idea of stopping people with anything that sounded like an order. Then it was gone.

"Stop him with what," he said. "Apologies."

I didn't answer. Pine showed on the far ridge. Riku shifted the crate and asked, casual as weather, whether my tall friend always made people hand things over or if that was a new hobby.

"New," I said. "And getting newer."

"Great. Love a growth arc." He snorted. "If he tells me to hand over this crate, I'm biting him."

I almost named what the mouth was. I didn't. Naming it on an open road felt like inviting the gray-green table to grow legs.

The Mira scrap in my pocket had a hard corner. I kept my thumb on it until the bridge smell fell behind and the ridge lifted.

"Try to keep up," Riku said.

I did.
