# Chapter 31 — Same Locks

Mei scratched the floor plan into condensation on a cold pipe and the maintenance alcove off Block C became a delivery route, because speeches got stamped.

Third count had finished. Recycled air tasted metallic. A fluorescent strip buzzed like a mosquito that had learned bureaucracy. Her knuckle scar left a wet arc on the pipe — a confession waiting for a witness who hadn't earned one yet. Riku named exits the way he named them in East Yards: aloud, twice, until my mouth could find them without light.

"Service core," he said.

"Service core," I repeated.

"Down two. Loading bay. Don't invent a speech when the shutter sticks."

"Loading bay. No speech."

"Service road if we're lucky. Concrete ditch if we're not. Don't romanticize either."

"Don't romanticize."

I held the timing. Watching rooms until the room confessed — the one skill I'd practiced since Nagihama without meaning to make it a curriculum. Ninety seconds. Badge fiction. Bodies through a hatch before the building remembered it preferred columns.

"Keys," Mei said. "East-wing junior leaves his ring on the medical-floor hook when Yano smiles at him. I've watched him do it four days. Tonight I borrow." She didn't say *steal*. Borrowing still had manners; Habu understood manners. "You two crack the cell bolts — bodies, not wishes. When the core hatch opens for shift, we push A and B through like cargo that grew legs."

A drop of condensation fell and erased half a corridor. Somewhere behind the Block C grate a kid practiced standing still like it was a sport with prizes. CCTV domes watched the main hall with the patience of glass that never blinked.

"And Habu," Riku said.

"Habu will call contingency if he smells a Voice." Mei's eyes cut toward the east wing — sealed glass, high cameras, the mouth that had swallowed a jacket and a hymn and a flinch. "Your jacket is already on site. He'll pull outer minds for his extract. Same hour. Same habits of lock." She didn't smile. "We don't wave. We don't coordinate. If his sentences clear the cordon while our hands clear the cells, the kids get a weather neither of us ordered clean."

"Unknowing aid," I said, tasting it — the ledger wanting a column, the kitchen thought wanting a door.

"Overlap," Mei corrected. "Call it aid when they're past the loading bay."

Riku knocked his fist against my shoulder — the old non-word, the one that meant *curriculum* without making it a sermon. "Tonight's job is don't freeze when it works. Working is when people die for real."

I thought of Jun in a lane. Of Ryn over a lip — leverage, not rescue, the soldier's grip opening on grammar instead of mercy. Of folders stamped complete under Yano's cheerful hand. The kitchen thought stood up anyway, bruised and upright. *Reshape* meant nothing if I wouldn't stand for the people in the cells when the building reached for them — and Mira's stove and Habu's soft voice could argue in someone else's corridor. Ours had bolts.

"I'm in," I said.

Mei looked at me like she was weighing hinge against weight again, and this time the glance lingered — unpaid, warm, dangerous only in the way hope is dangerous. "Good. Bring the brain. Leave the apology in the bunk."

She smeared the condensation map flat with her palm. Maps that stayed visible got stamped. The pipe kept sweating. Somewhere a gray-green laughed at nothing Habu had said, and the laugh sounded like a form approving itself.

"Ninety seconds," Riku said again, like a prayer that preferred numbers.

"Ninety," I said.

Mei walked away first, chalk-white fingers trailing the wall like a second opinion. We followed at a useful distance — specials who had learned the complex's manners well enough to look boring.

---

Afternoon stretched into vocational detail on Level Two: mop carts, wax smell, the polite fiction of hygiene. Habu's glass office glowed soft white. Kanzaki's watch caught fluorescent light on the observation walk — silver, patient, the same metal that had checked time while a girl swung over a city bowl. I kept my eyes front the way Mei had ordered, and still felt the east wing like a tooth you can't stop touching with your tongue. Cellblocks A and B breathed through their grates. Someone in B laughed once — short, startled, almost illegal — and the laugh died under a camera's red blink.

"Hold the timing," Riku said under his breath as we pushed the cart. "Don't invent courage early."

"I'm inventing boredom," I said.

"Good. Habu buys boredom."

Evening made the medical floor theatrical.

Kanzaki walked Habu's glass corridor — soft voices, hard paper, silver watch catching the last honest strip of hallway light. Yano's processing wing stayed on too late, a white square that made the air look like it was waiting for a stamp. Through east-wing glass I caught a smear of movement — Mira's stillness, Kaito's shoulder, the particular geometry of two people who had paid rent on dark — and then only reflection, camera glare, the closed sign's empty manners. Suzumori did not appear at glass; she was a sound you felt in your teeth when the HVAC skipped, a hymn under the building's tired note.

A moth hit the corridor bulb and kept trying. Habu's smile did not miss a beat. Paper changed hands with the soft sound of endings being scheduled early.

Riku and I sat on our Block C mats and recited the route until the Haiga-dust boy in the corner told us to shut up or he'd invent a form of his own. We shut up. We listened instead to the volunteer cells settle: coughs, a whispered joke that tried to be older than the kid telling it, someone crying without permission and being shushed by someone younger. Armbands rustled. Thin blankets. The smell of floor wax and boiled institutional food and fear that had learned to whisper.

Through the grate I could almost feel A-block's restless feet — kids who still thought morning was a promise instead of a stamp. B-block quieter. Too quiet. The kind of quiet that made my hands want work.

"If the core hatch doesn't move," Riku murmured, "we abort. Halfway is a sealed corridor."

"If it moves," I said, "we don't stop to see why."

"Finally," he said. "Learning weather."

Good. Weather was a job. Weather didn't ask for speeches.

I sat with my back to concrete and did not sleep. The pad's *no* sat in my throat like a spare key I still refused to name. Somewhere behind east-wing glass Kaito was building sentences the way Mira had taught him — who, what, clean — aimed at guards and doors and the living minds that owned them. Same mouth that had screamed *let her go* at a roof and opened a hand on leverage. Somewhere Suzumori was steering him toward Mei's number like a bell checking for cracks. Somewhere Habu's contingency waited in a binder for the night a breach and a vocal signature arrived in the same column, and Kanzaki's watch was already practicing the face of a man who preferred boring forms and knew luxury when he saw it leave.

Same locks. Same hour. Two reasons.

The complex held its breath. HVAC. Distant PA static. A kid in A-block whispered a joke again and got shushed again. I counted the seconds the way the sitting guard had counted — not polite, not helpful, just stubborn — until the numbers stopped being numbers and became a door.

Riku's mat creaked once. "Haruki."

"Yeah."

"When it works, don't look at the east wing. Look at the kids."

"I know."

"Knowing and doing aren't the same job."

I almost smiled into the dark. "You're stealing my material."

"I improved it," he said, and went quiet enough to pass for readiness.

Outside our grate, Habu's corridor light made a rectangle of manners on polished floor. Inside, Block C breathed like a room trying not to be noticed. I ran the route once more without moving my mouth: alcove logic, medical hook, bolts, service-core hatch, loading bay, service road. Unknowing aid waiting in someone else's sentences. Overlap waiting to be renamed if we lived long enough to argue about words.

---

When the HVAC's note changed — the tiny dip that meant the west cycle was arming, badge readers believing a shift change they hadn't earned — Mei's shadow crossed our grate once.

Chalk-white fingers lifted: *now*.

Riku stood. I stood. Block C held its breath for boys it hadn't agreed to love. Mats creaked. The Haiga-dust kid rolled over and pretended to dream. Outside, waxed floor waited. The service-core hatch made the soft complaint of something about to become luck or poetry.

My hands were empty and awake. The kitchen thought stood up one more time, bruised and upright, and for once it didn't ask *whom*. It asked *where*, and the cells answered.

We went into the corridor toward bolts that still believed morning would count them — and toward locks that had already agreed, unknowing, to open for more than one reason at once.
