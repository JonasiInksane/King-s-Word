# Chapter 24 — Free the City

The gap opened as a service door with no smile on it — bleach, onions, the underside of a house that hurt people upstairs.

Riku didn't wait for a stamp.

"On your feet. Garden gate if we can. Visitor gate if we can't. Soft boy — exits."

I named them out loud the way he'd beaten into me in the pine lot: service stair behind, cross-hall left, garden glass ahead, the way we'd come already three turns dead. Gorō stood with us, cardigan on, signed form still a joke in my pocket.

A gray-green came around the corner reaching for Gorō's shoulder like luggage.

I moved my feet before my mouth. Grip, twist, the escape Riku had drilled until it was boring — the soldier's hand opened wrong and I was past him with Gorō's elbow in my palm, and Riku was already a step ahead, not looking impressed, only counting.

"Curriculum works," he said. "Don't get cute about it."

We ran.

---

The mansion's polite throat became a throat that shouted. Amber lights went mean. Soft carpets tried to trip us into being civilized. We hit a kitchen that smelled like stock and fear; a cook flattened against a fridge; Riku took a side door because side doors were what surviving Tuesdays looked like.

Two more gray-green in the garden corridor. One grabbed my collar. I planted, turned, felt the jacket seam scream, and Riku's boot found a knee with the economy of a man billing per flinch. The second reached for a radio. Gorō — sixty-something, gearbox hands — swung a fire extinguisher with the accuracy of a man who'd watched too many fools miss their stop, and the radio became a bad idea on the floor.

"Don't follow," Gorō barked at us, already looking past the garden glass to the pine dark where a gate hung half-open like a mouth that had forgotten to close. Alarms stacked. Headlights crawled the uphill road. The human job and the living job split in the same second.

"Gorō—!"

"I drove trams through floods, boy. I know a gap." He shoved the extinguisher into my empty hand like a farewell fact. "Keep the toll warm. Tell Machi I walked."

Then he was gone — cardigan into the pines, not escorted, not signed out, *running*, the way old men run when the alternative is becoming a receipt again.

I took one step after him.

Riku's hand found my shoulder. "He's out. We're not. Move."

Painful success. The job done without us delivering him. The hill still had us in its teeth.

---

Pursuit funneled us the wrong way.

Seals dropped behind the garden corridor. The visitor gate became a rumor. The brass-plate wing — the weather of precise voices — was suddenly the only throat still open, and gray-green drove us into it like dogs into a chute.

We broke into the garden study through a side door Riku kicked like it owed him money.

The mild civilian from the koi was at the desk, sleeves rolled. An FDC captain stood against the wall, already wrong in the eyes. Mira at a boy's shoulder like a held note. And the boy —

Kaito.

Not market-tired. Not almost. Scheduled. Nose pink with Backwash already spent on the room. When he looked up and saw me, something flickered — recognition without warmth — and then he filed me under *obstacle* the way Aihara filed cities under *managed*.

"You're early," the civilian said, pleasant, as if we'd come for plaques. "Lieutenant Aihara. Timing is a specialty of mine."

Kaito didn't look at him when he answered me. "Stay out of the way, Haruki."

My name in his mouth should have been a bridge. It landed like a command that hadn't decided to be one yet.

Riku's hand hovered near the weight in his waistband and did not draw. "We're leaving," he told the room, which was a lie the room intended to correct.

Kaito turned back to the desk. Mira's chin tipped — *who, what, finished*.

"You," Kaito said to Aihara. Exact. Cold. "Tell the truth about Kōgetsu Municipal High. The night of the attack. Why the alert timing was wrong. What you wanted from the two boys. Speak clearly. Do not lie. Do not stop until I say stop."

The air pressure changed. The captain flinched. Aihara's mouth opened on pulled teeth and then — worse — on a briefing.

---

The school had been flagged *before*. Acquisition cell. Birthday. Rooftop. Two boys who didn't know what they carried. J-Alert scheduled around the hunt — wrong for the city, right for the well. Calamity as cover.

"Assets," Aihara said, fond as a man discussing infrastructure. "Alive, preferably. A voice that cannot be disobeyed isn't a crime scene. You should be proud."

The roof where a soldier had held Ryn over the lip as leverage — wrist locked, thirty meters under her sneakers, *drop her or come quiet* — while Kanzaki checked his watch. A desk. This desk.

"Kaito—" I stepped in. Hands up. Soft. The old reflex. "Don't. He's talking. We can—"

"We can what?" Kaito's voice had gone flat — not empty yet, but decided. "Ask him nicely? File a complaint with the cook?" Mira didn't smile. She didn't need to. "He timed the night they used her on the roof. He calls us wells. Takamatsu doesn't get to keep him."

Soldiers hit the corridor behind us. Riku spun, dropped the first with a trip that was pure lot-work, barked "Left!" at me, and I moved, feet first, because mouths had already failed this room once tonight.

Aihara was still talking — names, cells, blade and filing hand — talking like relief, like the Word and his vanity had shaken hands.

Kaito wiped blood from his lip with his wrist.

I saw the sentence forming. I knew that sentence. I had heard it under a plum tree in another life and watched a boy become weather.

"Kaito, NO—!"

I lunged.

Too late, or exactly on time for failure.

"Kill yourself," Kaito said.

Not screamed. Not *not again*. Premeditated. A sentence with furniture under it. STAGE 4's door opening on a schedule.

Aihara's face did clinical surprise. Then his body obeyed with the same competence he brought to stamps. The captain made a sound; Kaito, without looking away from the desk, said, "Stay still. Be quiet. Count to five hundred," and the captain became furniture.

I hit the edge of the desk hard enough to bruise, hands empty, the shout still in my throat like a tool I hadn't known how to use. Complicity through a failed intervention — the ledger paid in a fight-space, not behind silk.

Mira checked Kaito's elbow once, practical. "Roof. Now."

---

The study became a storm of gray-green.

Riku finally drew — not to spray the room, to *own* a doorway for three seconds. The crack of it was ugly and useful. "Stairs!" he yelled, and I saw what he saw: Kaito and Mira already moving for a service ascent the mansion kept for people who mattered, craft-noise somewhere above like weather inventing itself.

We went after them because the alternative was becoming receipts on a carpet that still smelled like wax.

Corridor. Grip escape when a hand found my wrist. Riku's shoulder into a chest. My feet remembering exits faster than my grief. Living minds only — I watched Kaito turn once on the landing and tell two soldiers, "Drop your weapons and sit," and they did, smiling wrong, and he didn't look back to see if I was impressed.

He wasn't performing for me.

He was clearing a route.

We hit the lower roof stair as floodlights walked up the pines and a rotor found its voice overhead. The landlord's house had an H-pad on top — of course it did; men who ran towns always kept a sky door for themselves.

Kaito's jacket vanished up the last flight.

Riku spat blood from a split lip and grinned like a bad idea. "Curriculum's not done."

"He's going to fly."

"Then we climb."

Behind us the study held a body that had approved of wells. Ahead of us the night held a craft and a boy who scheduled deaths and a city that didn't know yet it had lost its landlord.

Gorō was somewhere in the pines, running free.

We were still inside the hill's mouth, going up.
