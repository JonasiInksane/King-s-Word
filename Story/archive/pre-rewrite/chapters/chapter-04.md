# Chapter 4 — Nagihama

My hand was over Kaito's eyes before he could see the street.

Not because he asked. Because his feet had stopped working the second we came out through the place where she'd landed, and somebody had to steer. I kept my own eyes open. One of us had to count curbs and shoulders and the gap between Kanzaki's men, who were busy being men who'd just lost two boys in smoke and a girl over a rail. How we got past them — my mother, a service corridor, the near explosion that ate the east stairwell — is the other story. Later. What matters is that by the time the river of people found the coast road, we were already in it, ash on our shoes and the east behind us the color I will now always mean when I say *east*.

I'm not going to tell you what the landing looked like. I know what Kaito did when he saw it, and what sound he didn't make, and I'm keeping that page. You don't get every page. Let me have one.

---

By dawn we were part of the river.

That's what the evacuation felt like from inside — not the screaming crush you'd imagine, but a slow gray current, the whole west-fleeing population of a dying city reduced to the pace of its most tired member. Grandmothers. Salarymen still in work shoes. A man carrying a birdcage with great care and nothing else, no bag, no coat, just the bird. Children who had stopped crying because crying, too, takes energy you eventually run out of. We moved with them along the coast road because the trains had stopped taking anyone who couldn't fight for a door, and ahead of us the road kept unrolling its gray ribbon toward a horizon that was, at least, not on fire.

I looked down hours in and saw the ash worked into my laces. Haiga dust. The exact gray Ryn had pointed out on a frightened boy's sneakers four days ago — *you can't wash it out* — and now it was on both our shoes. We were the frightened boys walking into some other town where some other sharp-eyed girl behind some other counter would clock us and go quiet.

Except there wouldn't be another girl like that. There was exactly one, and she was behind us, and we were walking away from her at the pace of a man carrying a birdcage.

Kaito didn't talk. And I need to be careful saying that, because "didn't talk" sounds like sulking. It wasn't. It was structural. Kaito had always been the volume of us, the sound the three of us made in the world, and something in him had been switched off at the roof lip and the silence where he used to be was a physical weight I carried on the same side as my bag. When he did speak — twice, to ask if I was okay, in that cracked half-a-radio voice the roof had left him — it cost him visibly, and he'd touch his throat afterward, surprised every time, like a man checking a wound he keeps forgetting he has.

I couldn't stop counting. Hours. Kilometers. The thing neither of us had said since the vending bank — *why did the hand open* — that we both walked around now the way you walk around a hole in a floor in the dark. He hadn't asked again. I think he knew I had an answer, and that neither of us was ready for me to say it.

---

We caught a train in the end. Not the fighting-for-doors kind — a local two-car thing, half its lights out, that a railway man in a filthy vest was running up the branch line into the hills because, he said, spitting off the platform, somebody ought to, and the company could dock his pay from the moon. Diesel and wet wool and too many people. We stood jammed in the join between the cars where the floor plates shifted, and the hills took us up and over, and when we came down the other side the air changed.

Salt. You smell a port before you see it. Salt and diesel and fish and the green rot-sweetness of tide-line, and under it woodsmoke, and something in my chest unclenched a single notch — the animal part that had been listening for the low third tone for two days and finally couldn't hear it anymore. The war was a rumor again. A thing on other people's phones.

In the crush I got pushed up against Kaito, closer than we'd stood since, and his collar had torn somewhere in the exodus and fallen open, and that was when I saw it.

Behind his left ear, at the soft place where the jaw begins, a small pale patch. Lighter than the skin around it. The kind of thing you'd never see unless you were pressed against someone in bad light with nothing to do but look. I have one too. Same place. Same side. My mother used to touch mine when I was small, absently, a thumb-brush while she was thinking about something else, and I'd stopped noticing it the way you stop noticing your own hands.

There was a reason to notice it now. I didn't have the reason. I stood in the swaying dark with tide-smell coming up through the floor plates and did not say *Kaito, look, we match*, because some part of me had started, without permission, keeping a private list of things about the two of us that were beginning not to add up. This went on the list. Silent. Unshared. Filed next to a soldier's grip opening on command and a lieutenant who'd said *do not listen to a single word he says.*

I looked away before he felt me looking. I was getting good at that. Not a skill I'm proud of.

---

Nagihama.

The sign was rusted at both corners. 凪浜 — *calm cove* — and the town lived up to it in the worst way, the way a held breath lives up to the word *quiet*. Small. A working port gone soft at the edges, boats under blue tarps, net-drying frames, a single main street bending down to the water with a shuttered pachinko place and a shrine and a shop selling the same three kinds of dried fish it had probably sold for eighty years. The calm was real and wrong at once — not peace, but distance. A place the war hadn't reached *yet*, and everyone in it walked with the specific carefulness of people who check the eastern horizon first thing every morning and are grateful, and ashamed of being grateful, and don't talk about either.

It was full of us. The river had reached here too, thinner, and pooled: refugees in the shrine grounds, in the school gym, sitting along the sea wall with their bags between their feet, looking east.

Kaito knew the way. That undid me a little, walking in — he knew it from the two summers the three of us had come out here when we were younger, back when Nagihama was just where Ryn's family was *from*, the boring inland-of-nowhere place she'd complained about being dragged to and then, every year, hadn't wanted to leave. Sugi's stall was in Kōgetsu but Sugi's *blood* was here; this was the house her husband was born in, the one she'd gone home to when the city fell, because when everything ends you go to the oldest walls you know. Kaito led us up the bending street and the stone steps slick with sea-damp and along the row of low tile-roofed houses to the one at the end, the one with the plum tree, and my legs knew it too once I saw the tree. We'd eaten under that tree. Ryn had fallen out of it at eleven and refused to cry.

The door was open before we reached it.

Amano Sugi stood in it, small and wiry and straight-backed, sun-dark hands folded in front of her indigo apron, white hair pinned in the ruthless bun that I had never in my life seen come loose. She'd heard the gate. She'd heard two sets of feet on the stones. She watched us come up the path and I watched her count — *two* — and look past us down the empty path for the third, and not find it.

Amano Sugi did us the hardest mercy there is: she did not make us say it.

She looked at the ash on our shoes and the ruin on our faces and the third person who was not there and was never going to be there again, and something moved through her, one long silent wave, from her folded hands up into her jaw and behind her eyes, and then it was gone, or not gone but *put somewhere*, stowed with the practiced hands of a woman who had stood in a doorway once already in her long life and counted and come up short. She had been a soldier's wife. She knew the shape we made. We were a telegram with legs.

"Come inside," she said.

That was all. No *what happened*, no *where is she*. Just: *come inside*. Gentle. An imperative so soft it was barely there. And God help me, after two days of walking, after a hand that opened because Kaito told it to, my body heard the small kind command and my throat closed — I flinched at being *told* to do a thing, even a thing as small and loving as coming in out of the cold. Kaito flinched too. We were both learning to be afraid of the imperative mood, and we didn't have the words yet for why.

We went in. She fed us — rice and pickled plum and hot tea, pressed on us with a firmness that brooked no grief at the table — and we ate because you cannot refuse Amano Sugi and because our bodies were animals that wanted to live even while the rest of us weren't sure we did. She didn't ask. She watched Kaito's hands shake around the cup and watched him not-quite-drink and watched the thin place in his voice when he thanked her, and I saw the flick of her old eyes, and I wondered for the first time how much the grandmother of a family this old actually knew about the things that ran in the deep water under a town like Nagihama. She said nothing. She refilled the cup he wasn't drinking.

Night came down over the cove. The held breath of the town went as quiet as it could get. I had just started, for the first time in two days, to feel the enormous exhausted pull of somewhere that was almost safe — when there was a knock at the open frame of the door, three soft raps, unhurried, and I looked up, and there was a girl standing in the doorway where Sugi had stood.

She was maybe seventeen. She filled the doorway the way Kaito used to fill a train car, except from the opposite direction — not warmth pushing out but a stillness pulling in, a quiet so composed it made the room rearrange itself around her without her lifting her voice. Dark hair cut blunt at the jaw. A face that didn't do the things faces do — no polite social flicker, no reading-the-room. Just watching, level and unhurried, the way the Kanzaki kind of person watches, and I felt my hackles go up before I understood why. Her left sleeve was pushed to the elbow, and along the inside of her forearm the skin was wrong — old burn-scar, shiny and puckered and pale, the deliberate ugly geometry of a thing that had been done to her rather than a thing that had happened. She didn't hide it. I got the sense she'd stopped hiding it a long time ago, on purpose, as a kind of statement she made before she said a word.

Sugi had gone very still by the stove.

The girl's eyes went over me — dismissed me, not unkindly, just *filed and set aside* — and landed on Kaito. On the shake in his hands. On the collar torn open at the pale mark I'd seen and said nothing about. On the wreck of him, the emptied-out silent wreck of the loudest person I had ever known. And where everyone else who would look at Kaito for the rest of that year would flinch, or pity, or fear, this girl looked at him and something in her face *settled*, like a hunter who has walked a very long way on a rumor and has just, at last, seen the tracks are real.

"You're him," she said. Quiet. To Kaito. Like the two of us weren't a stranger's house and a grieving old woman and a boy she'd never met. Like she'd been coming to this door her whole life. "The one from Kōgetsu. The roof."

Kaito didn't answer. He didn't have the voice, and he didn't have the will.

She crossed the room. Sugi said her name then — "*Mira*" — low, a warning, an old woman's warning that had history bent into it, that told me these two knew each other and that Sugi did not want this girl at her table tonight. Mira didn't stop. She crouched down in front of Kaito so she was looking up at him, so he'd have to see her, and she said the thing. The thing no one else in the world would say to him. The thing I couldn't say two nights ago in the dark under the vending bank because it wasn't true, and that she said now with the absolute unhesitating conviction of someone for whom it had never once been in question.

"It wasn't your fault," Mira said.

And I watched my best friend lift his head.

"You want to know whose fault it was?" Her voice never rose. It didn't have to. "The men who came up those stairs with guns for two boys and a girl on her birthday. The ones who held her over the edge to make you listen. The ones who've been coming up everyone's stairs for years." She reached out — not touching him, just offering the hand, palm up, an open hand where two nights ago a hand had opened and killed. "You didn't do this. *They* did this. And you're going to be able to make sure they can never do it to anyone again. I've been waiting a long time for someone like you."

I opened my mouth. I don't even know what I meant to say — *he's not, we're not, leave him alone* — some apology, some deflection, the only weapon I've ever owned.

But Kaito was already looking at her open hand.

And for the first time since the roof, in that wrecked ruin of a voice, my best friend said something that wasn't flat, that wasn't empty, that had the terrible beginning of the old volume climbing back up under it — not warmth this time. Something else. Something with a coal at the center of it.

"Say that again," Kaito said.

And she did.
