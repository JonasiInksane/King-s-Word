# Chapter 27 — Count

Forty-one in B, the guard said — thumb already chasing a total that wanted finishing more than truth — and the wall board flickered the same lie in green before sunrise had bothered to show up.

Third-floor corridor. Mesh. Rifles. Fluorescent strips warm on our faces. Disinfectant in every breath. They poured us out in rows that looked like school assembly if you squinted past the guns. A and B emptied younger and thinner, some still wearing sewn armbands from towns that had competed to be patriotic. A kid in B kept hitching his sleeve so the band showed, like proof of having belonged somewhere. Block C got the stretch near the wall display, irregular and special and already sweating through last night's bleach.

Warm plastic and boiled rice from a mess that hadn't decided if it was a cafeteria or a sentence. A vending machine hummed a four-note jingle nobody had paid for. Habu stood behind glass at the junction in a soft cardigan that made the rifles look like someone else's problem.

"Forty-two," said a voice from the east-wing mouth of the corridor.

Not loud. Corrective — the way a shop manager fixes a register before the customer notices the math. The guard turned, annoyed in advance, and found a girl already walking toward him like the tablet had been late for *her* appointment.

Taller than Ryn — first wrong comparison my brain made, and I hated it for being automatic. Sharper jaw. Short practical dark hair with no cowlick story. War-prisoner issue worn like she'd chosen it on purpose. A pale scar across the left knuckles — work or cuffs, not a story anyone wanted told soft.

She took the tablet without asking, smiled like the tally had always been hers, corrected the column with a tap that didn't apologize. Wall board caught up: forty-two, green, obedient. A whiteboard behind it, chalk half-erased, embarrassed to still exist.

"Forty-two in B," she said. "You skipped the kid with the cough by the water cooler. He's still breathing until he isn't." Two more taps — luck or contempt — tablet back in his hand. "Try counting people instead of finishing your shift early. The cameras notice."

The guard flushed. Someone in A laughed once and stopped like laughter had a penalty. Habu watched from behind glass, soft cardigan, soft face, note on a tablet — count, girl, or both. Men like him didn't waste ink.

Riku's tongue clicked beside me. Interest or warning. Hard to tell before coffee that didn't exist here.

"She's going to get stamped for that," I murmured.

"Or promoted," Riku said. "Buildings love useful knives."

Junior barked count's end. We stood. We sweated. Habu's soft voice congratulated the corridor on existing in columns, and the tablet girl — Mei, name still unknown — walked back toward the east wing like she'd borrowed the morning and intended to return it corrected.

---

Vocational detail after count — Habu's word for work that couldn't bleach the rifles off the walls.

Block C hauled crates someone with our blister pattern had hauled last week. Volunteers scrubbed until disinfectant won against dust. A junior shouted numbers. Fluorescents made every face tired and guilty, which is what fluorescents do for buildings pretending innocence.

I learned Riku's geometry while my shoulders learned the crates: intake lobby, biometric blue; service core muttering like a bored elevator shaft; loading bay on a painted timer strip; parking ramp that looked like a gift and sat in a camera's charity. East wing closed — *interesting* in Habu's dialect. Medical processing one floor down, alcohol wipes through the vents.

Between loads Riku made me name exits aloud until the Haiga-dust boy muttered we sounded like a cult.

"Better a cult than a column," Riku told him. The boy decided not to ask what that meant.

Sweat found my collar and stayed. Mesh sang under gloves that weren't mine. A gray-green walked the corridor with a tablet and the boredom of a man whose morning had been boring on purpose — the kind of boredom that meant someone upstairs had already decided what today was for. I named loading bay, service core, parking ramp under my breath, then the intake lobby again, then east wing closed — watching rooms until the room confessed, the way I'd learned to watch kitchens and bars and now a building that preferred you didn't look back.

"Again," Riku said.

"Lobby. Service core. Loading bay. Parking. East wing closed."

"Closed is a rumor until you check the lock. Don't check yet. Feet first. Curiosity later."

Another crate. My blister found a twin. In A-line a kid sang half a song and got told songs were for after vocational graduation — the building's way of saying *never* without the word.

---

The tablet girl was at the water cooler when we reached it — sleeve up, scar catching light without performing. Disinfectant and the metallic ghost of old cuffs. She filled a cup like she'd done it in worse places. The cooler coughed ice with the sound of a small machine deciding to be kind.

"Takamatsu specials," she said. Not a question. "Roof boys. Whole complex hummed before your van did. Sound travels when people are bored and scared. Also when they bet on whether specials last a week."

"We're—"

"Going to apologize for existing." Amused. "I hear it from here. Don't. Soft makes guards lean, and leaning is how forms get interesting." Eyes to Riku. "You don't look like you apologize. Good. Someone carries manners so the other one can refuse them."

Riku snorted. "He walks like the road owes him receipts. I'm just the receipt."

"Serizawa Mei." No hand — hands got counted. "War prisoner. Eastern paperwork. Not a volunteer — they hate mixed categories. Volunteers are *structure*. We're *leverage*." Light, the way hard things get said when you refuse to make them holy. Different face from the one I kept almost seeing. Same refusal to flinch for an audience. "Want the map, ask me. Want hope, ask someone who still believes the parking ramp is a door."

"Is it?" I asked.

"Picture of a door," Mei said. "Habu keeps the key in his manners. Manners open for tablets. Manners close for running." She drank, wiped her mouth with the scarred hand. "A and B think they're waiting for vocational graduation. East wing hears trucks at the loading bay at night. I've counted trucks. Trucks don't take graduates to jobs."

Riku's stillness — machinery under water. "How long have you been counting."

"Long enough the board lies on purpose and I correct it anyway." Cup crushed flat. "Correcting keeps me useful. Useful lasts longer than righteous." Shoulder-bump on purpose, a dare. "You collect guilt. Collect exits instead. Guilt doesn't open latches."

Junior shouted about crates. She went back to east-wing shadow like *closed* was a rumor she'd already annotated. Plastic cold in my teeth. Traitorous thought: the corridor had gained gravity — not Ryn's, not replacement, just new pull with its own weather.

Riku watched her go. "Don't cast her as a rescue. She rescued herself once and got a nicer cage for it."

"I wasn't—"

"You were." Nearest crate kicked into alignment. "Feet. Same religion. Building's a louder freeze. Start seeing ghosts in living girls, I'm putting you on night watch with the cough kid."

I nodded. Kept her under *map*. Left the warmer drawer shut.

Junior shouted again. Back to crates. Cooler ice cough. Mei's shoulder-bump stayed on my sleeve like a correction that wouldn't brush off.

---

Afternoon rhythm: count, work, thin meal smelling of rice and metal trays, second count, fluorescent light that never became night. Whispers moved sideways, sticking to sweat.

A and B had lost three last week. *Transferred east.* *Vocational graduation.* *Inventory adjustment.* Words didn't match the faces. The Haiga-dust boy — Kenji, offered after the thin meal like a coin he could afford — said medical floor smelled like chalk and endings, then shut up. Another irregular claimed he'd seen a truck leave the loading bay with no headlights and too many forms.

"Forms without headlights," Riku muttered on his bunk, arm over his eyes. "That's not a truck. That's a ditch with an engine."

Kenji stared at ceiling mesh. "Don't repeat that near Habu. Habu collects repeats."

I thought of Minato's oden. Of Gorō's unfinished beer. Of a kitchen somewhere that stirred wars and now, apparently, stirred buildings until the proof dissolved. The whom-question sat up once, glanced at the volunteer corridors, and put itself away for later — later being a luxury Shizuhara sold by the hour, interest included, no refunds on hope.

Evening count: Mei near the wall board, not stealing, watching numbers like other people watched exits. Habu's soft voice congratulated a complete day. She smiled with all her teeth and none of her eyes. He congratulated her civic helpfulness. She thanked him like a knife thanking a cutting board.

Walking back, a volunteer from A — fourteen, armband crooked — whispered to nobody, "If you're special, stay special. Specials don't ride the trucks." Guard looked over. Whisper became dust.

Another kid in B hitched his sleeve. Patriotic and useless under fluorescents that didn't care about towns. Cough kid made the line. Forty-two, if anyone still counted people instead of shifts.

Riku leaned close filing into Block C. "Tomorrow we learn what *adjustment* means with our own eyes. Tonight stay awake enough to hear trucks. Dream about saving corridors with manners, dream quieter. Manners are Habu's native language."

Through the mesh the volunteer floors settled under CCTV glow. Somewhere in the east wing a door locked with the manners of a butler. Somewhere below us a loading bay pretended. Somewhere my best friend was still a craft-shaped absence, and the tablet girl had just taught a building how to count its own lies.

PA cleared its throat — soft, endless — corridor etiquette nobody needed. Badge beeped three doors down. Mei's correction lived on the wall board in green digits that refused to lie for one more hour.

Mei was not Ryn. Not a volunteer. Gravity anyway — warmer than any place that called children columns had a right to be.
