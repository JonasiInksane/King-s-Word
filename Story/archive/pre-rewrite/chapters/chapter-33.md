# Chapter 33 — Zero

Gallery guns spoke first, and Habu did not run — men who erase children don't need to run; they flip a page.

The barrels were in the observation galleries — not aimed at Kaito, not at Mira, not at the east-wing extract. Aimed at the river of volunteers between corridor and service core. Habu's voice stayed soft on the PA while the hallway became a throat clearing itself.

"Inventory zero," he repeated, as if reminding a class of a due date. "Completed forms prefer clean floors. Gallery crews — execute. Do not waste rounds on the east anomaly. Prioritize the corridor column. Seal and vent."

The night tore indoors.

Metal shutters slammed down both ends of Level Two like eyelids deciding the dream was over. Recycled air turned thick, chemical, wrong — purge vents exhaling something that burned the nose before it burned anything else. Gallery guns spoke in short, trained bursts. Concrete dust jumped. Fluorescent strips flickered and kept working, because the building's manners outlived its contents.

I saw the cough-laughing kid lift halfway up and then stop being a sentence I could finish. His body folded against a wall that had never agreed to hold him. I saw armbands scatter like dropped receipts. I saw a girl from B reach the core hatch, fingers on the cold lip, and the lip did not save her.

The sound was worse than the mansion pad — not one command, not one hand opening, but a system finishing its math in a sealed box, metal talking faster than any mouth. The corridor had nowhere for the noise to go. It bounced. It lived in your teeth.

Someone screamed.

Someone else tried to make the scream into a name and lost both.

The HVAC skipped once, then resumed as if embarrassed.

Riku made a noise I had never heard from him — not a swear, not a joke, something torn out of the ditch he'd been carrying since fourteen. He grabbed at kids, shoved them sideways into alcove shadow, used his body like a door. A round chewed plaster where his knee had been. He didn't flinch for himself; he flinched for the next small shoulder.

"Down — alcove — move—"

His voice cracked and repaired itself mid-order. Curriculum under fire.

Mei did the same without theater — scarred knuckles white on a skinny wrist, hauling, counting under her breath the living as if chalk could still argue with steel.

"Left — left — don't stop — don't look—"

A girl tried to go back for someone already gone. Mei's hand found her collar and redirected her toward the hatch with the same absolute economy she'd used on keys.

"Living first," Mei said. "Dead don't need an escort."

I ran into the river and tried to pull. My hands found fabric, bone, the heat of a child who had believed an open hatch was weather. I dragged one into a maintenance niche and went back for another and the corridor answered with more math. Dust tasted like disinfectant and blood. My ears rang until the world became a narrow tunnel of wax and light and the wrong kind of weather. Sealed shutters made every breath feel borrowed.

A boy I'd lifted earlier clung to a pipe, eyes too wide.

"Is it over?" he asked, as if over were a stamp you could request.

"Not yet," I said, and hated how honest that sounded.

It couldn't argue. The chalk couldn't. I couldn't.

East wing: Kaito had Mei's block door open — I saw it through a gallery slit in a flash of lockdown amber — Suzumori humming through the wrongness, Mira pulling him toward the number they'd come for. The corridor behind them looked empty the way a promise looks empty after you've already spent it. A door held for nobody. A name without a body attached.

Mei wasn't there.

Mei was in the dust with us, choosing cells over legitimacy, scarred knuckles still counting what she could still touch.

The extract failed mid-reach the way kindness fails when the binder is faster: empty room, wrong girl, a mission holding a door for nobody.

I saw Mira's mouth move — recalculation, not grief. I saw Suzumori's hum try to make the empty room into a path anyway and fail the way keepers fail when the target has already chosen a different map.

Kaito turned toward the corridor. Toward the wipe. Toward the kids his open hatch had poured into Habu's contingency.

I will never forget his face in that light. Not scheduled. Not STAGE. Just a boy watching a building erase the proof he'd flinched for — and understanding that his sentences had opened the road into the gun. For half a breath he looked like the hallway kid again — loud, raw, still capable of flinching for someone else's pain. Then the amber made him older.

"No," he said, and for a heartbeat it was the old volume, raw, useless against metal and manners — the same volume that had hit a roof and opened a hand on leverage instead of saving anyone.

He shouted something cleaner after — drop, stop, kneel — grammar Mira would have approved, aimed at living minds behind triggers.

The Word does not land on rifles.

Living minds behind the triggers were already obeying Habu's binder, or their own training, or both — sealed in galleries, shutters down, protocol louder than any sentence that had to travel through concrete. Some of them might have flinched if he'd had another second, another angle, another night that hadn't already written this page. His mouth found cleaner grammar and still could not outrun a contingency that had been waiting in a soft voice for exactly this weather.

Mira's hand clamped his arm. Not comfort. Recalculation.

"The asset. The craft. We didn't come to die in a corridor."

He didn't move toward the craft. He stared at zero.

Suzumori's hum frayed. Even she flinched, once, at a chord no keeper wanted — a sound like a bell remembering funerals. Then she was humming again, thinner, steering what she could steer: Mira, Kaito, exit geometry. Not the dead. Keepers choose living priorities; the corridor does not get a vote.

I dropped to my knees on linoleum that wouldn't stop tasting wrong and tried to reshape anything — a word, a prayer, luck, the kitchen thought standing upright in my chest like a broken chair. The building did not listen. The whom-question laughed without sound.

*Reshaped by whom?*

Not me. Not here. Not in time.

The boy under the pipe was still there, mouth moving without air. I put my hand on his shoulder and felt nothing answer back that I could use. Somewhere Riku cursed Habu's soft voice into a religion of hate. Somewhere Mei's count broke and restarted and broke again. Somewhere a gallery light swept the hall like a highlighter finishing a paragraph. Vents kept exhaling. Shutters stayed shut. The river had nowhere left to run.

When the guns paused, the corridor was a completed form.

Smoke drifted from a gallery that had fired until the math was satisfied. The HVAC kept humming as if nothing human had ended. Somewhere deep in the service core a survivor ran without looking back, and I was glad I couldn't see their face, because faces make debts.

Volunteers who had been a river were loss under fluorescents that still pretended to be day. A few had made the lower landings; most had not. Bodies lay in the geometry of a stamp. Armbands looked like punctuation. Habu's PA clicked off with politeness.

Kanzaki checked his watch.

The silver catch of light made the whole night feel scheduled — the same wrist that had checked time while a girl swung over a city, now checking time while children stopped being sentences.

Yano, somewhere, would be cheerful about the paperwork tomorrow if tomorrow allowed paperwork.

Mei knelt beside a still armband and closed the kid's eyes with two fingers, brisk, unsentimental, devastating. Her face did not perform grief. Grief would have been a luxury the complex could file. She just finished the one kindness left that didn't require Habu's permission. Dust stuck to the wet at the corner of her mouth; she wiped it with the back of her hand like chalk residue.

For a second her hands shook. Then they didn't. That was worse.

"They opened," she said to no one. "We opened. And the building had a second key."

Riku pulled me up by the collar hard enough to hurt. His face was wet and furious and awake — the wetness not named, the fury named enough.

"Don't name it yet," he rasped. "Naming comes after we're not dying in the column with them."

I wanted to stay on my knees forever and invent a better verb. He didn't let me. Partner without the word: haul first, philosophy later.

My feet found linoleum. My lungs found air that still burned. Neither felt like forgiveness.

But I had already named the only thing that mattered: we had done it right, and right had been a road into Habu's math.

Across the sealed corridor Kaito stood in the amber light with Mira and an empty extract, and whatever decency he'd carried into Quiet Field finished dying in front of the same zero.
