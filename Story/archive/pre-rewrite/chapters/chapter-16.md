# Chapter 16 — Dry Fire

Standing still, it turned out, had a schedule.

Mornings were feet — the lot in the pine shadow, grip escapes, exits recited until I could name them half asleep. Days were whatever paid: hauling pallets at a depot that never asked our names, a dish pit behind a ramen counter where the steam forgave everything, one afternoon spent holding a ladder for an electrician who communicated entirely in sighs. Evenings were the city, learned street by street the way Riku had promised — which corners talked, which konbini didn't look at you twice, which alleys belonged to cats and which to worse.

A week of it. My blisters graduated into calluses. The yen envelope stopped being a rumor. I slept whole nights.

And the more ordinary the days got, the more clearly I saw the thing underneath them. Gray-green wasn't dramatic here — it was administrative. Convoys idled at Station Plaza with requisition stamps on the crates. Uniforms held corners the way furniture holds a room. The borrowed hospital sat on the western skyline collecting its own weather. The Federation didn't occupy Takamatsu so much as *own* it — the way a landlord owns a building he never visits: through paper, and through men paid to carry the paper.

I said something like that to Riku one evening on the bleach-building roof, watching a convoy crawl west with its escort of clipboards.

"Took you a week." He didn't look away from the trucks. "Now say the rest of it."

"The rest?"

"You're not chasing a boy anymore. You've planted yourself in front of *that*—" he tipped his chin at the convoy, the Plaza, the stamped skyline, the whole machine of it "—for whenever it moves. Him. Her. The thing that's hunting all of you. That's the job you've actually picked, soft boy, whether you've said it out loud or not." He stood, stretched until something in his back surrendered. "You don't stop a machine with polite feet. Tomorrow, before work."

"Tomorrow what?"

"Sleep," he said, and refused to elaborate, visibly enjoying it.

---

The gun was uglier up close than it had any right to be.

Not a rifle from a war feed — a short, scuffed sidearm he produced from under a bleach-room floorboard like a magician who hated magic. Morning light through the dirty window made the metal look bored.

"Unload," Riku said.

"It looks unloaded."

"Cute. Check anyway." He watched me fumble the magazine out, count the absence of bullets like they were konbini change, show him the empty chamber with hands that wanted to apologize to the concept of firearms. "Good. Dry fire first. You don't get live until you stop pointing it at your own foot."

"I wasn't—"

"You were negotiating with it." He kicked my ankle into stance — not soft, not cruel. "Feet. Same religion. The metal is just a louder way to freeze."

We were on the roof of the bleach building, low enough that Plaza paper wouldn't care, high enough that a missed angle wouldn't put a hole in a neighbor's kettle. Takamatsu noise rose around us: karaoke murder, a tram bell, someone winning nothing in a claw machine three streets over. Riku stood behind my shoulder and corrected my grip by moving my fingers like I was a broken tool he had decided, against advice, to keep.

"Sight. Breath. Press. Don't yank — it's a trigger, not a stuck drawer."

I dry-fired. The click was small and humiliating. I laughed once, helpless, and he snorted.

"Again. And stop aiming at the skyline like it insulted you. Pick a thing. A tank. A pipe. Small."

I aimed at a water tank on the next roof and clicked emptiness at it until my wrists shook. Between rounds he made me clear, show safe, holster in a waistband that was not designed for this century. The hour kept flickering between scary and stupid: me asking the gun politely to cooperate; Riku promising to bill me per flinch.

"Why a gun," I said, when my arms were jelly. "We stopped chasing. We work. We keep our feet. What changed?"

"Nothing changed. That's the point." He took the sidearm, checked it himself out of habit that looked like flinch, and set it down between us. "You saw it from the roof. You've decided to be inconvenient to something that owns the stamps, the corners, and the sky over that hospital. That's not a boy you're squaring up against — it's a machine with a payroll. Feet make you hard to grab. This makes you expensive to hold. I'd rather you have options that aren't *sorry*."

I nodded. The soft shelf in me filed the lesson under *feet, louder*. Not under *I am a weapon*. That shelf was still reserved for Kaito.

---

Afternoon was rent.

The envelope was healthy again, and we intended to keep it that way. Riku knew a man who knew a woman whose shotengai storeroom had surrendered to gravity, and for four hours we hauled shelving and flattened cartons and stacked crates of pickled things while the owner — tiny, ancient, tyrannical — directed us with a fly swatter like a conductor who hated the orchestra.

She paid in yen and, when Riku charmed her exactly one degree past professional, in a bag of day-old melonpan.

"You have a face like my late husband," she told me, which I chose to take well.

"Condolences twice," Riku said, and got the swatter across the shoulder, and we left before her generosity could develop conditions.

The city, walked at work-speed instead of chase-speed, kept turning out to be made of people. A tram driver who rang his bell at kids in the road with the weary rhythm of a man playing the same three notes his whole life. Two grannies arguing genially about a persimmon tree. Gray-green at the Plaza edge, yes, checking papers with that bored menace I'd learned in Kuroishi — but three streets off the Plaza the city simply went on being itself, loud and frying things, and I caught myself cataloguing exits out of habit and then cataloguing smells instead. Riku noticed. He didn't say anything, which from him was applause.

---

The bar found us the way important places do: by being the only lit door on a street that had otherwise given up.

Back lane off the Central Arcade, past the karaoke tower's service entrance — a wooden face the width of two doors, red lantern gone brown at the seams, and over the curtain a hand-painted sign: **みなと**. *Minato.* Harbor. In a town two ridges and a hundred kilometers from any salt.

"An inland harbor," I said. "That's either poetry or a zoning error."

"It's dinner," Riku said, and pushed through the curtain.

Inside: six stools, two tables, a shelf of bottles with more biography than label, and steam off a pot of oden that reached me somewhere behind the sternum, in a room I hadn't known I was still carrying. The woman behind the counter was fifty-something, broad-shouldered, hair in a bun speared with what I was fairly sure was a chopstick, and she assessed us in one flat glance — dust, knuckles, my healing lip — the way harbormasters assess weather.

"Working boys or trouble?"

"Working," Riku said. "Recently. There's a receipt."

"Hm." She set down two bowls before we'd sat, as if the ordering had been a formality she'd skipped on our behalf. "Then sit. Trouble eats standing up, that's how I know."

Her name was Machi — *Mama Machi* to the whole street, we'd learn, though she claimed the *Mama* was unauthorized. The bar was called Minato because she'd run one by an eastern harbor until the war ate the harbor. She said this the way the tram driver rang his bell: worn smooth, three notes, next stop. She did not ask where we were from. In her bar, apparently, everyone was from the same place, and the place was called *before*.

The regular at the corner stool was called Gorō — retired tram driver, hands like ginger root around a beer he made last the whole evening, opinions about everything, and a rule that he was owed one new fact per night by anyone under thirty. Riku paid the toll with something about cargo manifests. I paid with the vending machine at my old school that ate coins, and Gorō laughed like a gearbox and declared the Federation had been eating coins the same way for forty years, and Mama Machi told him that was politics, and he said everything was politics, and she said not her oden, and the whole bar — all six stools of it — agreed the oden was exempt.

I hadn't laughed like that since Nagihama. Maybe since before.

Riku got a beer without being asked his age. I got melon soda in a beer glass, which Mama Machi set down with such total ceremony that it took Riku a full three seconds to break, and then he was laughing into his elbow and I was defending my glass with both hands and Gorō was toasting me solemnly like a man honoring the fallen.

Somewhere in the second bowl, the war came in the way it always does — sideways, on the radio, a chyron's worth of *managed* — and the bar got one degree quieter. Gorō muttered about his tram line, the one they'd shortened twice for "materiel priority." Mama Machi wiped a glass that was already clean.

"You two are from out east somewhere," she said. Not a question. "You've got the look. Like you're listening for a chime."

"Everybody's listening for a chime," Riku said.

"Everybody here," she agreed, and looked at me, because I hadn't said anything and apparently that was loud. "What about you, melon soda. What do you listen for?"

I turned my glass around on the counter. In my pocket: one scrap of Mira's handwriting and a whole city I'd decided to stop ransacking. "I used to think the war was weather," I said. "Something that just — happens to you, and you carry an umbrella and don't ask why. But somebody somewhere is *making* it. It has a kitchen, like your oden. Somebody stands in it and stirs." I stopped, embarrassed by my own mouth. "I'd like to see the kitchen. That's all."

Gorō snorted. Mama Machi didn't. She looked at me for a second longer than the sentence deserved, then filled my glass without being asked.

"On the house," she said. "Come back Thursdays. Thursdays I make the good broth, and the boy who wants to see the kitchen shouldn't starve before he finds it."

---

We walked home full for the first time in weeks, and Riku was quiet in the way he got when he was filing something.

"You know that was a dangerous sentence," he said finally. "The kitchen thing. Wrong bar, wrong ears, that's a clipboard conversation."

"You think it was stupid."

"I think," he said, clicking his tongue once, "that it's the first thing you've said since Kuroishi that wasn't about your friend." He shouldered the door of the bleach building open and held it with his boot, which was Riku for courtesy. "Feet tomorrow. Dry fire after. And we're going back Thursday — she'll feed us, the old man knows every ward in this town from a tram window, and you—" he waved a hand at me, all of me, the whole soft inventory "—apparently do better with a harbor."

Magazine out. Chamber clear. The ritual he'd beaten into my hands, done on the table by lamplight while three streets over somebody's ballad wobbled up off the karaoke tower and refused to land.

Thursday was three days away. For the first time in a long while, I wanted a calendar for a reason that wasn't a chase.
