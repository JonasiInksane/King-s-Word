# Chapter 23 — The Hill

The message arrived the evening after the market the way Minato preferred its miracles — small, unromantic, and carried by someone who'd already paid.

A woman from Gorō's freight web, rope burns healed to pale rope, set a folded slip on Machi's counter and did not stay for broth. Gorō was at the heritage liaison office. Cooperative. Eligible for release if an employer or character witness signed before transfer inland. The slip smelled like ink and someone else's relief.

Machi read it twice. Then she wrote a letter in a hand that did not apologize: he was her regular; he paid his tab; freight questions were nonsense; send him home. She folded his cardigan into a cloth bag — the one they hadn't let him take — and pushed both across the counter.

"I don't go up that hill," she said. "They already have my season. You two go. Sign what they put in front of you. Bring him back walking."

"We're picking up a tram driver," Riku told me on the lane, bag over his shoulder, "not invading a country. Procedure. Exits. No speeches."

"I wasn't going to—"

"You have a face that invents speeches." He tapped the bag. "Cardigan. Letter. Soft boy. Be boring."

The tofu couple watched us leave from their bowl. The empty stool watched too. I filed the market almost under the same shelf as the whom-question and followed Riku north.

---

The pine road climbed after the speakers ended the evening, polite as always. The mansion sat in the shadow of trees that had outlived governors — walled garden, gravel that didn't crunch so much as approve, a glimpse of koi water black under lanterns that were heritage instead of festival. Officially a liaison office. Actually the landlord's mouth.

The visitor gate had a clipboard and a smile that had been through training.

Riku handed over Machi's letter like it was a delivery receipt. The smile almost laughed — two boys and a cardigan, asking for an old man — then read the stamps on the slip, found the shape it liked, and stamped us into existence.

"Annex waiting," the smile said. "Do not wander. The gardens are not for guests."

Inside was soft carpets and heritage plaques and gray-green in the corners like furniture that breathed. The air smelled like floor wax and old money and the particular cleanliness of places that hurt people with paper instead of fists. Riku walked half a step ahead, naming exits under his breath the way he'd made me name them in the lot — main gate behind, service corridor left, garden door with the koi light through glass.

"A fight you can leave," he murmured, "is a fight you won."

"We're not fighting."

"Yet."

---

Gorō was in a garden annex that wanted to be a waiting room and mostly succeeded — low chairs, a water pitcher, a window on the koi. Not a cell. Soft detention. He looked intact and furious, cardiganless, ginger-root hands folded like he was still holding a tram lever.

"You shouldn't have come," he said, which was how he said *thank you*.

Riku set the bag on his knees. Gorō pulled the cardigan on like armor that remembered him. For one second his face did something private, then locked back into gearbox.

"Fact," he told me, dry. "The coffee up here is worse than the depot's. That's how you know it's important."

I almost laughed. Hope, stupid and real, sat down in the annex with us.

A clerk brought a form. Character witness. Employer affirmation. Signature lines that believed in themselves. Riku signed where indicated. I signed where indicated. Gorō signed like a man releasing a brake. The clerk smiled the municipal smile and said one more desk, one more set of initials — the liaison's — and would we wait in the corridor, please, it would only take a moment.

"A moment," Gorō muttered. "I've been a moment for three days."

We waited in the corridor.

---

The corridor was longer than a moment. Soft light. Portraits of men who'd owned the town under different titles. Through a glass garden door I saw a mild civilian at a table by the koi — fifty-something, sleeves rolled, feeding the water with the patience of a hobby. An FDC captain stood beside him, not sitting. It took my eyes a beat too long to decide which of them outranked the other. The sitting one never looked up. The standing one never relaxed.

Riku's tongue clicked once against his teeth.

"What?" I whispered.

"Shape," he said. "File it. Don't name it."

The clerk returned without initials. The liaison was occupied. We would wait a little longer. Perhaps in the inner hall — closer to the office — if we would follow. Gorō stood with us. Walking, not cleared. The cardigan made him look like Minato again, which felt like a spell that might break if anyone noticed.

They walked us deeper.

Carpets thicker. Voices farther. Riku's exit list got quieter and more urgent — stairwell, courtyard, the way we'd come already three turns behind. My kitchen thought rose without permission: somebody stirred here. Somebody signed the seasons and the contributions and the voluntary interviews. I did not have a name for him. I had a mild man by a koi pond and a house that smelled like wax.

We were told to wait on a bench outside a door with a brass plate too polished to read in the dim. The clerk left. Footsteps receded. For a stretch of seconds we were alone with Gorō and the hill's throat.

"When they open it," Gorō said softly, "you take the stamp and you walk. No looking at gardens. No inventing courage."

"We're here for you," I said.

"I know. That's the problem."

---

The door at the far end of the hall opened before ours did.

Not the clerk. A different hinge, a different wing — light spilling, then a figure still enough that my market eyes flinched with recognition I couldn't trust. Dark hair. Level shoulders. Gone in the turn of a shoulder, or never there, or Mira and my almost inventing her out of steam and fear.

Then — raised voices, not loud, *precise*, from behind a door we hadn't been offered. A man's calm. Another voice younger, harder, shaped like someone who had stopped asking. I couldn't catch words. Only the weather of them: interrogation without shouting.

Riku's hand found my sleeve. "Not our door."

"Someone's—"

"Not. Our. Door." His eyes did the exit math and found it wanting. "We're inside the landlord's mouth. Gorō's with us. Paper isn't finished. We do not go hunting sounds."

A soft chime rolled through the mansion — not J-Alert, something local, heritage-polite — and the corridor lights warmed a shade toward lockdown amber. Somewhere a lock engaged with the manners of a butler.

Gorō exhaled through his nose. "That's not a stamp."

Our clerk did not return.

The brass-plate door stayed shut.

We sat on the bench with a signed form that meant nothing yet, a cardigan that meant everything, and a house that had decided the evening was no longer about release paperwork.

Riku leaned close, voice for us alone. "When a gap opens, we take it. Until then — breathe. Soft boy. Boring."

I breathed.

Through the garden glass, far down a cross-hall, the mild man by the koi was no longer feeding fish. He was standing. The captain was gone. The night had moved its pieces without asking us.

We had come to pick up a tram driver.

We were still inside the hill.

And somewhere in the walls — close enough to hear weather, not words — another errand was already running, and it was not ours, and it did not care that our form was almost finished.
