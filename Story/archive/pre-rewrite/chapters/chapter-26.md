# Chapter 26 — Transfer

The van's engine was already running when they marched us out of the wine cellar — cuffs first, zip-ties cut only long enough to swap for paper tags, boots on pine-polished stairs that smelled like someone else's war.

They'd left the bulb on down there. That was the insult that stuck: not the stone, not the radio voice calling us *assets*, not Riku testing zip-ties until his wrists went shiny and stopped. Continuous light. No night to put your face into. No dark to pretend the pad had been a dream with better editing.

"Curriculum didn't cover sky," Riku had said on the roof. In the cellar he revised: "Curriculum didn't cover landlords with H-pads."

I didn't laugh. My throat still held the pad — the *no* I hadn't owned, the city flinching like one animal, Kaito's craft becoming a rumor with rotors. Every time I closed my eyes I saw Mira's face do math. Two pressures. A recalculation with nothing to do with mercy.

Time in a wine cellar is a rumor. We measured it in boot passes above the stone and in the way Riku's breathing evened when he decided rest was a tool. Once, eyes shut, he said, "If they wanted us dead they'd have finished it on the roof. Dead is cheap. Paper is expensive. Relax into being expensive."

"That's not relaxing."

"It's the only kind available."

I thought about Machi's lantern. Gorō's unfinished beer. A cardigan that had walked into pines. The whom-question sat in my chest like a coin that had gotten wet and wouldn't spend clean. *Reshaped by whom?* The world had put me under a butler's lock, and the answer was still unpaid.

---

The garden was just a garden. Koi ignored us. A sealed van waited with its engine already sure of our itinerary.

"Where," Riku said.

The nearest gray-green didn't look at him. "Relocation. Vocational."

"That's two lies," Riku muttered, and got a cuff-chain jerk for the arithmetic.

I apologized to the van's bumper with my shin and didn't say sorry out loud. Progress, of a sort.

Takamatsu peeled away in a strip of black glass.

Through the grilled slit: karaoke tower dark, Station Plaza paper weather, the north hill's mansion lights already pretending nothing had happened on its roof — nothing except a soldier holding a girl over the lip as leverage while gray-green muscle spread across concrete, and Kaito screaming at the grabber instead of the edge. Not rescue. Leverage. I'd learned the difference in one second and hadn't stopped learning it since.

A J-Alert speaker on a corner recited something polite about managed conditions. Then pine. Then less pine. Then a road that forgot neon on purpose and remembered concrete instead.

Riku sat with his shoulder against mine — not comfort, shared geometry for corners the van took like it hated passengers. His knee bounced once and stopped. Counting turns. Of course he was counting turns. Every few kilometers a number under his breath, a map built out of absences.

"You naming the pad yet?" he asked, quiet enough for the grille.

"I'm trying not to."

"Good." A pause. "Naming is how they write you into their ledger. You start calling a thing *power* and suddenly every clipboard wants a column for it."

"What do I call it then."

"Weather you walked through. Shame if you need a shorter word." He tipped his head toward the grille. "Don't give the driver a vocabulary."

Hours. Diesel. The cuffs chewed. Someone up front played a radio low — war chyron voice, *managed*, the same lullaby cities used to put themselves to sleep. A commercial for melon soda came on, absurd as a joke told at a funeral, and then the lullaby returned.

Once, through static, a different tone: a clipped request for confirmation on *Takamatsu vocal anomaly, two signatures*, and a name I knew the way you know a watch sound.

Kanzaki.

Not in the van. On the air. Patient. Procedural. The kind of man who checked a calamity against a wristwatch and found the calamity late. The driver answered with a code and a destination that wasn't a city.

"Shizuhara," he said, like a weather report. "Detention Complex. Intake before second count. Specials in C."

Riku's bounce died for good. His shoulder went from geometry to blade.

"You know it?" I whispered.

"I know the *kind*," he said. "Towns sew armbands. Buildings collect the leftovers. If this is what I think, don't invent hope in the first hour. Hope makes you slow."

The van hit a seam hard enough to make my teeth click. Through the slit: chain fence, floodlights on a multi-floor concrete-and-glass block that looked like a hospital that had decided to stop healing people, a loading bay mouth lit the color of stale tea, CCTV domes catching our approach like bored eyes.

Shizuhara arrived as fluorescent manners.

---

They walked us into an intake lobby that smelled of disinfectant and warm electronics. Biometric pads glowed soft blue along a glass desk. A camera watched the ceiling as if the ceiling might try something. A woman in a white coat flashed past a corridor mouth — small, cheerful, already writing on a tablet that looked happier than any of us — and was gone before I could decide if her smile was a weapon.

Then the man with the soft voice filled the lobby the way kindness fills a room when it has paperwork behind it: completely, without raising anything.

"Welcome," he said. Mid-fifties, soft face, soft hands that had never needed to look soft. Civilian cardigan over gray-green authority, standing in a glass office that overlooked the lobby like a fishbowl with opinions. "I'm Habu. Colonel, if the paper insists. Here we prefer first names and accurate counts." He smiled at our cuffs as if they were a misunderstanding he was too polite to correct yet. "You two are late inventory from Takamatsu. Special hold. We'll find you bunks that match your paperwork."

"We're not—" I started, apology rising like a reflex.

Riku stepped on my foot. Hard.

Habu's eyes flicked to me, kind. "Everyone says that on the first day. Shizuhara is a relocation and vocational correction site for youth who need structure after irregular service. You'll work. You'll eat. You'll be counted. If you cooperate, the forms stay boring." He made a small note on a tablet. "If you don't, the forms get interesting, and interesting forms are expensive."

Behind him, a junior with a badge waited, bouncing on his heels like a boy who still thought badges were for school.

"Block C," Habu said. "Mixed irregulars. Not the volunteer floors — those are A and B. East wing is closed to you." He said *closed* the way a shopkeeper says *sold out*. "Corridor count is on the hour. Miss it and we assume you've become inventory loss, which is a phrase I dislike using before breakfast."

He looked at Riku when he said *inventory*. Not unkind. Not anything. The word just sat there, lobby-clean, and Riku's jaw did something that would have been a laugh if it had had any air left.

"Questions?" Habu asked, as if we were guests who'd arrived early for supper.

"What's east wing," Riku said.

"Closed," Habu repeated, still smiling. "Curiosity is a vocational skill we discourage in the first week. It tends to invent forms." He stepped aside. "Junior will show you your beds. Welcome to Quiet Field. Try to be countable."

---

Block C was a cell-corridor with twelve bunks behind mesh and seven boys who had already learned not to ask questions that made the air thicker. Fluorescent strips hummed the same note as the lobby. One boy had Haiga dust ghosting a shoe seam. One faced the wall like the wall owed him money. Nobody offered names. Names were another kind of column.

Riku claimed the corner with a sightline on both doors before I'd finished blinking. I sat on the opposite bunk and felt the zip-tie ghosts in my wrists. Through the corridor slit I could see A and B lines filing past — younger faces, armbands still sewn on some sleeves like jokes that had stopped being funny. Structure. Vocational. The words tried to be soft. The mesh did not.

A boy on the near bunk watched us without blinking. "Takamatsu specials," he said finally. "Roof. Whole building hummed before your van did. Don't ask me how. Sound travels when people are bored and scared."

"Thanks," I said, because my mouth still believed manners were a currency.

He snorted. "Don't thank. Eat when they feed you. Stand when they count. And if the white coat smiles at you, invent a fever that isn't interesting."

Riku spoke without looking at me. "Exits: intake lobby, service core, loading bay, parking ramp. East wing — closed. Closed means interesting."

"Habu called us inventory."

"He called the whole building a ledger." Riku's jaw worked once. "I've heard *volunteer* said like a supply category. I've never heard a colonel say *inventory loss* about breakfast. That's not a slip. That's a worldview with a badge."

Somewhere down the fluorescent hallway a soft PA cleared its throat with unbearable politeness. Second count complete. All accounted for. A guard's war app buzzed its two ugly tones through the wall, and nobody reached for a future. CCTV glass caught our bunk light and threw it back smaller.

I sat with my back to mesh that smelled like other people's fear washed out with bleach, and tried not to name the pad, the van, the soft man with the tablet, Kanzaki's voice on the radio like a returned debt. Naming would wait.

Somewhere above a different sky, Kaito was flying with an old woman who hummed and a girl who did math with her eyes. Somewhere in Takamatsu, an empty stool still faced a lantern. Somewhere in my chest the kitchen thought tried to stand up and found the floor had moved inland to a building that smiled when it said *loss*.

The PA clicked again — soft as a librarian — and recited nothing useful about corridor etiquette. Footsteps passed our mesh. A badge beeped at the Block C latch, polite and final, like a door practicing how to own you.

Riku's eyes found mine across the aisle, awake as knives.

"Tomorrow they count us," he said. "I want to see who holds the tablet."

Outside our door, fluorescent light kept its continuous opinion. The badge beep faded into the next corridor, and Quiet Field breathed around us like concrete pretending it had never learned what disposal meant.
