# Chapter 21 — Under Curfew

Two days after the stack went quiet wrong, Takamatsu put lanterns up like nothing had happened — which was how you knew something had.

Canceling the summer festival would have been an admission, and the Federation preferred its admissions managed, stamped, and preferably someone else's. So the shotengai association hung rice paper and bamboo from the covered arcade as scheduled, and the polite speakers on Station Plaza shortened the evening by an hour without ever using the word *curfew*, and the karaoke tower stayed dark except for one stubborn booth that sang like a man refusing to leave a party he hadn't been invited to in the first place.

Riku and I were on lantern duty again — the association still liked our feet — and a boy of about ten kept trying to light his section with a match he wasn't supposed to have.

"Put that out before you invent a different festival," Riku told him.

"It's windy," the boy said, as if wind were a personal insult.

"It's always windy under a roof with a hole. That's what holes are for." Riku took the match, pocketed it, and handed the kid a clip instead. "Clip first. Fire second. Civilization is mostly order of operations."

The kid looked at me for a second opinion. I shrugged. "He's right about holes."

We hung until the arcade looked almost like joy. Almost — because every third corner had a gray-green who hadn't been there last week, and the polite speakers kept reminding everyone that evening commerce would conclude at the earlier hour *for public safety*, and the depot had stopped calling us for anything that smelled like medicine. The foreman had said, without meeting our eyes, that Momo was fine and the heron was fine and we should find scrap work that didn't involve stamps for a while.

Machi had sent us out with broth in a jar anyway. "Lantern boys starve stupid," she'd said. "Don't."

We ate on a curb in the shade of a half-hung string, watching the association president argue with a ladder. The broth was good. The city was trying. I respected the effort even if the paperwork disagreed.

I kept tasting the clean run underneath it anyway — perfect paper, a smiling gate, an old woman out a door. Kaito's handwriting. Our truck in the quiet. The roof in the quiet too, if you listened long enough: leverage over a lip, a hand opening on the wrong grammar.

Riku licked the rim of the jar. "You're doing the face again."

"Which face."

"The one that thinks guilt is a map." He capped the jar. "It isn't. It's weather. You walk through it or you drown in it. Same boots either way."

---

Minato got visited again that evening, and this time nobody pretended it was the season.

The tea-suit came back with two gray-green instead of an escort that tried to look decorative. Same soft voice. Same municipal smile. Different ledger — thicker, stamped with something that made Machi's eyes go flat before he'd finished the greeting.

"Association security contribution," he said. "Adjusted for recent irregularities in the western logistics corridor."

He did not say *stack*. He did not say *quiet floors*. He said *corridor* the way you say a word that has been through legal twice.

The room knew anyway. Gorō's knuckles whitened on his glass. The tofu couple stopped mid-argument, which was historic. Someone at the wall table put money away without being asked.

Machi counted notes into the ledger without letting her face spend anything. Faster than last time. Harder. When she slid the tin back under the counter, the suit stamped a receipt that sounded like a door locking.

"Thank you for your civic cooperation," he said. "The hill appreciates merchants who understand timing."

He'd said *the hill*.

Gorō made a sound that might have been a laugh if it had had any air in it. Machi looked at him once — one harbormaster look — and he swallowed the rest.

The suit left. The gray-green peeled off the wall and followed. The broth on the stove kept simmering like a machine that hadn't been told the shift had changed.

"Irregularities," Gorō said to his beer. "I drove freight through irregularities for forty years. Now irregularities drive through me."

Nobody told him to be quiet. The room had already learned that quiet was what you did after the stamp, not before.

---

They came for him before the second beer was empty.

Not a raid — a collection. The tea-suit again, or his cousin with the same smile, and three soldiers who stood like furniture with opinions. One of them read from a tablet without looking up.

"Gorō. Voluntary interview regarding freight routing and irregular manifests associated with the western logistics corridor. Cooperation is appreciated."

*Voluntary.* The word hung in the bar like a bad joke nobody was allowed to laugh at.

Gorō set his glass down carefully, the way you set down something you intend to pick up later. He stood. His cardigan caught on the stool; he freed it without hurry. Forty years of trams had taught him how to leave a cabin without panicking the passengers.

"Don't follow," he told us — Riku and me, specifically, like we'd already started to. Then, to the room, dry as gearbox dust: "Keep the fact-toll warm. I'll want interest when I'm back."

Machi's face went harbor-hard. Not soft. Not pleading. The look of a woman measuring a storm she'd survived before and did not intend to romanticize.

"He'll need his coat," she said.

"He won't," the tablet soldier said, polite. "It's warm in the office."

They walked him out under the brown-edged lantern. He didn't look back. Looking back was for people who thought looking changed the destination.

His beer sat unfinished on the counter, foam collapsing in slow rings.

The tofu grandmother reached over, after a minute that lasted too long, and turned the glass a quarter turn so the handle faced his empty stool — the way you set a place for someone late. Nobody told her it was superstition. In Minato, superstition was just courtesy with better odds.

---

The bar thinned after that the way a room thins after a fight that didn't happen. People paid. People left. The tofu couple stayed and did not argue about snow. Machi wiped the counter in lines that got straighter and meaner.

I sat with the unfinished beer in the corner of my eye and felt the clean run climb back up my throat. Gorō's freight boys. Gorō's vouch. Gorō's slip that smelled like too much money for gauze. Somebody had needed a quiet night at the stack, and the quiet had been built out of his web, and now the hill was collecting the man who'd kept the web warm.

"We were part of it," I said, too quiet for anyone but Riku.

"We lifted crates," he said, same as the roof. Then, harder: "They didn't take him for crates. They took him because the mansion lost something and needs a name that fits in a ledger. Old tram drivers fit. Soft boys with guilt faces also fit — so keep yours in your pocket."

"I'm not going to storm the hill tonight."

"Good. Curriculum doesn't cover suicide by architecture." He tipped his chin at Machi. "Tonight we stay useful. Useful means present. Present means she doesn't close alone."

So we stayed. We washed bowls. We carried the oden pot when Machi decided it had earned retirement for the night. We listened to the polite speakers outside announce the earlier end of evening commerce one more time, cheerful as a weather report. When the last regular left, Machi locked the curtain and poured three cups of tea that were not on any menu.

"He talks too much about the hill," she said, not looking at us. "I told him. Talking is how they learn your address." She drank. "Doesn't mean they get to keep him."

"No," I said.

She glanced at me then — priced me, the way she priced everyone — and whatever number she got, she didn't share it. "Go home before the speakers invent a reason to walk you there."

---

Outside, the festival lanterns were lit under the shortened evening, a soft gold river down the arcade that ended too early at the curfew line. Kids still ran under them. A vendor still sold grilled something that made the air smell like summer refusing paperwork. Joy, rationed. Alive anyway.

We walked without talking until the bleach-building stairs.

"If I could reshape the world," I said, and heard how big it still sounded, even quiet, even to one person, "it wouldn't mean anything if I let the hill eat the people in the room while I was busy staring at kitchens."

Riku stopped on the landing. Waited.

"He took us in," I said. "Fact-toll. Flood story. Jobs. He told us not to follow, which is how you know we should be figuring out how." My hands were fists without permission. "I don't care about the old woman tonight. I don't care about helpful boys and counting guards. I care that Gorō's stool is empty because somebody on the north hill needed a receipt. We're getting him back. Or we're making sure the mansion doesn't get to eat the rest of the bar for free."

Riku studied me the way he studied exits — not impressed, not mocking, just checking whether the door I'd named was real.

"That's not a speech," he said finally. "That's a job."

"It's a job."

"Jobs have steps. Step one is not getting disappeared before breakfast." He started up the stairs. "Step two we invent when we know where they put tram drivers they call *voluntary*. Until then — feet in the morning. Scrap work. Watch Minato. Watch the hill without letting it watch you back."

"And the helpful boy?"

"Still in the city. Still not tonight's door." He didn't look soft when he said it. He looked like a man who'd learned the hard way what happens when you chase the wrong fire while your own house is burning. "You wanted to hold Takamatsu. This is what holding costs when the landlord reaches down."

On the roof we could see the arcade's gold still burning against the early dark — festival under glass, beauty on a shortened clock. Below it, somewhere, an empty stool faced a glass turned the wrong way for anyone still sitting.

I didn't look at the mansion.

I looked at the lanterns, and at the job that had finally grown a handle, and at a city that refused to die on schedule even when the speakers told it the evening was over.

"We get him back," I said again, not loud, not for a bar full of scaffolders — just for the night and the person standing in it with me.

Riku clicked his tongue once.

"Then stop announcing it to pigeons," he said, "and be annoying enough tomorrow that the hill regrets inventing paperwork."

Below us the arcade's gold kept burning against the early dark — festival under glass — and the empty stool, somewhere under it, waited like a job with a handle.
