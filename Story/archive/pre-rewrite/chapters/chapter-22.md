# Chapter 22 — Across the Market

The day after Gorō vanished into a voluntary word, the city offered us fog dressed as answers — which is the only kind of weather forecast the hill ever paid for.

At the depot the foreman stared into Momo's hydraulic fluid like it held scripture and said, without looking up, that interviews went to offices and offices went to paper and paper went wherever paper went, and he was very busy not knowing. One of Gorō's freight cousins — a woman with rope burns and a laugh she'd misplaced — said *heritage office* the way you'd say a cemetery's polite name, then told us not to ask her again where old men go when the hill gets hungry. Machi, polishing a glass that still didn't need it, said only: "North. Pines. You already knew."

She was right. Knowing the shape of a door and knowing how to open it were different jobs, and we were still inventing step two out of scrap work and shortened evenings.

Riku let me ask until asking started to sound like guilt with a shopping list. Then he put a cloth bag in my hands.

"Machi needs oil and greens before the speakers end the night," he said. "Standing in the bleach room inventing raids doesn't buy either. Market. Curfew. We move like people who still live here."

"And Gorō."

"Still missing. Still not found by speeches." He jerked his chin at the door. "Come on. Soft boy. Be useful."

---

North Market under the shortened evening was denser than it had any right to be — the whole shopping day compressed into fewer hours, bodies shoulder to shoulder under festival lanterns that turned steam gold. Grilled corn. Squid that had traveled farther than sense. A woman selling pickled plum out of a bucket like she was running a bank. Kids ducked between legs. Vendors shouted prices at a speed that assumed you already knew you were late.

Gray-green held the ends of the aisles the way furniture holds a room. Not a sweep. Presence. The polite kind that reminds you the evening has a clerk.

Riku moved through it like water that had studied people. I followed with the bag and tried to be a boy buying oil, which was harder than it sounded when every empty stool in Minato still faced the wrong way in my head.

"Oil," Riku said, pointing. "Then greens. Then we leave before the speakers get cheerful."

We got the oil. We were two stalls into the greens — Riku arguing softly with a seller about whether wilted counted as character — when the market opened a gap the way crowds sometimes do, for no reason except physics and luck.

Steam lifted off a takoyaki pan.

And there he was.

---

Kaito.

Across the aisle, past a pyramid of stacked melons and a woman counting change too slowly, wearing the dark jacket like it had grown onto him. Taller in the shoulders than the boy who'd left Nagihama — or maybe just standing like someone who'd learned to take up space on purpose. Thinner in the face. Tired in a way sleep wouldn't fix. Still him. The same mouth. The same hard bright eyes that softened only for two people, and one of them was dead on a roof where a soldier had used her as leverage and a command had opened the wrong hand, and one of them was standing in a night market with a bag of oil like an idiot.

He saw me the same second.

The market noise didn't stop. It just went somewhere else, a few feet outside my body. For one held breath there was only the gap, and the steam, and the fact of him — not a rumor, not a helpful handwriting on a guard's smile, *him*, close enough that if the crowd had been kinder I could have crossed it in eight steps.

He didn't shout.

I didn't shout.

Neither of us ran.

A half-step started in my legs and died when I saw the rest of the picture: gray-green turning into our aisle from the left, slow, bored, eyes doing the inventory of a crowd; and at Kaito's shoulder, the girl — Mira — still as a held note, one hand near his sleeve, not gripping, just *there*, the way a warning is there before it becomes a word. She didn't look at me long. She didn't need to. Her glance at him said *not now* with the clarity of a closed door.

Kaito's mouth moved.

Not a command — I knew his commands now by the weather they left behind. This was smaller. Softer. The shape of a name, or an apology that had nowhere to land. The market ate the sound before it reached me. Maybe there was no sound. Maybe I only wanted there to be one.

His hands were full of ordinary things — a wrapped loaf, a paper cone of something fried — errand hands, human hands. He looked like a boy sent out for food. He looked like my best friend. He looked like both at once, which was the part that hurt cleanest.

I lifted my empty hand a fraction. Hello. Stop. Stay. I didn't know which.

His eyes did the thing they used to do on the Rail Loop when he'd already decided to step between me and trouble — except the trouble was the aisle itself, and the patrol, and the girl at his shoulder, and whatever mission had made him into someone who could empty a *yes* and still buy bread under lanterns.

He didn't cross.

I didn't cross.

The friendship sat in the gap like a third person, alive, inconvenient, unpaid.

---

Then a handcart stacked with crates came through like a verdict, vendors yelling, shoppers compressing, steam and melons and elbows, and the gap sealed.

When it opened again — five seconds, ten — the jacket was gone. Mira's stillness was gone. Extracted, not fled; the kind of disappearance that has practice behind it. The patrol continued its bored inventory, finding nothing worth a clipboard.

I moved.

One step, two — bag swinging, mouth already shaping his name — and Riku's hand found my shoulder the way it had found my collar in other towns, firm, unanswerable.

"Not here."

"He's—"

"I saw." Riku's voice was low, for me only. "Not with this many eyes. Not with gray-green counting legs. You want Gorō, or do you want a spectacle that gets you both filed under *irregular*?"

The market roared back into my ears all at once — prices, oil, a child crying over dropped candy, the polite speakers warming up somewhere toward Station Plaza. Kaito was in this city. Close enough to touch. Untouchable.

I stopped.

The cost arrived on delay, like Backwash I'd heard about and never owned: I had chosen the human job, and the near-miss had used that choice as cover to walk away. Or I had chosen not to burn a market full of people for a reunion that wouldn't survive a patrol. Either way, the aisle was just an aisle again.

Riku's hand stayed one beat longer, then let go. "Greens," he said, rough. "We still owe Machi a bag that looks like we came for food."

We bought the greens. The seller gave us a look that said we'd been standing weird. Riku paid without banter. I carried both bags and felt the weight of oil like it was trying to be a metaphor and failing into being just oil. I preferred the oil.

---

The speakers ended the evening on schedule, cheerful as always. Lanterns dimmed in sections as vendors pulled cords and cursed softly about early nights. We walked the arcade toward Minato with the crowd thinning around us, festival gold going to ember.

"He was there," I said, finally. Not loud. Not for argument. Just so the night would have to hold it. "Same city. Across a stall. He looked—"

"Tired," Riku said. Not a question.

"Like him." My throat hurt. "Still like him."

Riku nodded once, as if that were useful information and also the worst kind. "Then he's not a ghost. Ghosts are easier. Living boys in markets means the hill still has our tram driver, and tomorrow we invent step two without chasing jackets through patrols."

"If I'd moved faster—"

"You'd have moved into furniture with opinions." He glanced sideways. "You stopped. That was the correct ugly choice. Don't decorate it."

Minato's lantern was still lit when we passed — brown-edged, stubborn. Through the curtain gap I saw the empty stool, the glass the tofu grandmother had turned, Machi's back at the counter like a breakwater. We left the oil and greens without staying. Machi took them, looked at our faces, and did not ask. Harbormasters know weather when it walks in.

On the bleach-building stairs I sat down hard, bags gone, hands empty, and let the almost sit on my tongue where the whom-question usually lived.

Close enough to touch.

Untouchable.

Same city as my best friend and an old man the mansion had decided was a receipt.

Riku unlocked the door above me.

"Tomorrow the job grows a second step," he said, "or we grow one for it. Either way — you saw him. Don't let it eat the stool."

I kept the almost on my tongue where the whom-question usually lived.

It ate a little anyway.

Below us the last market lanterns went dark on the shortened clock, and somewhere in the wards a jacket moved through streets I knew and didn't know, and neither of us called out, and the night — for once — did not ask us to be louder than we could afford.
