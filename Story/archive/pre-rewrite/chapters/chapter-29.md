# Chapter 29 — East Wing

The service core latch opened without a shift change — hydraulics polite, boots choosing concrete over standing — and I was already at the mesh before my sleep finished arguing.

Night at Shizuhara was a fluorescent's opinion. The cellblock smelled like dust that had learned to sleep standing up — thin mattresses, boiled rice ghosts, the iron tang of mesh that had ridden in on somebody's sleeves. Outside our door, the corridor held its breath the way buildings do when the count is over and the paperwork has gone to bed. Elevator shafts muttered. A soft PA clicked once and decided against a second take. Somewhere in Block A a kid coughed once and was shushed by someone younger, which was the kind of hierarchy that made my stomach do arithmetic it hadn't asked for.

Riku was already at the mesh beside me, silhouette cut out of thinner dark. "Timer's wrong," he whispered. "Or somebody moved the people who own the timer."

The corridor glass was cold enough to feel like a rule. Across the junction, under the service core's borrowed light, a gray-green sat down against a wall as if someone had offered him a chair made of numbers. He smiled at nothing. Counted under his breath — I couldn't hear the digits, only the rhythm of a mind that had been politely rearranged.

Living minds. I didn't have the language for that thought — only the pad's echo, Kaito's mouth, a city flinching. Luck, if I needed a shorter word — and I hated needing one.

"Don't invent a rescue," Riku murmured. "Watch first. Soft boys invent rescues and buildings invent poetry."

I watched. My hands found the mesh and stayed there, useless and awake.

---

We weren't the only ones awake.

Across the corridor mouth, near the east wing's closed sign, three shapes used the dark like they had paid rent on it. One small, unhurried — humming, thin as thread, the kind of sound that made the night feel older than Habu's tablet. One still in the way Mira was still, the stillness that filled doorways and made soldiers forget what questions were for. And one taller, jacket, the particular way a boy takes up space when he used to fill silence with volume and now fills it with aim.

My body knew him before my ledger did.

Kaito.

Not a rumor. Not a market gap across melon steam. Not a helpful handwriting on a guard's smile three towns ago. Here. In Habu's building. Blood-memory under his nose in my imagination even if I couldn't see the pink from Block C. Mira at his shoulder. The old woman from the H-pad — Suzumori, if rumor had a first name — humming like the night were a bell she'd heard before and was checking for cracks.

Riku's hand found my sleeve. Not comfort. Anchor. "Don't shout."

I didn't shout. My throat had already practiced that failure on a mansion roof — soldier's grip on a wrist over the lip, leverage while gray-green spread like a net, Kaito screaming *let her go* at the grabber and the hand opening evenly on command. Not rescue. Leverage. Tonight my throat stayed furniture.

They crossed the open junction without hurry. That was the worst part — hurry would have meant fear. This was schedule. The service latch stayed open for them the way a mouth stays open for a word it has already decided to obey. The sitting guard kept counting. Somewhere a camera dome decided not to scream.

They took a stair that should have gone east.

It went one floor wrong.

---

From our mesh I could see the medical gallery through a strip of observation glass Habu hadn't bothered to dim for night — a vertical slice of processing floor, white and patient, the kind of window buildings leave for staff who prefer not to smell what they supervise.

Kaito walked into that slice first.

I saw his shoulders stop before I understood why. Mira's hand came up half a beat late. The humming woman did not flinch.

Through the glass: a volunteer on the table. Small. Armband already in the tray. Yano's white coat moving with cheerful economy. A machine's soft tone. The child's foot twitched once — wrong, unfinished — and then the machine finished what the stamp had started, and the foot did not twitch again. Green digits on a wall board updated without drama. *Adjustment clean.* The PA in that room recited something soft about protocol. Nobody answered. Nobody needed to.

Horror first. Not folders. Not Habu's polite vocabulary. A living kid becoming a completed line while the building hummed like a refrigerator that had learned manners.

Kaito's face did something that wasn't schedule.

A flinch.

Small. Human. Still him enough to hurt.

Then Mira touched his elbow, and the flinch clocked back into mission, and they steered off the wrong floor toward the east wing with the humming woman as if the closed sign had been a suggestion written by someone who'd never met them — and as if the glass had only ever held inventory.

The east wing door opened for them.

A gray-green stepped aside, smiling the empty smile of the pad's soldiers — the helpful kind, the polite kind, the kind that made orderly cousins laugh without humor in bars. Kaito's head turned once toward the volunteer corridors — A and B quiet behind mesh, armbands pale in leftover light — and the flinch tried to stand up again and got sat back down.

The door shut.

The service latch closed a minute later, innocent as a form that had never been stamped wrong. The sitting guard was gone — walked, or walked-for, I couldn't tell — and the corridor exhaled disinfectant and elevator noise like nothing had hummed in the dark.

Mei.

Of course.

---

Rest after that was a rumor with bad manners.

I sat on the bunk and listened to Riku not sleep either — the particular stillness of a boy who'd worn a band and left it folded, now hearing the cells breathe through thin walls. The Haiga-dust kid in the corner muttered about forms. Someone in Block C turned over and the mattress complained. A moth argued with the corridor fluorescent and lost.

My chest kept stacking the pad and the market gap and the rooftop into one ugly coincidence: Kaito was in the kitchen now, and the kitchen was full of children, and I still didn't know whether his mouth was a door or a ditch.

"You're thinking loud," Riku said to the ceiling mesh.

"I'm professional about the thinking."

"Don't steal my bird material." A pause. "We don't wave at him. We don't ask. We use whatever hole the night leaves and we free the cells. Curriculum."

"Curriculum," I agreed, and hated how thin the word sounded against a closed sign.

Before dawn, cooler ice found us.

---

We found Mei at the water cooler, fingers already white like she'd been correcting the night. The corridor was the color of unfinished paperwork. Frost lived in the cooler's plastic lip. Habu's glass office hadn't woken its smile yet. Somewhere a soft PA tried a line and decided against a second take.

"Your friends have terrible manners," Mei said. "They woke my wing with a hymn and a threat. And they took the scenic route through medical like tourists who can't read floor numbers."

"They're not—" I started, and stopped, because *friends* was a word with a pad-shaped hole in it.

Mei capped her cup with the patience of someone who'd learned not to waste lids. "The tall one asked for the girl in the east wing by number. The calm one did math with her eyes. The old one looked at me like I was a kept thing season." Her mouth twitched. "I told them if they wanted an extract they could take the tablet and fight Habu for the clipboard. They didn't laugh. Remnant types never laugh at the right time."

Riku went flat. "Remnant."

"Amatsu leftovers," Mei said, as if naming a brand of soy sauce. "They collect legitimacy and weapons. Tonight they collected a wrong floor and a sitting guard. Tomorrow Habu will call it a drill and stamp someone." She looked at me harder — the hinge-and-weight look, unpaid and precise. "You know the tall one."

"Yes."

"Then you know why my wing smelled like nosebleeds." She didn't ask what the Word was. She didn't need to. War prisoners learned the shape of power even when nobody named it. "They're here for me. You're here for the cells. Try not to step on each other's feet when the locks agree with both of you by accident."

Ice lifted off the cooler rim in a thin gray ribbon. First light found the service latch shut again, innocent. The east wing looked closed — a mouth that had swallowed three shapes and was pretending it had only ever held columns. A gray-green walked the corridor with a tablet and the boredom of a man whose night had been boring on purpose.

"You think they'll help the volunteers?" I asked.

Mei's smile went sharp without borrowing anyone else's face. "I think he'll open what he needs open to reach me. If A and B are behind the same habit of keys, the cells get a weather they didn't order." She wiped cooler frost on her sleeve. "That's not help. That's overlap. Overlap is how you survive Tuesdays in a building like this."

Riku clicked his tongue once. "Two jobs on one floor plan. We don't coordinate with the jacket. We use the hole he leaves."

"And if the hole closes on the kids?" I said.

"Then we open something else with hands," Riku said. "That's the part that still belongs to us."

I watched the volunteer lines wake into count — small bodies standing in rows, armbands catching fluorescent light like receipts that hadn't decided what they proved yet — and felt the kitchen thought stand up bruised and upright. Kaito was behind a closed sign learning what Quiet Field did with proof. Whatever decency he had left had flinched at that glass. Flinches weren't keys. But they were weather reports.

Mei bumped my shoulder on her way to the wall board. "Eyes front, special. If you stare at the east wing all day, Habu will invent a form for longing."

I faced the corridor. Mesh. Armbands. Manners.

Habu's first smile of the morning walked the glass office like kindness on a schedule. Yano's medical light flicked on. The volunteers recited something that sounded like numbers pretending to be safety.

Somewhere behind that closed sign, my best friend was deciding which column his remaining human belonged to — and Habu's building was already writing Tuesday in gray-green ink, waiting to see whose sentence the locks would believe first.

A badge beeped at the east wing latch. Soft. Final. Awake.
