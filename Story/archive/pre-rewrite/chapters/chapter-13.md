# Chapter 13 — Survive a Tuesday

Riku was already walking when he said it — crate on one shoulder, chin aimed at the East Yards like the conversation was a toll booth we'd already paid.

"If you apologize to that vending machine," he said, "I'm leaving you here and telling the squid auntie you died heroically."

I had only been looking at it. The machine hummed at the edge of a sidewalk that had become crowded without asking permission — wet glass, wet asphalt, a karaoke tower missing a letter, takoyaki smoke dragging festival into an ordinary Tuesday. Pine hills above the sprawl. An elevated line braking somewhere like a polite argument. My hand had hovered near the coin slot out of habit, which in my defense was how I survived Kōgetsu, and which in Riku's defense was how people got hit by buses that kept schedules.

"I wasn't going to," I said.

"You were composing the apology in your head. I could hear the draft." He jerked his chin past the shotengai mouth. "East Yards. I drop this. You stop sightseeing. Then you can chase your tall friend's growth arc through the neon. Deal."

Mira's scrap sat in my pocket like a second blister — *Takamatsu* circled, *cleaner. still say the target.* Same city. Ward behind.

I kept up.

---

We cut through claw-machine pink and a salaryman losing money with religious commitment. For half a block I could almost pretend we were late for Ryn's shift at the arcade — apron, cowlick, the ordinary crime of being sixteen on a weekday. Then a gray-green sleeve showed between parked bikes near a pharmacy, and the pretend ended the Federation way: no bang, just weather with a uniform.

Riku checked the sleeve in a shop window and kept walking. Smarter than looking straight at it. I filed that under *feet*, which was becoming a religion I hadn't asked to join.

---

The East Yards smelled like diesel and cardboard and the dust of places that load and unload without permission. Chain-link. Sheds. A man with a tablet who did not want his face remembered took Riku's crate, counted sleeves, paid in an envelope that looked bored to be money. No speeches.

Then Riku looked at me.

"You're going to die of manners," he said, almost cheerful. "In a town this size. Someone grabs you in an alley and you say sorry and they put you in a truck and I find out from a stranger's paperwork. Annoying."

"I didn't ask you to—"

"I know. That's the annoying part." He kicked a scrap of wood out of a pallet stack — broom-handle length, splintered, honest. "Free sample is over. Full course. Now. Before the yards get interesting."

"Here?"

Behind us, Central Arcade was already a rumor of noise. In front of us, an empty lot under transplanted pines the city had planted to pretend the yards had dignity. Shade. Gravel. A bright street visible through the fence like a joke we weren't invited to.

"Here," Riku said. "City loud. Lesson quiet. Move."

---

He threw the stick. I caught it wrong and apologized to it. He laughed with his whole face, ugly and bright, and for a second the yards felt like Sakaba-dōri if Sakaba-dōri had scraped knuckles.

"Hands up. Feet like you mean to stay on the planet." He walked a circle around me. "Wider. You're standing like a boy waiting for a teacher to forgive him."

I widened. He kicked my ankle — not cruel, precise — and my weight dropped into a stance that felt stolen from someone braver.

He grinned.

"Good," he said. "You look like someone who might survive a Tuesday."

I snorted. It turned into a real laugh. "Is that the graduation speech."

"That's the enrollment." He came in without warning — grab at my wrist, the same shape as the tough under Ishibashi's bridge. My mouth shaped *sor—* and his other hand flicked my forehead.

"No. Feet. Don't gift-wrap your neck."

We ran it again. I failed funny: tripped on gravel, apologized to the pine, got my elbow hooked and spun like laundry. We ran it until funny became sore. My palms burned. My shoulder remembered stone from yesterday and filed a complaint. Riku did not go easy. He also did not go mean. He talked in short cuts between grabs — step off the line, break the thumb-side, don't look at my eyes like you're asking permission to exist.

"Again."

"I can't—"

"You can apologize later. Move."

I moved. The city kept being loud beyond the fence: a train braking, someone winning nothing in an arcade, a vendor laughing too hard at a joke. Inside the lot I learned the smaller noise of my own breath when a hand closed and I did not say sorry first.

The clean success was small and stupid and mine. His fingers found my sleeve; I stepped off, twisted the way he'd shown once, and my arm came free with a sound like fabric changing its mind. I didn't fall. I didn't apologize. I stood there blinking like the kid on the Ishibashi railing after an hour of *stay*, except I had done this myself.

"See," he said. "Feet. Not speeches."

I was breathing hard enough to taste dust. "If I find him—"

"Your tall friend." Riku's eyes flattened a millimeter. "You stop him with feet. With not freezing. Mouths are how people get trucks." He jerked his chin at the bright street. "Don't start naming magic in my lesson. I teach bodies."

I nodded. He was right about trucks.

We went one more round. I failed again, got a bruise blooming under my ribs where his knuckle found the gap in my pride, and laughed anyway when he called my stance "a scarecrow that learned to flinch."

"Tomorrow," he said, catching the stick when I dropped it, "you stop dropping weapons you don't even have yet."

"Yet," I repeated, and something in my stomach flipped — just the ordinary terror of guns existing in the same sentence as my hands.

"Feet first," Riku said, reading the flip. "Don't get ambitious. Ambition buys nosebleeds."

---

After, he bought two onigiri from a konbini at the yard gate with the envelope money and tossed me one like a challenge. Plum. Not Sugi's. Close enough to hurt and help in the same chew.

"I'm crashing in the cheap ward by the arcade tonight," he said, not looking at me. "Room with a lock that pretends. You keep up, I won't shove you in a ditch. You fall behind, I don't wait. Clear."

I waited for the allergy speech. It didn't come. He glanced sideways instead, grin thinner. "Consider this… overlapping inconvenience."

"Overlapping inconvenience," I repeated.

"Don't make it a brand."

I almost thanked him. He pointed a finger at my face before the word left.

"Itchy," he warned.

I ate the rice instead. Same cheap ward. Not a vow. Something that might survive a Tuesday.

---

Central Arcade gave us three sentences of residue before Station Plaza could: tall boy at dawn, North Market; calm girl corrected his wording; he told a man to *put that down*, and the man did, and afterward the boy had blood under his nose. Still in the city. Another ward.

"First light was Ishibashi," I said. "Now dawn here. He's faster."

"Or you're slower." Riku flicked my bruise through my shirt, not gentle. "Hence Tuesday school."

---

Near Station Plaza the air got paper-shaped.

Gray-green by the ticket gates — not a sweep, not yet, just two men with tablets watching faces the way Kanzaki's world watched faces. A third talked low with a civilian who had the same bored-envelope energy as Riku's drop contact. The civilian's eyes snagged on Riku for half a second too long. Riku's shoulders did not change. His mouth did: smile for tourists, eyes for exits.

"Walk," he said softly. "Not run. Running is a confession."

We walked into the neon wash of the arcade strip. If someone was sniffing the East Yards drop, staying in Takamatsu was suddenly both trap and only option. If Kaito was still under these pines and speakers, leaving would be another way to arrive too late.

The civilian by the gates did not follow. He watched. That was worse. Riku's jaw set once, then smoothed.

Under a claw-machine parlor's pink light he said, "Cheap ward's that way. You coming."

"Yeah."

"Good. Tomorrow we do feet again. And if your friend sits anyone down in North Market, you don't poet-stare. You move." He eyed my shoes once — no joke, just inventory — and added, "We find you boots that aren't a eulogy."

For a breath Takamatsu was only takoyaki and diesel and the bruise under my ribs proving Tuesday school was real. Behind us, Station Plaza kept its weather. Ahead, the pink light.

I followed it in.
