# Chapter 5 — Low Tide

There was no body to bury.

Kōgetsu had fallen — not all at once, cities die in stages, arguing the whole way down — but the eastern half was gone under the thing that had risen over the wards on Ryn's birthday, and the western half was a closed evacuation zone that no one was being let back into, least of all two boys the Federation Defense Command had a particular interest in. No going back. No street to stand on, no place to lay flowers, no tired clerk writing her name into a book. Just the fact of her, and then the absence of her, and nothing in between for our hands to hold.

Grief needs somewhere to put itself down. It comes into the house like weather and it needs a surface — a table, a grave, some flat place to be set — and when there isn't one it just circles the rooms of you, homeless, knocking things off shelves.

Sugi understood this without saying it. On our second morning in the house — Mira's knock still only one night behind us — she came out into the gray kitchen light and put a folded square of white cloth on the table between us.

"We'll do it at low tide," she said.

That was the whole of the announcement.

---

She spent the day building the rite the way she'd spent the first night building a meal — with her hands, out of whatever was to hand, because it was the only language grief couldn't take away. Some of the old women of Nagihama came, the widows, the ones who checked the eastern horizon every dawn and knew exactly what we were. They didn't offer words either. They brought things: a paper lantern, a length of good rope, salt, a bottle of clear liquor with no label. One of them folded the white cloth into a shape I didn't recognize and Sugi did, and the two of them worked at it at the table with their heads bent together, and I understood that this was a thing this coast had done many times, for many boys who went east and became a name on a scrolling ribbon, and that the town had a shape ready for a grief with no body in it because the town had needed that shape before.

It was Kaito who produced the thing we would give the sea.

He'd been silent all day, banked-down, Mira's words from that first night still sitting under the grief where I could feel them but not see them. In the afternoon he went out to the entryway where our two ruined jackets hung, and came back holding something in both hands the way I'd held the cake box a lifetime ago, careful as if it might wake, and set it on the table next to the folded cloth.

The hideous cat. The one from the arcade, the birthday night, the one he'd fed nine hundred yen and lost and I'd won by accident on my second try. Crushed flat down one side, stiff with dried salt, its one remaining plastic eye gone milky. I had no idea — none — that any of that night had survived.

"She gave it back to me," Kaito said. "On the roof. Before." He stopped. His jaw worked. "Said it was too ugly to be hers. Said I'd won it fair, technically, because you cheated." A sound came out of him that was trying very hard to be a laugh and did not make it even a third of the way. "I put it in my jacket. I don't — I don't remember doing it. It was just there after."

So that was the body. A nine-hundred-yen cat with one milky eye. That was all of her that made it out of the city, and it made it out in the pocket of the boy who would spend the rest of the story unable to forgive himself, and if you had told me God designed it that way on purpose I would not have argued.

---

Low tide at Nagihama pulls the sea back a long way, over flats that shine like hammered tin under a sky going lilac and gray. We walked out onto them at dusk, the whole small procession of us, the widows and Sugi and the two boys and the salt-stiff cat riding on the folded white cloth in Sugi's careful hands, and our feet left prints in the wet sand that the sea would take back within the hour, which felt, that evening, like the entire point.

The widows sang something low and old that I didn't know and will never find again. Sugi spoke to the water in a dialect too thick and too private for me to follow, and none of it was for me anyway. I stood in the cold coming up through my shoes and watched the horizon out east where, even here, there was that low uneven glow along the edge of the world, the war going on being the war, indifferent, and I thought about a girl who vaulted a counter for a crushed egg sandwich because a frightened boy had panicked, and I felt the weather in me finally find its surface, finally have somewhere to set itself down, and I broke. Quietly. The way I do everything. I stood on the shining flats and I cried without any sound and Sugi reached back without looking and held my wrist, hard, the way the soldier had held Ryn over the lip as leverage, and did not let go.

Then it was time to give her to the sea.

The rite was simple. You waded to the tide line with the thing that held the grief, and you set it on the water, and you let the low tide carry it out — you *let it go*, that was the phrase one of the widows used, gentle, ancient, meaning nothing by it, *now we let her go, now we let her go home to the water* — and I felt the words go into Kaito like a blade goes in, clean, barely a sound. I saw him flinch. I flinched too. Two nights of walking and a lifetime of ordinary Tuesdays had taught our bodies to fear those exact words, and here they were dressed up in kindness at the edge of the sea, and neither of us had the language yet to explain why they were unbearable.

Sugi held out the folded cloth with the cat on it. To both of us. An offering, and a question.

I took my side of it. I was ready. That's the thing I've never known whether to be ashamed of — that I was *ready*, that the soft part of me that has always known how to release a grudge, defuse a fight, say sorry to a rectangle, that part of me knew how to open my hands and let the water have her. I could do it. I wanted, God help me, the peace of having done it.

Kaito took his side. We waded out together, the cold climbing our shins, and we crouched at the tide line and set the white cloth on the black water, and it caught and lifted, buoyant, turning slowly, the milky-eyed cat riding it out toward the last light — and I opened my hands.

And Kaito didn't.

I felt it through the cloth first, the tug, the wrongness — the little raft turning back against the pull because one corner of it was still held. I looked over and Kaito had a fistful of the cloth and a face I had never seen on him, not once, not in three years, a face with nothing loud in it at all, and the tide was pulling and he was not letting it, his knuckles white and shaking above the black water, and the sea wanted her and he would not *let her go*.

"Kaito," I said.

"I can't," he said. Just that. Not to me. To her, maybe. To the cat. To the whole indifferent glowing horizon. "I can't. I'm sorry. I can't do it again. I'm not — I'm never doing it again."

And he pulled the cloth back in. Gently. He lifted the ruined cat off the water and pressed it to the front of his jacket with both hands and stood up in the cold shallows with the sea running off him, and the tide went out without her, and none of the widows said a word, and Sugi closed her eyes, and I understood — a beat too late and a beat too clearly — that my best friend and I had just done the same grief in two different directions, and that the distance those directions opened was going to keep opening for the rest of our lives.

On the sea wall, above us, a figure stood watching. Mira. She didn't come down onto the flats. She didn't say anything, didn't beckon, didn't smile. She just watched Kaito refuse to let go, and something in her stillness looked, for the first time, almost like tenderness — the tenderness of a person recognizing her own shape in someone else. Then she turned and walked back into the town, and left him to us, for now. She could afford to. She'd seen what she needed to see.

---

We sat on the sea wall afterward, the two of us, while the widows walked Sugi home. The cat sat between us, drying in the wind, staring at nothing with its one good eye.

I almost told him then. Everything. The hand that opened too evenly when he shouted at a man who was using Ryn as leverage. The lieutenant who'd said *don't listen to a single word he says.* The mark behind his left ear that matched the one behind mine. It was all right there, and he was right beside me broken open in a way he might never be again, and some part of me knew that this — now, tonight, before anything hardened — was the last easy moment we would ever have to say the true things to each other.

"You okay?" I said.

"No," he said.

We watched the water. That was as close as I came. I have had a long time to hate myself for it and I've made my peace with the hating; it's just another thing I carry now.

"I'm going to find out who they were," Kaito said finally, quiet, even, heat under the quiet now, no flame. "The ones who came up the stairs. The ones who held her there. The ones who send them. I'm going to find out, and I'm going to make it so they can't do it to anyone else. Ever." He wasn't looking at me. He was looking east. "That's the only thing left that I can do for her."

It wasn't a threat. It wasn't even angry, not the way anger usually is. It was the calmest thing I'd ever heard him say, and that was what frightened me — that the loudest person I had ever known had found, at the bottom of all that grief, a small cold quiet room, and had decided to live there.

I didn't tell him it was a bad idea. I didn't tell him anything. I just sat with him while the tide finished going out, two boys and a ruined cat on a sea wall at the edge of a country at war, and I let him have the plan, because it was the only thing holding him together and I had nothing better to offer, and because — this is the truest thing I know about myself — I have never once been able to take away the thing a person was leaning on, even when I could see, clear as the glow on the horizon, exactly where it was going to lead.

---

That was the end of the *before* — of melon soda on cold platforms and a dead 'K' that meant tradition, of a girl who was the gravity we all orbited, of three of us taking up the whole width of a pavement because we were fifteen and the city was ours and nothing had happened to us yet.

Everything after that night is a different story, and it's the one I actually have to tell you: the one with the factions in it, and the war under the war, and what the two of us turned out to be, and the day I finally understood what my voice could do and what his had already done. That story is hard, and it does not have many soft places in it, and I have been putting off the telling of it for pages now because once I start I have to go all the way through.

But the prologue ends here, on the flats at low tide, with the sea pulling out and my best friend holding on.

We were still, that night, on the same side of the water.

It's the last thing I'm sure of.
