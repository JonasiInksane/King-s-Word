# Chapter 8 — Say It Clearer

Jun could sit up without crying when he breathed. That was the bar Hanae's aunt had set for *better*, and the boy cleared it on the third try while Kaito stood in the shrine doorway holding a cloth-wrapped rice ball like it might detonate.

"You're okay," Kaito said. Flat. Careful. Each word tested for traps before release.

The boy — Jun, I learned that morning, late and useless — nodded at his own hands. "Thanks. For trying."

Kaito's jaw worked. He set the rice ball down gentle as a confession and left before anyone could thank him again.

I found him on the shrine steps with his elbows on his knees, whispering at the air. *You. With the — no. You in the gray. Drop — drop the — who. What. No spare words.* The thin seam in his voice again. Not roof-damage this time. Rehearsal.

Mira arrived the way weather finds low ground. She didn't sit. She watched him shape invisible sentences.

"Clear beats loud," she said. Mild. Exact. "You learned that on the pier. Loud hit everyone. Clear hits the one you name."

"He still got hurt," I said.

"He got hurt less than a truck," Mira answered, not looking at me. "Next time, finish the sentence. Don't leave a clause lying around for the world to trip on."

Kaito nodded like a student. I hated that more than the blood on the crate.

---

The tip came after dark.

Mira's people kept name-crates in a shed behind the dried-fish shop — stamps, lists, paper that could get you onto a westbound boat or into a hole depending on who read it. Someone local had sold the location. Two FDC soldiers and a civilian in a jacket who knew which door stuck.

Mira's runner — girl about our age, split lip, satchel — sprinted into Sugi's lane whispering *shed, now, they're in the alley*, and Kaito was on his feet before she finished.

"You don't have to come," he told me.

"I know," I said, and went anyway.

---

We heard the gun before we saw it.

Not a shot — the click of a slide, the ugly mechanical certainty of a sidearm coming up. The alley behind the shed was narrow, wet with fish-wash and moonlight. The runner was cornered against the crate wall. One soldier had the gun on her chest. The other blocked the mouth of the alley. The tipster had already made himself scarce.

Kaito stepped into the open like he'd been practicing for this exact frame.

I saw him choose. On the pier it had been heat and the roof bleeding through. Here he inhaled, and I watched the sentence assemble — who, what, no spare words — and when he spoke it was not a scream. It was built.

"You with the gun," Kaito said. Clear. Aimed. "Drop the gun and step back three steps."

The soldier's fingers opened.

The gun fell.

It hit wet stone, bounced once, and the soldier's body obeyed the second clause — three steps back, neat as drill — straight into his partner's shoulder. The partner's rifle jerked. A shot went wild, a white crack that tore the alley in half.

The runner should have dived. I saw her start to. Then Kaito's mouth moved again — half a word, sharp, leftover panic, *"Don't—"* — unfinished, the clause Mira had warned him not to leave lying around, and the girl's whole body locked mid-crouch like someone had driven a nail through her heels.

The ricochet or the second man's correction — I still don't know which — took her through the upper arm. She hit the crates hard. Blood, immediate and bright. Not fatal. Ugly. The kind of wound that would have been a scrape if a voice had not entered the math again.

The first soldier stood three steps back with empty hands, blinking at the gun on the ground like it had betrayed him. The second swore and swung the rifle toward Kaito.

Mira was already between them, palms out, talking the flat language of stamps and mistakes and *this one's under municipal seal*, buying seconds with lies. Sugi would have been proud of the technique. I was busy watching my best friend's nose start to bleed while he stared at the dropped gun with something like love and at the girl's arm with something like grief and could not seem to decide which feeling got to keep his face.

---

They extracted. Mira's word for it. Paperwork and shouting and a crowd spilling from the shop because gunshots in Nagihama still mean something. The soldiers left with nothing in the crates that mattered — Mira's people had a false stack for exactly this — and the runner, Sato, bit a rag while a widow packed her arm and called her brave and stupid in the same breath.

Behind the shed, in the fish-smell dark, the three of us stood in a triangle that used to be two.

"That one worked," Mira said. No smile. Precision. "Gun down. Three steps. Clean. The cost was the second clause you didn't finish — the *don't*. You left it half-made and it held her still for the bullet." She tilted her head at Kaito. "You see it now. Clear gets you the thing you asked for. Unfinished gets you the blood. So you finish. Every time."

"Stop talking to him like he's a tool," I said.

"He isn't a tool," Mira said, still mild. "He's the first person on this coast who can make a soldier open his hand. That makes him responsible. Responsibility looks a lot like practice from the outside."

"Haru." Kaito's voice was thin, scraped. He wiped blood with his wrist and missed. "You saw it. He dropped it. He *stepped back*. Because I said so. Not luck. Not the pier going wrong. I built the sentence and it — it did the thing." His eyes found mine, bright and awful. "It's me. When I say it. Isn't it."

Not a question he wanted undone. A confirmation he already owned.

I could have said the name then. The Word. Kanzaki's radio. The whole ledger. I felt it rise in my throat like vomit.

What I said instead was the line I had been building since the net shed, the only clear sentence I had:

"If you keep doing this, I don't know how to stand next to you."

It landed. Soft boy grammar, aimed true.

Kaito flinched like I'd hit him. Then the coal came up under the flinch and held.

"I'm not asking you to stand next to it," he said. Quiet. "I'm asking you not to lie to my face and call it luck while girls get shot in alleys. You already told me I'm not crazy. So don't take it back now." He gestured at the dark where Sato had been carried. "It worked for a second. The gun hit the ground. If I'd finished the *don't* into something real — *don't shoot*, *don't move your hands* — she'd be clean. I can learn that. I have to." His voice broke on the next name and came back harder. "Ryn is why. I'm not losing anyone else because I was too scared to say the next word."

He didn't say *save*. He never had, not honestly. On the roof a soldier had held her over the edge as leverage — wrist locked, thirty meters of city under her sneakers — and Kaito had screamed *let her go* thinking he was naming the grabber. The hand had opened. That was the whole tragedy. Leverage, not rescue. A command that hit the wrong grammar.

"You didn't save her," I said, before I could stop myself. "You told him to open his hand."

"I know what I did." His eyes didn't leave mine. "I'm not making that mistake twice. I'm finishing the sentences."

"You're going to lose me," I said, and heard how young it sounded, and hated it, and meant it anyway.

He looked at me a long time. The nick in his eyebrow. The blood under his nose. The boy from the Rail Loop and the boy from the roof occupying the same skin.

"Then stand further back," he said. Not cruel. Worse — practical. "But don't ask me to stop. I can't. Not if this is real. Not if careful means people live."

Mira said nothing. She didn't have to. The alley had already agreed with her.

---

We walked home under the same roof. Same lane. Same plum tree. Same cat on the shelf with its milky eye.

The air between us was wrong. Not silence — we had always had silence. This was a silence with a shape, like a third person walking between our shoulders.

At the bend of the lane I looked back. At the far end, where the shrine path met the dark, a plain coat stood watching. Still. Phone low at his side. The face that refused to stick.

Kaito didn't turn. He was still mouthing clauses under his breath — practice, not prayer.

I watched the coat until it wasn't there, and then I followed my best friend inside.

He kept at it in the dark after the lamp was out. Who. What. Finish it. I lay on my futon and listened to the shape of the silence between us and Mira's pull like a tide working at the stones, and I did not know how to stand next to the aiming in him — only that I was still under the same roof, still trying, and that trying already felt like standing further back.
