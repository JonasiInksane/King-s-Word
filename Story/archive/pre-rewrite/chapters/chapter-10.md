# Chapter 10 — The Other Side

Yellow tape where the plum tree met the stones. Men in gray-green carrying things away under tarps. A loudspeaker voice explaining, with unbearable manners, that an *isolated weapons discharge* and a *secondary medical event* had concluded, that residents should remain calm, that the Federation Defense Command thanked Nagihama for its cooperation.

They washed the stones until the water ran clear and the smell of iron went under the smell of fish-wash, which is how towns pretend a day can be restarted.

I sat on Sugi's kitchen floor longer than a person should. The cat watched me from the shelf with its one milky eye. Kaito's jacket hook was empty. His futon stayed rolled. The apology he had left in the air — soft, useless, not a command — kept replaying at a volume I could not turn down.

Sugi set a bowl beside my knee and did not tell me to eat. She had used up her imperatives for the day on the one that kept me from following him into the gap between the fish shop and the shrine path. I flinched when she said my name. She noticed. She said nothing about that either.

---

Hanae found the house before full dark.

I had never seen her. Only heard her name attached to Jun like a label on a crate — *Hanae's brother* — the way refugees get filed by the living person the war has not taken yet. She was older than him by a handful of years, dust in her hair, hands already shaking before she spoke. The widow-aunt was behind her, eyes emptied out.

"They said he ran," Hanae said. Not to me. To the doorway. To anyone who would make the sentence false. "They said he disobeyed. They said there was an accident with a soldier after."

Sugi brought her in. Fed her. The language of bowls when the other ones fail.

I stood there with the true sentence locked behind my teeth — *he looked at Kaito like he trusted him, and Kaito said stop until his voice broke, and stop would not stop, and then* — and I gave her none of it. What would I have been giving her. A boy with a magic mouth. A girl who watches and files. A town that washed the stones.

"I'm sorry," I said, and hated how small it was, how it was the same soft useless shape Kaito had left me with.

Hanae's face did something complicated. "Were you there."

"Yes."

"Did he suffer."

I thought of papers still half in his hand. Of the trust-look. Of how fast the lane had changed.

"It was quick," I said, which was a kindness and a lie in the same breath, the kind I am built for.

She nodded once, as if quick were a coin she could spend. Then she left with the aunt, and the house was quiet again, and I understood that Jun had stopped being a plot beat in my ledger and become a hole in someone else's kitchen, and that Kaito's voice had been in the math whether it had fired the rifle or only failed to stop it.

---

Sugi waited until the lamp was lit.

"You will not tell the gray-green what you saw," she said. Not quite an order. Close enough that my shoulders went up. She softened it by looking at the empty hook. "And you will not make a shrine of that boy in your head. He did a terrible thing after a terrible thing. Both can be true."

"He was trying to stop the rifle," I said. "Not — he wasn't trying to save anyone. He was trying to make a soldier put it down."

"He was trying to make the world obey him," Sugi said. Flat. A war widow's grammar. "I have watched men do that with guns for forty years. A mouth is not cleaner." She refilled my tea without asking. "If you stay, I will keep you. I kept my husband's house through one war. I can keep a soft boy through the paperwork. If you go after him—" Her eyes flicked to the door, to the inland dark. "You may not come back the same. Or at all. I am too old to pretend that is romance."

"I'm not going for romance."

"I know what you're going for," she said. "I've seen that look on women who followed soldiers east. It never ends in the kitchen."

I almost told her about the radio on the roof. *It's the Word.* I almost showed her the pale mark behind my ear and asked if she had ever seen its twin on him. I did neither. The ledger stayed mine. Saying it out loud in this house would have made her a target, and Sugi had already spent enough of herself on two boys and a dead girl who used to fall out of the plum tree.

---

They came for questions after midnight.

Two soldiers, not the mint-and-boredom one — he was a tarp now, a *secondary medical event*. These were clipboard men with eyes that had learned to look for witnesses. They stood in Sugi's doorway and asked for the young man who had been seen near the plum tree with the other young man who had fled west.

Sugi's stamp voice came out of her like a different person — municipal seals, evacuation registry, a sick grandson, a fever, a lie so practiced it smelled like truth. Sato appeared at the lane bend with her arm in a sling and a sheaf of papers that should not have existed, Mira's leftover machinery still running without Mira. Between the widow and the runner they made me into someone too ordinary to load into a truck.

One soldier looked at me too long anyway. I felt the barrel-memory under my jaw from a different day and kept my mouth shut so hard my teeth ached. No imperatives. No soft de-escalation. I had learned, late, that my kindness had a shape that could be dangerous too, and I did not trust it in a doorway full of gray-green.

When they left, Sato caught my sleeve in the dark beyond the gate.

"They went inland before night," she said. No recruitment. No sermon. A direction, cut down to the size of a sentence. "West road up the ridge. Toward Kuroishi. That's all I have. I don't work for her anymore. I just don't want another Jun." Her eyes were bright. Angry. Afraid. "If you're going to follow, go before they seal the coast road. And don't ask me to come."

"I won't."

She almost smiled. It died. "Your friend. The loud one. Tell him—" She stopped. Shook her head. "Don't tell him anything from me. Just stop him if you can."

Then she was gone, and the lane held only the yellow tape and the washed stones and, at the far bend where the shrine path met the dark, a plain coat standing still with a phone low at its side.

I looked at him.

He looked at me.

He did not show the screen this time. He did not speak. He only watched, the way he had watched from the train and the sea wall and the lane after the alley, and then he turned into the dark as if he had confirmed a sum and found it correct.

I went back inside before I could invent a meaning for him that would let me sleep.

---

I did not sleep.

I lay on my futon and stared at the rolled empty one and let the ledger run until it burned. Roof — soldier's grip, leverage, not rescue. *Let her go.* Hand opening. Radio. *It's the Word.* Birthmark. Pier. Alley. Stop stop stop. Jun. *Not again.* Kill yourself. I'm sorry.

I whispered the name once, alone, into the pillow, the way you test a loose tooth — *kotodama*, *Word*, whatever the lieutenant had meant — and the dark did not answer, and I felt no cleaner for having shaped it.

What cleaned nothing and decided everything was simpler:

Mira was going to write his next sentences for him.

If I stayed, I would become Sugi's soft boy in a kitchen while the loudest person I had ever loved learned how to end the world one clear imperative at a time.

I could not stop him from here.

I might not stop him out there.

But I would not let her be the only one standing at his shoulder.

---

Dawn came gray. The eastern bruise still glowed. Nagihama held its breath around a death it had agreed to misname.

I left the cat on the shelf.

Sugi saw me at the door with my bag and did not try another *no*. She put onigiri in my hands, pressed hard enough to hurt, and said, "Come back if you can. Come back *as yourself* if you can. That is all I ask."

"I'll try."

"Trying is what got that other boy killed," she said, and then she pulled me into a hug that smelled like pickled plum and charcoal, and for a second I was five years old in a house that still had a girl who fell out of trees, and then she let go.

I walked west.

Past the yellow tape. Past the plum tree. Past the washed stones. The ridge road lifted inland, away from salt, toward Kuroishi, toward whatever lists and hiding places Mira had promised him, toward a voice I was afraid to hear and more afraid to leave alone.

Behind me the cove stayed calm in the wrong way. Ahead, the road was only road.

I was not on his side of the water.

I was moving.
