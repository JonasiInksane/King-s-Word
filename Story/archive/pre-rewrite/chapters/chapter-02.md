# Chapter 2 — Sixteen

I met Kaito under the dead 'K' at six with a cake box balanced on both hands like it was a sleeping animal.

Saturday gold had already dipped the west windows in fire — June humidity turning the air amber, the whole city looking tea-stained and almost soft. The eyebrow nick, the scraped knuckles, the jacket he never washed: he looked at me and said, "You look like a butler," and I said, "You look like you slept in a batting cage," and we were both right. Fringe in my eyes. His hair dried into chaos. Uniforms that had tried their best. Best evening of my life. Put that on the record before anything else.

We did it in the wrong order on purpose. Ramen first — six seats, a cook who never spoke, broth that tasted like someone's grandmother had been simmering it since before the war. Then the arcade, where Ryn — hair down, cowlick free, no apron, silver stud catching every game-flash — destroyed us both at the light-gun, then destroyed a stranger who challenged the high score, then bought him a drink from the machine to apologize, because that was Ryn: take your dignity, then worry about you. Claw machines. Kaito spent nine hundred yen failing at a hideous cat plush; I won it on the second try by accident and had to pretend I'd let him "warm the machine up." He threw an arm around my neck and called me a traitor and a hero in the same breath.

"You planned that," he accused. "You *let* me lose."

"I let you warm up the machine," I said. Let. Just a word. We threw it around all night — *let me try, let it go, let's go, let her win, no let HIM win* — the small loose imperatives of three people who trusted each other completely, language worn so smooth none of it had any weight at all.

The war app buzzed twice on the walk. Both times I turned it face-down without looking. On the Daigo screen the lying font said EASTERN LINE HOLDING, names scrolling underneath, and for the first evening in a long time I let them pass unread, because it was Ryn's birthday and I had decided — the way the whole city decided every morning — that nothing was wrong.

One moment I should not have forgotten.

On the Rail Loop up to the school, emptying evening car, a man by the far door who did not belong to the evening. Still in a way people aren't still — not waiting, not tired. Watching. Mid-thirties or forties; short unremarkable hair; a face designed to be forgotten; plain dark coat too warm for June. No phone, which alone marked him strange. When Kaito laughed too loud and the car softened around him the way rooms did, the still man did not soften. He looked at Kaito. Then at me. Then he took out a phone after all and turned the screen toward us for a second — not filming, just *showing* — text too far to read — and the doors opened at the hill station and we tumbled out laughing, and I forgot him completely.

I would give a great deal to have not forgotten about him.

---

The rooftop chain was already broken. We climbed into open air and the city was *there* — all of it at once — Rail Loop a glowing ring, Sakaba-dōri a smear of pink and gold, the river holding the sky upside down. Out east, past where the bright stopped, a low uneven orange along the horizon we all understood and none of us named. Height makes everything look like it isn't happening to anyone. That's the terrible part.

Cake on an upturned crate. Candles. Kaito produced a lighter from somewhere, because Kaito could always produce a lighter from somewhere. Small flames leaned in the warm wind. Ryn looked at us over sixteen little fires with an expression I'd never seen — unguarded, almost frightened by how much she was allowed to have. Stud, cowlick, amber in the candlelight. Fifteen and sixteen and forty at once.

"Make a wish," Kaito said.

"I know how birthdays work, idiot."

"Then *make one*." The command in it, the volume. She rolled her eyes, closed them. The wind held its breath.

"Don't tell us," I said, "or it won't—"

"I wish," Ryn said, eyes still shut, "that it stays exactly like this. All three of us. I wish nothing ever—"

She never finished it.

I've wondered whether, if she'd finished it complete, some part of the world already listening might have tried to grant it. I'll never know. Because that was the moment the sky over the eastern wards, thirty kilometers off, turned white.

Not orange. White. So total it printed the rooftop in hard black shadow — Ryn's face, Kaito's open mouth, sixteen flames suddenly pointless. No sound yet. Light first, then the breath where you think *what*, then *no*, and then the air itself gets an order and obeys: pressure through you rather than past you, water tanks rattling, every car alarm in the bowl going off at once until the Rail Loop broke into a scream of ten thousand horns.

Then the app in all three pockets made a sound I'd never heard — a third tone under the two, low, in the teeth. Every screen in the city went flat red at once, and on the red, in the font that had lied every morning of our lives, four words that this time were not lying:

**LINE BROKEN. RUN WEST.**

One heartbeat of freeze. Below, headlights already reversing west. On the eastern horizon the white faded and something rose into the space — not only smoke — a *shape*, vast and wrong and unhurried, the kind of thing the news called Anomalous Materiel and refugees called by older shorter words, the thing that had eaten Haiga and left gray dust on a frightened boy's shoes. Too far to have a size. It had a size anyway.

"Okay," Ryn breathed. Calm the way she went calm when a kid ran for the door — fear routed straight into motion. She blew out the candles in one breath, plunging us into the red of ten thousand screens. "Okay. Down the stairs. Grandma's stall is west, my brother said west is—" Voice caught on *brother*, then back. "West. We go. Now. Both of you, with me, *right now*—"

The rooftop door banged open.

Not students. Not police. Gray-green of the Federation Defense Command, fast practiced spread across the concrete like a net that had rehearsed this shape. At the front, unhurried where everything else had begun to hurry, a man stopped three steps out, lifted his wrist, and checked his watch — actually checked it, in the red light, with the city ending behind him — the way you'd check whether a train you were expecting was, at last, on time.

Tall, lean, early thirties. Black hair parted with military exactness. Clean-shaven. Pale precise face. Cool gray-brown eyes that did not blink enough. Not a crease wrong. Silver watch on the left wrist. A man who had never once been late, and never once been kind without paperwork.

He lowered his wrist. Eyes across the three of us, then stopped — the way the still man's eyes had stopped — on Kaito, then on me.

"There you are," said Lieutenant Kanzaki Reiji, in a voice with no edges on it at all. "We've been looking for you two for a very long time."

Kaito stepped forward — of course he did — half a head taller, hands loose, chin up, nick white in the red light — putting himself between the soldiers and Ryn and me, breath already pulling in for something loud, something reckless, some command he didn't know was a command.

The net closed faster than the sentence.

One of them didn't go for Kaito. He went for the girl with the cake crumbs on her sleeve — wrist, collar, a practiced haul toward the low unguarded lip where we'd stood admiring the view a minute ago — and Ryn made a sound that wasn't quite a word as her sneakers left the concrete, as the city bowl tilted under her, as gray-green muscle held her out over thirty meters of birthday light like a receipt.

Kaito's shout started and didn't finish.

I turned, and she wasn't beside me anymore.
