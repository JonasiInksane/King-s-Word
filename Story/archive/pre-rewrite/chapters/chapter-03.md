# Chapter 3 — Let Her Go

"Both of you. You come quiet, or I drop her. That's the whole conversation."

That was the first sentence that landed. Not the gunlights. Not Kanzaki's watch. The mint-and-boredom voice of a man standing at the low lip of the roof, boots planted, arm locked straight, holding Ryn out over the lit-up bowl of the city like a receipt he was willing to tear.

One of Kanzaki's men had taken her — wrist, collar, practiced haul — while Kaito was still stepping forward to put himself between gray-green and the rest of us, and now her sneakers hung in thirty meters of birthday air and her free hand was clamped around the soldier's forearm because there was nowhere else to put it. He was upright the whole time — weight back, shoulders set, the other hand fisted in the fabric at her collar so the leverage stayed his. Cords in his neck. Face blank with work: the work of making two boys stop moving. The message was the drop.

Gunlights cut the birthday candles into white stubs. Someone shouted *down*; someone else shouted a number; the J-Alert in three pockets screamed under the rotor-wash of whatever was unmaking the east. Far below, the Daigo screen stuttered FRONT STABLE into static. I was already at the lip. My legs had gotten there before the part of me that still thought tonight was a cake and sixteen candles.

"Haru," Ryn said. Very quietly. Very reasonably. Like she was about to tell me where the spare key was. "Don't. Don't do anything stupid. He's—"

"Hands where I can see them," the soldier repeated, louder, for the whole roof, the particular calm of a man who had done this to other people's children in other towns. "I said *quiet*."

He had her. Hold onto that, because afterward people sand it softer. Federation gray-green, one of the men who'd kicked the chained door and spilled across the concrete like a net, using a sixteen-year-old girl's weight as punctuation. Not hauling her up. Holding her *out*.

Ryn's eyes flicked past me — to Kaito — then back. "Okay," she breathed, mostly to him, mostly to the air. "Okay. Just — don't—"

Kaito hit the edge beside me.

I felt him more than I saw him — the heat of him, the way the air always seemed to lean toward wherever he was. A gray-green reached for his collar; Kaito put an elbow into the man's throat without looking and kept moving, the way he stepped between me and third-years, the way he always moved when the question was simple. He looked down and he saw exactly what was there: a soldier in Federation gray-green with his hands on the girl Kaito loved, dangling her over the city like a lever, threat already spoken. Kaito had spent his whole life reading the world through *who is hurting the people I love*. The answer did not need a bad angle. Uniform. Grip. Drop waiting in the soldier's mouth.

He didn't think. Thinking was my job; feeling and moving was his. He threw himself half over the ledge with one hand fisted in the back of my shirt for an anchor he didn't ask permission for — my ribs hit concrete; my breath left — and he put every raw terrified furious ounce of himself into his voice, into the biggest loudest most absolute thing his body had ever produced, aimed straight down at the man holding his best friend at the edge of the world—

"**LET HER GO!**"

I have heard those three words my whole life. *Let it go, Haru. Let her win. Let me try. Let her go, man, she doesn't want to talk to you.* Nothing words. Soft idle grammar — end an argument, release a grudge, be kind. *Let her go.* Means *release your grip.* Means *stop holding on.* Means *be gentle.*

The soldier's hand opened.

It was not violent. That is the horror I have never been able to put down. Not a wrench or a throw. His fingers simply uncurled — all at once, evenly, the way your hand opens when you've decided you no longer need to hold the thing in it. His face didn't change. No triumph in it and no choice in it either; I have looked at that blank soldier's face ten thousand times in the dark and there was *nobody home*, no argument between the threat he'd made and the order going in his ear — just a command, and a hand obeying before the man could even be surprised that the drop he'd promised had arrived on someone else's schedule.

He let her go.

Ryn's free hand scraped concrete. Fingernails. A sound like chalk on stone. Her eyes were still on me. She didn't scream then either.

She looked at me. In the quarter-second she had, with the city lights streaming up past her and the wind taking her hair, she didn't look at the hand that had opened or the drop under her. She looked at me, and her mouth moved, and I have spent years deciding what it was shaped like, and the kindest lie I let myself believe on the worst nights is that it was shaped like *okay*.

Then the space where she'd been was just city lights.

---

I don't have the next part in order. I've tried. It won't hold a line.

I know I went for the lip. I know two sets of gray-green arms took me at the waist and the shoulders and I fought them — elbow, heel, the soft boy's stupid hands turned into something that wanted blood — and I got a glove across the mouth and a knee in the ribs and it accomplished nothing. I know I was screaming, because my throat was raw for three days and my mother, when she finally found me, kept touching my neck as if the damage were on the outside.

I know Kaito had gone completely silent, which was more frightening than any sound, because Kaito filled silence the way water fills a low place. They had him by both arms. He wasn't fighting. He was staring down. His mouth was open. No sound.

The soldier who had let her go was on his knees with his hands over his face, making a sound I hope never to hear a grown man make again — whatever had emptied out of him for that one second flooding back with the knowledge that the threat he'd used as a tool had become, under someone else's grammar, the thing itself. Another gray-green tried to pull him up; he wouldn't stand. His empty hand kept opening and closing on air, as if it were still looking for a wrist.

Gunlights swung. Someone fired into the sky — warning, punctuation. The birthday cake table went over. Candles hissed out on wet concrete. The dead 'K' of the karaoke tower across the ward flickered once like it was trying to finish a word.

And in the middle of it, in the roar and the red emergency light and the far-off unhurried shape still rising over the burning east, Lieutenant Kanzaki Reiji crouched calmly at the lip of the roof, looked down at the street for a long moment with his precise pale face, and then turned and looked — not at the soldier whose hand had opened, not at me — at *Kaito*.

He looked at Kaito the way a man looks at a reading on an instrument he has been waiting a very long time to see move.

"Confirmed," he said. Not to us. Into the little radio at his collar, in that voice with no edges. "It's the Word. Both of them, but the loud one is active. Repeat — the Word is *awake*." A pause. Kanzaki glanced at the kneeling soldier without sympathy. "One casualty. Civilian. Yes. It was — instructive." He stood. Brushed the grit from his knee. Checked his watch as if the night were behind schedule. "Bring them both. Alive is the whole point. If the loud one opens his mouth, you put your hands over your ears and you do not listen to a single word he says. That's an order I will only give once."

*Do not listen to a single word he says.*

I didn't understand it. Not then. Fifteen, Ryn gone over an edge, the east ending, and a man in uniform talking about Kaito's *mouth* like it was a weapon he'd been sent to collect.

But some animal underfloor of me put it away — next to a third-year walking out of a classroom a beat too early while Kaito wiped a pinprick of blood off his knuckle, next to a stranger on a train turning a phone screen toward us, next to *nothing is going to ruin it* and *the world obeyed* — and one day all those put-away things would stand up together and show me their faces.

That night I only knew they were reaching for Kaito, and he was still staring down over the edge with his mouth open and no sound, and his hands, when they took hold of him, were shaking so hard I could hear his knuckles knock together.

---

They almost got us on the roof. Then the near explosion took the east stairwell.

Shove in the air first — pressure, ears ringing, light the color of a wound where the municipal screen had been. Concrete teeth. Dust like a second night. Eleven of Kanzaki's certainties went with the stair, or so the later counts said; I only knew the net broke. Men stumbled. Gunlights swung wild. Someone yelled *cover*—

Kaito moved first. Not with volume. With the animal left when volume died. He tore one arm free, put his forehead into a gray-green's nose, and the grip on me loosened enough that I could bite and twist and run. West service door, wrong-shoulder-first. Stairs. Smoke. My mother's world — hospital soap and old pipes — waiting three floors down because twenty years of night shifts had drawn her maps in places Kanzaki's men didn't know yet.

Boots behind us. A shout. A shot that took plaster instead of spine. We fell more than ran into the ground-floor wreckage where a vending bank had collapsed into a gap just wide enough for two boys who still thought the city owed them a way out.

The war saved us from the men the war had sent. There's a joke in there I have never once found funny.

Under that collapsed vending bank, close enough to feel him breathe, I could tell from the wet catch that his nose had begun to bleed. In the dark his blood looked black. He wiped it on the back of his hand and stared at the smear like he'd never seen his own blood before.

"Haru," he whispered. First thing since. Voice wrong — cracked, thin, half missing, like a radio between stations. He would sound like that for a week; whatever he'd done with his mouth on that roof had taken a bill from his throat. "Haru. I told him to let her go. He was — he was going to drop her, he *said* he would, I just wanted him to stop, I said *let her go* and he—" He stopped. His whole body one long shudder. "Why did he *do* that. He had her. He was *holding* her. Why did he open his *hand*—"

And this is the part I have never forgiven myself for, out of a whole night made of things I have never forgiven myself for.

I didn't tell him it wasn't his fault.

I wanted to. The words were right there — easiest softest kindest grammar I owned. I opened my mouth to give it to him.

And I found I couldn't. Because I had seen the hand open. I had seen how *evenly* it opened. And some cold clear terrible part of me, the part that watched and never stopped keeping count, had already done the arithmetic and did not believe in coincidence, and would not let me lie to him with the conviction he needed. So what came out was nothing at all. I held onto him in the dark and said nothing, and he felt the nothing, the way he felt everything, and I think that was the first crack. Not the roof. This. The silence where his best friend's absolution was supposed to be.

He pulled back from me half an inch. In the dark I couldn't see his face. I've been grateful for that ever since.

The boots faded. The building groaned. Somewhere above us the city we'd grown up in was coming apart under a sky the color of a wound, and Ryn was sixteen years and one night old, and she was down there somewhere in all of it, and we were going to have to walk out through the place where she'd landed to get free, and we both knew it, and neither of us said it.

"Okay," Kaito said finally. Flat. Emptied. A voice I didn't recognize, all the volume gone out of it, the volume that had been the whole point of him. He wiped the blood again. "Okay. We go west. She said west."

He said it like a command.

And I hated, with my whole heart, that some small cowardly grateful part of me moved to obey.
