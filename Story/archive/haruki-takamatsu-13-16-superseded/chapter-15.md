# Chapter 15 — Eat First

The oden is hot enough that talking would be stupid anyway. Daikon first, then a fish cake that has been in the pot long enough to mean something. Riku finishes half his bowl before he looks at me like I am allowed to have a mouth again. The old man on the corner stool watches us over his one beer with the bored patience of someone who has taxed every new face for years.

I start once. "We're looking—"

Mama — I do not know her name yet — sets her wipe cloth down hard enough that the counter answers. "Eat first. I said it once. Boys who ignore me get the door and a cold street."

Riku kicks my boot again under the counter, harder than last time. I shut up and continue eating. Somewhere behind the noren a radio murmurs war feed at a volume meant for people who already know how the day is going. A pair of work boots scrape the mat, think better of it, and leave. Mama does not look up for them. She looks at our bowls until both are more empty than full.

"Now," she says. "Who are you chasing, and why should I care."

"A Tall boy about my age with a scraped voice. There's a girl with him, dark hair, maybe a sack. Someone on the arcade said she asked for Minato this morning."

She looks at Riku. "He with you, or are you with him."

"Cousin," Riku says.

"Sure." She snorts once. "Loud one came through around noon. Girl paid. He looked like his throat had been through a grater. They ate standing up until I told them to sit. He didn't talk much. When he did, people near the door got quiet in a way I don't like naming."

"Quiet how."

"Like they were waiting for the air to choose a side." She tips the wipe cloth into a bucket. "I don't sell poetry. I sell broth. Are they still here — no. Girl asked about same-day work that doesn't care where you're from. I told her the North Market loading line takes hands until dark if you show up looking useful. They left that way. Two, three hours ago."

"Did he say where they were going after."

"He said almost nothing. She did the asking. She looked like she already had three exits planned and was only picking which one wouldn't get him written down." Mama jerks her chin at my empty bowl. "North Market. Past the shotengai bend. Don't run there with your mouth open. Gray-green likes the plaza end."

"Was he hurt."

"He was upright. That's the version I sell." She glances at Riku. "You know this place."

Riku stands. "Bowls were good."

"I charged you." She jerks her chin at the noren. "Go — after Gorō is done collecting. He gets one and then you leave."

---

The old man on the corner stool sets his beer down like it owes him rent.

"One new fact," he says, "before you boys leave my evening looking unfinished."

Riku glances at him. "We're not—"

"Everyone under thirty pays the tax in this bar," the old man says. "Name's Gorō. Drove trams in this town longer than you've been apologizing. Fact: North Market closes the casual line at sundown. After that it's registered crews only, and registered crews ask for paper. If your loud friend wanted same-day cash, he either got on before dusk or he's already inventing a second plan."

"That's two facts," Riku says.

"Interest." Gorō's laugh sounds like a gearbox. "Also: if the girl is smart, she won't let him stand in a doorway tasting metal where soldiers can hear. Minato hears things. Minato also forgets things, if you know how to behave."

"We know," I say.

"Then on your feet." He tips the beer an inch. "Use them before the line closes."

Mama points a chopstick at him without looking. "Enough, Gorō."

"I'm helping."

"You're collecting." She looks at me once more. "If you come back still upright, there's rice. If you come back with gray-green on your heels, there's the alley and you're not my problem."

Riku pushes the noren. I follow. Outside, the pine-shadow street is cooler. Neon from the arcade leaks pink at the turn. My knees remember the road more than the broth; the heat fades faster than I want it to.

"North Market before sundown," Riku says.

"Got it."

---

The arcade is louder on a full stomach. Fry oil. A kid yelling prices for phone charms. An elevated car screams overhead and the plastic stools under the awnings shiver like they are used to it. We cut past the takoyaki stand from the second door without stopping. Gray-green shows up near the plaza mouth — two of them, talking to a shopkeeper who is nodding too hard — and Riku's fingers find my sleeve. We take the early cross, same as the yards.

Past the bend the neon thins into loading noise: trucks, pallet jacks, a loudspeaker that only works on every third syllable. North Market smells like wet cardboard and diesel. The casual line is a strip of open asphalt beside a chain fence where men without clipboards wait to be pointed at crates. The sun is already low enough that the fence throws long teeth across the pavement.

I ask a loader with a red rag around his wrist because he is the first person who looks like he answers strangers for a living.

"Tall boy," I say. "Scraped voice. Girl with a sack. Came from the arcade side — maybe two hours ago asking for same-day work."

He keeps moving boxes. "Girl, yeah. Boy with a bad throat. They took a half-shift on the vegetable trucks. Foreman liked that she didn't argue about gloves."

"Are they still on."

"Shift ended early. Truck filled. Foreman cut the extras." He tips his chin deeper into the lot. "They asked about a second place that pays after dark. Someone said the depot side might still need hands. Or they made that up. I wasn't listening for a novel."

"Depot side which way."

"Follow the diesel until it smells like old trams."

Riku is already looking at the sky. Orange going flat. Not sundown yet, but close enough that Gorō's tax feels less like a joke.

"Depot side," Riku says. "If the trail dies there, we eat rice tomorrow and you stop looking like the road owes you a reunion before dark."

"It doesn't die there."

"It dies wherever it dies." He starts walking anyway, crooked-tooth almost-smile gone thin. "Day's not done. Move."
