# Chapter 16 — Depot Side

The diesel thickens until the arcade pink is only a rumor behind us.

Depot side is older than North Market and meaner about it. Tram barns with boarded windows. A yard of idle cars with numbers scraped half off. The air smells like hot metal and wet brakes even when nothing is moving. Floodlights are already on over the main gate, though the sky still has a strip of orange left.

Riku slows us before the gate light can make us into a clean silhouette.

"Ask soft," he says. "If anyone asks why you're here, you're looking for evening work. Not a reunion."

"I know."

"You say that." He tips his chin at my hands. I put them down. "If I pull your sleeve, you come. No speeches in the open."

A clipboard man at the gate looks us over like we are late for a job we never applied to.

"Casual's closed," he says. "Registered only after sundown."

"We're looking for two people," I say. "Tall boy, bad throat. Girl with a sack. Came from the vegetable trucks maybe an hour ago."

"Everyone came from somewhere." He taps the clipboard. "You got paper for evening."

"No."

"Then you're scenery." He points past the fence with the pen. "If they took the after-dark rumor, they went along the barn wall, not through my gate. Gate has ears. Wall has rats. Pick which one you want to be."

Riku is already moving along the fence line before I invent a second question. We follow the diesel until it smells like old trams — Gorō wasn't joking — and cut between a stack of ties and a barn with one working bulb over a side door.

Voices ahead. Not gray-green. Work voices. A pallet jack beeps like it hates its own wheels.

Then I see them.

Not close. Across the open strip between barns: tall, wrong posture from too many roads, dark hair beside him with a sack that looks heavier than it should. Mira's hand is on his elbow the way someone steers a person who has stopped listening to streets. Kaito's mouth moves once — I can't hear it — and the air near a loader goes oddly still for half a second, the way Mama described without wanting to name it.

"Kaito—"

Riku's hand finds my sleeve hard. Not a warning. A stop.

A floodlight sweeps. Gray-green at the main gate turn toward the sound of my voice like dogs hearing a pan drop. Mira's head snaps our way. For one clean breath I think she sees me — school roof, hallway, the part of my life that still had a timetable — and then she is pulling Kaito into the dark between the next barn and a parked crate truck. Kaito looks back once. Scraped, tired, alive. He doesn't call my name. Maybe he doesn't believe it. Maybe Mira doesn't give him the chance.

The crate truck's engine catches. Reverse lights. They are gone behind the bumper before I get my sleeve free.

"Move," Riku says.

We move — not after the truck, not into the floodlight, sideways into the tie stack until the gray-green decide the noise was a bird and go back to boring the gate man. My pulse is doing something stupid in my throat. The truck turns out of the yard toward the pine road, diesel and red tail lights, and then even that is only a sound.

I start after it on foot like an idiot. Riku blocks me with a forearm that has carried packages heavier than my plan.

"You'll lose them in two turns and gain a clipboard," he says. "Truck's faster than you are."

"That was him."

"I saw."

"We can still—"

"We can still get written down for shouting a name across a depot at dusk." He looks at the empty gap between barns, then at me. "They're alive. Girl's steering. They're not sleeping under your feet tonight. That's the fact."

"I'm not hungry."

"You will be after you stop shaking." He starts back toward the fence line the way we came. "Depot's done. We don't invent a third job in the dark with soldiers already bored."

I stand there long enough to hate him for being right. Then I follow.

---

On the walk back through North Market the casual line is gone. The chain fence throws longer teeth. A loader I don't recognize shrugs when I ask about a crate truck headed pine-road — half the trucks head pine-road; he doesn't collect faces.

Riku buys nothing. He just keeps me moving until the arcade pink comes back and the elevated line screams like it did this morning, when I still thought "hours behind" meant something I could close with legs.

"Minato," he says at the shotengai bend. "Rice. Then you sleep somewhere that isn't a depot rat wall."

"What about your package work."

"My package work can live without me for one night." He glances at my stance, clicks his tongue once, leaves the correction unspoken. "Don't make me regret that sentence."

---

Mama looks up when we push the noren. Gorō's beer is still the same height. She takes one look at my face and points at the counter without asking who we failed to catch.

"Upright," she says. "Barely. Sit."

Rice comes in two bowls, plain, steam honest. Pickles on the side like an argument she already won. I eat because my hands need a job that isn't reaching for a truck that already left.

"Depot side," Riku says, around a mouthful, because Mama's eyes are waiting. "Saw them. Girl pulled him into a crate truck. Pine road. He shouted. Gray-green looked interested. We left before the interest grew legs."

Mama snorts. "Of course he shouted."

"I—"

"You saw him," she says, flat. "That's more than noon sold you. Shouting is how you turn a near thing into a report. Next time you shut your mouth and move your feet."

Gorō lifts his beer a millimeter. "Pine road climbs toward the north hill wards. Old tram freight used to cut under there. If they're hunting night pay, they'll find a warehouse door that doesn't ask for paper until morning. Or they'll keep moving. Trucks don't owe you a schedule."

"One fact," Mama says.

"That was one. The rest is scenery." Gorō's gearbox laugh is quieter this time. "Boys who chase taillights sleep in my old barns and wake up with clipboard dreams. Don't."

I look at the rice. It doesn't look like a reunion. It looks like food.

"We go again tomorrow," I say.

Riku doesn't argue. He also doesn't promise. He finishes his bowl, sets the chopsticks down neat, and tips his chin at Mama.

"Room?" he asks.

"Floor in the back if you wash in the morning," she says. "Cash for the rice. His face already paid for the lecture."

I pay what I have left that isn't pride. Gorō watches like he is filing us under unfinished business. Outside, somewhere past the pine shadow, a truck I cannot see is carrying the only person in this town I came here to find.

Riku stretches once on the mat Mama kicks toward us behind the curtain and closes his eyes like a person who has decided the day is actually done.

"Tomorrow," I say, quiet enough that it might only be for me.

"Tomorrow you ask soft," Riku answers, already half gone. "Tonight you shut up and sleep."
