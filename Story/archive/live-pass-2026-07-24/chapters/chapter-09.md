# Chapter 9 — Inland

Dust takes the salt out of my clothes by the second turn in the road. Mira walks ahead like she already knows which fork matters. I match her pace.

My throat hurts when I swallow. When I talk, it comes out scraped.

"How far?" I ask.

"Kuroishi by dark if we keep moving," Mira says. "There's a room above a dried-goods shop. My aunt's friend. She won't ask good questions if we don't give her bad ones."

"What if she asks anyway?"

"Then I talk. You keep your mouth shut unless someone points a clipboard at you." She glances back. "Can you do that?"

"I don't know."

"Try."

At a roadside stand she buys two cups of tea and puts one in my hand.

"Drink. You look like you're going to tip over."

"I'm fine."

"You're the color of that tea. Drink."

I take a swallow. The tea is bitter and smoky, still too hot, and I'm halfway through the cup when a truck comes up the road behind us. Gray-green in the cab. Mira's hand finds my sleeve and pulls me a step toward the weeds without making it look like hiding. We wait there until the diesel fades and the road is empty again.

"You were lining up words," she says. "When it passed. Your mouth moved before you said anything."

"I wasn't going to—"

"I didn't ask if you were going to. I said I saw it." She starts walking again. "Come on. Dark doesn't wait."

---

Kuroishi is a ridge town with too many shutters. Long main street. Closed tool shops. A croquette cart that smells like oil. A black shrine gate at the far end. A few soldiers in gray-green stand at a bend with clipboards — not a real checkpoint, but enough that people cross the street before they reach them.

Mira's contact runs the dried-goods counter like the war is a rumor she refuses to stock. She looks at my face, looks at Mira, and points upstairs without smiling.

"One night," she says. "Maybe two. You wash the cups. You don't bring soldiers to my door."

"We won't," Mira says. "Orii-san—"

"Don't Orii-san me like we're friends because your aunt sent rice once." She flips the counter latch. "Up. Quiet. If anyone asks, you're cousins from the coast and you're bad at lying, so don't practice."

The room is a futon, a window on the alley, and a sink that drips. I sit on the floor with my back to the wall and wipe blood from under my nose that started somewhere on the last hill. Mira sets a rice ball beside me and sits across the room.

"You should sleep," she says.

"I keep seeing him."

"I know." She folds her hands in her lap. "I've seen it three times now — the pier, the alley, the road today when the truck passed. Every time it costs you more and you aim it better. I don't like where that's going."

"Are you scared of me?"

"I'm scared of what happens if you stay where they can put a face to a file." She looks at the window, then back at me. "I'm not asking you to be anything tonight. Just don't open that window if someone knocks."

I turn the rice ball over in my hands without eating it. The sink drips. Down in the street somebody drags a shutter closed for the night.

"Why are you doing this?" I ask. "You could be home. Your aunt's gate. Towels. Nobody writing your face into anything."

Mira is quiet long enough that I think she's going to pretend to be asleep. Then: "When the Federation came through my aunt's town, the people who helped got one day to decide. Help now or explain later. Most of them picked later." She smooths the blanket over her knees. "My aunt picked now. It cost her the shop. I grew up hearing which one she'd pick again."

"That's not an answer about you."

"It's the answer you're getting tonight." But she says it without any edge, and after a moment she adds, "You stood up at the pier for a boy you barely knew. Whatever else your mouth is, that part I understood. Sleep, Kaito."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow we see if Orii meant one night or two. Then we move before this town learns our faces."

---

Morning, Orii sends us for water because the upstairs pipe is dead. The well line behind the shrine is short. A man in a civil-defense armband is arguing with an old woman about papers. His voice is too loud for the hour. When he sees Mira he points at her sack.

"You. Bag check."

Mira hands it over. Towels. A spare shirt. Nothing worth a scene. He digs anyway, slow, like he wants someone to flinch.

"She's with me," I say.

He looks at me. "I didn't ask you."

Mira takes the sack back when he drops it. "We're done. Thank you."

He steps sideways into her path. Not a rifle. Just a man who likes being the bend in the road.

"I said bag check," he says. "I decide when you're done."

"There's nothing in it," Mira says. "You saw."

"Maybe I want to see again."

I should walk away. I should keep my mouth shut. I hear the plum. I hear *shut up*.

"Let her pass," I say. Quiet. Aimed. Finished.

He does. One step aside, eyes blank for a second, then irritated like he forgot why he was standing there. Mira walks through. I follow. My nose goes hot. I wipe it on my wrist before Orii's street can see.

In the alley Mira looks at my wrist, then at my face.

"That again," she says.

"I didn't know another way."

"I know." She adjusts the sack on her shoulder. "Next time, if you can, walk first. Talk second. Talking like that leaves a mark people notice — and this town already talks too much."

"Then teach me how to walk past men like that."

"Later," she says. "Right now we wash cups and we leave before Orii changes her mind about the second night."

Back upstairs she washes the cups like she promised. I sit by the window. Mira sets a clean cup beside me.

"We can't stay," she says. "Orii meant one night. FDC asks more questions inland when a port town has a mess, and your mouth just left a mark at the well."

"Where after this?"

"Ishibashi if the stamps hold. Then a bigger town — more doors, more people who don't ask where you're from. A spare futon won't be enough for long."

"People you know."

"People who owe my aunt favors," she says. "Same thing, if you're lucky." She nods at the cup. "Drink. Then we pack. There's a morning bus if we still have the fare — if not, we walk."

I drink the water she brought. It goes down easier than it should, and under the tiredness I find the thing I'm not going to tell her: at the well, when the man stepped aside with his eyes gone blank, part of me wasn't scared at all.

Part of me was glad it still works.
