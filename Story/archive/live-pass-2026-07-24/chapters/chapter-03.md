# Chapter 3 — Low Tide

We went west because she had said west, and because every road still open pointed that way.

Kōgetsu was coming apart behind us — jammed trains, people with suitcases that would not close, the J-Alert screaming the same four words from every pocket and pole. We walked until the sidewalks ran out, then walked the shoulder of the coast road with ash in our socks. The hideous arcade cat was jammed under my arm. I do not remember deciding to keep it. It had been in Kaito's jacket after the claw machines. At some point it became mine to carry.

Kaito barely spoke. When he did, his voice sounded scraped raw. Once, under a vending machine light, he bent to drink and his hair fell aside. I saw a pale mark behind his left ear — the same shape as the one behind mine that I had always told myself was nothing. I did not say anything. I put my fingers against my own ear like I was scratching a bite, confirmed what I already knew, and kept walking.

We did not talk about the roof. We walked.

The branch-line train that brought us over the last hills — morning by then, after a night of walking — was two cars and half full of refugees who were not speaking either. Then the plum tree. Then Sugi's door. Then the cat still under my arm. And we were here.

---

Sugi hasn't looked away once while I talked.

"So," she says. "That's how."

"That's how," I say.

Kaito is in the other room on a futon that still smells like sun. His breathing is too quiet. Sugi pours more tea without asking if I want it.

"You told it straight," she says. "Most people dress it up."

"I don't know how to dress that up."

"Good. Don't learn." She pushes the cup closer. "Tomorrow we do what widows do. Tonight you eat, and you sleep, and you do not apologize to my floor for existing."

"I wasn't going to—"

"You were. I can hear it from here." She glances toward the other room. "He eats too, when he wakes. Even if he argues. Especially if he argues."

---

The girl from down the lane shows up the next afternoon with a bag of rice and a stack of clean towels.

"Saeki Mira," she says, and bows like we are ordinary guests and this is an ordinary evacuation. Seventeen, dark hair cut blunt at the jaw, an easy smile that does not ask for anything. "Sugi-san helped my aunt last winter. I thought — towels. And you look like you haven't slept."

"We haven't," I say, before I can stop myself.

"Then these are late, but still useful." She sets the rice on the counter and holds a towel out to Kaito. He does not take it at first. She waits, then folds it into his hands anyway. "Well water runs clearer if you let the first bucket go. The shoes by the door — if those are all you have, my aunt has spares that might fit."

"You don't have to—" Kaito starts, voice scraped.

"I know I don't have to." She looks at Sugi. "Is the plum still dropping fruit on the roof in August? My aunt swears it does and she wants a basket before the birds get them."

"It does," Sugi says. "Birds are thieves. Same as boys."

Mira smiles like that is a normal joke in a normal house. She does not ask what happened in Kōgetsu. She does not look at Kaito like he is a victim. She looks at him like he is a boy who hasn't slept.

"Thank you," I say.

"Anytime." She means it enough that Sugi lets her stay for tea.

Kaito watches the steam rise off his cup and says almost nothing. Mira does not fill the silence with speeches. She talks about tide charts, a broken bicycle pump, and whether the shrine gym still has space for mats. When Kaito's cup sits untouched, she nudges it an inch toward him and says, "Drink before it goes cold. Cold tea is a crime in this lane."

He drinks. Not much. Enough.

When she leaves, Sugi watches the door and only says, "Good kid. Lane's full of them, if you look."

---

They hold the send-off at low tide, on the flats beyond the sea wall.

No body. There was never going to be a body. The widows from the shrine bring a square of cloth and a white flower that has already started to brown at the edges. Sugi sets the arcade cat on the cloth as if that is enough to stand for a girl who vaulted a counter for a crushed egg sandwich. Someone sings something low I do not know. The eastern horizon still holds that uneven glow.

I stand in the cold coming up through my shoes and cry without any sound. Sugi reaches back without looking and holds my wrist hard.

One of the widows says it gently, the old phrase, meaning nothing by it except kindness: "Now we let her go. Now we let her go home to the water."

Kaito flinches. I flinch too. Our bodies learned those exact words on a roof. Here they are dressed up as mercy at the edge of the sea.

The rite is simple. You wade to the tide line with the thing that held the grief, and you set it on the water, and you let the low tide carry it out.

I open my hands.

The cloth catches. The cat rides a little way, ridiculous and sacred, and the sea takes one corner of the square and begins to pull.

Kaito's fist closes on the other corner and does not open.

The tide pulls. He holds. His knuckles go white above the black water. There is nothing loud left in his face — only the refusal of a boy who cannot do the one thing the rite is asking.

"Kaito," I say.

He shakes his head once. Not at me. At the water.

"You don't have to finish it today," Sugi says from behind us, quiet enough that only we hear. "But you have to step back before the tide decides for you."

He still does not open his hand. She talks him one step back from the tide line — cloth and soaked cat still clenched in his fists, never given to the sea. I do not pry his fingers open. I can't.

On the sea wall above us, Mira stands with a few other neighbors who have come to watch without crowding. She isn't smiling. She has her arms folded against the wind. When we start back up, she only looks worried, the way any decent person would look worried for a boy who couldn't finish a goodbye.

---

Inside, Sugi sets the arcade cat back on the shelf by the window, birthday sticker still stuck to the plastic, damp and still here. Kaito sits against the wall with the wet cloth in his lap. I sit across from him.

"You can sleep in my room," Sugi says. "Or the futon. Pick one and stop staring at the floor like it owes you an answer."

"Futon," Kaito says. Scraped. Barely a word.

"Futon," I say.

Sugi nods once, and goes to put rice on. Outside, Nagihama keeps being calm. The plum tree moves in the salt wind. Down the lane, someone laughs at something that has nothing to do with us.
