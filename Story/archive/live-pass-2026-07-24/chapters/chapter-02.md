# Chapter 2 — Let Her Go

I met Kaito under the dead 'K' at six with Mom's cake box held in both hands.

He looked at me and said, "You look like a butler," and I said, "You look like you slept in a batting cage," and we were both right. My fringe was in my eyes. His hair had dried into a mess. We were both still in uniform because neither of us owned anything nicer that wasn't wrinkled.

"Where is she?" I asked.

"Konbini. Closing early. She said if we're late she'll start without us."

"She wouldn't."

"She would. That's why we're early."

We did the night in the wrong order on purpose. Ramen first, at the good place — six seats, a cook who never spoke, broth hot enough to make you stop talking for a minute. Ryn showed up with her hair down and no apron, silver stud catching the kitchen light, and stole half of Kaito's chashu before he could pretend to defend it.

"It's my birthday," she said. "Theft is legal until midnight."

"That is not how law works," I said.

"It is tonight."

Then the arcade. She destroyed both of us at the light-gun, then destroyed a stranger who challenged the high score, then bought him a drink from the machine to apologize. That was Ryn: take your dignity, then check if you were okay.

Kaito spent nine hundred yen failing at a hideous cat plush. I won it on the second try by accident and had to pretend I'd only been warming the machine up for him. He threw an arm around my neck and called me a traitor and a hero in the same breath.

"You planned that," he said. "You *let* me lose."

"I let you warm up the machine," I said.

"Let me try again."

"Let it go," Ryn said, already walking toward the next cabinet. "We've got a roof to climb and Haru's holding a cake like it might escape."

We threw *let* around all night after that — *let me try, let it go, let's go, let her win* — small ordinary words, worn smooth, with no weight on them at all.

The war app buzzed twice on the walk toward the Rail Loop. Both times I turned it face-down without looking. On the Daigo screen the font said EASTERN LINE HOLDING, and under it the ribbon of names scrolled by. For once I let them pass unread. It was Ryn's birthday. I had decided nothing was allowed to be wrong.

---

On the Rail Loop up to the school, the evening car was emptying. There was a man by the far door who did not belong to the evening.

He was not waiting the way other people waited. He was watching. Mid-thirties or forties; short unremarkable hair; a face built to be forgotten; a plain dark coat too warm for June. He did not look at his phone. When Kaito laughed too loud at something Ryn said, and the rest of the car softened around him the way rooms did, the still man did not soften. He looked at Kaito. Then at me. Then he took out a phone after all and turned the screen toward us for a second — not filming, just showing, text too far to read. The doors opened at the hill station. We tumbled out laughing into the warm dark, and I forgot him completely.

I would give a great deal to have not forgotten about him.

---

The rooftop chain was already broken. We climbed out into open air. The Rail Loop glowed in a ring below us. Sakaba-dōri was a smear of pink and gold. Out east, past where the bright stopped, a low uneven orange sat on the horizon. We all understood what it was. None of us named it.

I set the cake on an upturned crate and stuck in the candles. Kaito produced a lighter from somewhere, because Kaito could always produce a lighter from somewhere. The flames leaned in the warm wind. Ryn looked at us over sixteen little fires with an expression I had never seen on her — unguarded, almost nervous about how much she was allowed to have.

"Make a wish," Kaito said.

"I know how birthdays work, idiot."

"Then make one before Haru's fringe catches fire."

"Don't tell us," I said, "or it won't—"

"I wish," Ryn said, eyes still shut, "that it stays exactly like this. All three of us. I wish nothing ever—"

She never finished it.

That was the moment the sky over the eastern wards, thirty kilometers off, turned white.

Not orange. White. Hard enough that it printed the rooftop in black shadow — Ryn's face, Kaito's open mouth, sixteen flames suddenly useless. No sound yet. Light first. Then the held breath where you think *what*, then *no*. Then the air shoved through us rather than past us. Water tanks rattled. Every car alarm in the city went off at once until the Rail Loop turned into a scream of horns.

Then the app in all three pockets made a sound I had never heard — a third tone under the two, low, in the teeth. Every screen in the city went flat red at once, and on the red, in the font that had lied every morning of our lives, four words that this time were not lying:

**LINE BROKEN. RUN WEST.**

Below us, headlights were already reversing west. On the eastern horizon the white faded and something rose into the space — not only smoke — a shape, vast and wrong and unhurried, the kind of thing the news called Anomalous Materiel and refugees called by older shorter words. The thing that left gray dust on a frightened boy's shoes.

"Okay," Ryn breathed. Calm the way she went calm when a kid ran for the konbini door — fear turned straight into motion. She blew out the candles in one breath. "Okay. Down the stairs. Grandma's stall is west — my brother said west is—" Her voice caught on *brother*, then came back. "West. We go. Now. Both of you, with me—"

The rooftop door banged open.

We had started to turn toward it — west, stairs, Ryn's hand already finding my sleeve — and then the doorway was full of gray-green. Federation Defense Command. They came through in a practiced spread and cut the roof into pieces before anyone had finished a step. Gunlights found the crate, the cake, our faces.

At the front of them, while the city screamed red below, a man stopped three paces onto the concrete and checked his watch. Tall, lean, early thirties, black hair parted exact, uniform without a crease, silver watch on the left wrist. Cool eyes that did not blink enough.

He lowered his wrist and looked at Kaito. Then at me.

"There you are," said Lieutenant Kanzaki Reiji, in a voice with no edges. "We've been looking for you two for a very long time."

"On the ground," someone else shouted. "Hands where I can see them. Now."

Kaito didn't go to the ground. He never did. One step toward the door, hands open but not up, chin lifted, putting his shoulders between us and the rifles.

"Hey," he said — too loud for rifles, the same volume he'd used on third-years his whole life. "We're kids. We're leaving. East is burning, in case you missed the sky—"

"Last time," Kanzaki said, mild as a timetable. "On the ground. Both of you. The girl too."

Ryn's fingers tightened on my sleeve. "Haru. Service door. West—"

We ran.

Not a plan. Just the word *west* and the red screens. Two steps. Three. A soldier got a hand on my jacket and I tore out of it. Kaito hit someone — I heard the grunt — and kept moving toward the west-side door that wasn't the one they'd come through.

That was when they stopped asking.

A gray-green peeled off the line, cut behind the crate, and took Ryn clean off her feet by the wrist. She shouted once — anger, not fear — and kicked him. He didn't argue with her. He walked her backward to the low lip where we'd blown out the candles, planted both boots on the edge, and held her out over the open air.

One fist in her collar. One hand locked around her wrist. His body on the roof. Hers not. Her sneakers found nothing. The city was a long way down.

"Enough," he called across the roof. Not shouting. Clear. "You two. Stop. Hands up. You come quiet, or I drop her."

I turned back toward her — five meters, maybe six, the crate between us and the lip — and that was as far as I got. Arms locked around my chest from behind. A knee in the back of my leg. Concrete came up fast. I hit it face-first and tasted blood.

Kaito made it farther. Two more steps toward the lip before they took him down — one on each arm, a third across his shoulders — and he fought like the ground was optional, thrashing toward the edge on his knees while they dragged him the other way.

"Haru," Ryn said, from out over the lights. Quiet. Reasonable. Like she was telling me where we kept the spare key. "Don't—"

Kaito's mouth opened.

He couldn't reach her. He couldn't reach the soldier. There was half a roof of gray-green between him and the lip, and they were still hauling him back, and he put everything he had into his voice anyway, loud enough that it cracked—

"**LET HER GO!**"

I had heard those three words my whole life. *Let it go, Haru. Let her win. Let me try.* Soft idle grammar. End an argument. Be kind.

The soldier's hand opened.

Not a wrench. Not a throw. His fingers simply uncurled — all at once, evenly — before the man could even look surprised. Nobody home in his face. Just a command going in one ear and a hand obeying.

He let her go.

Ryn's free hand scraped concrete. Fingernails. A sound like chalk on stone. Her eyes were still on me. She didn't scream.

She looked at me, and her mouth moved, and even then I was already trying to decide what it was shaped like. The kindest lie I reached for was that it was shaped like *okay*.

Then the space where she'd been was just city lights.

---

I don't have the next part in order. I've tried. It won't hold a line.

I know I reached for her after the hand opened. I know two sets of gray-green arms dragged me back from the lip and I fought them and got a glove across the mouth and a knee in the ribs and it accomplished nothing. I know I was screaming, because my throat was raw for three days afterward.

I know Kaito had gone completely silent. They had him by both arms. He wasn't fighting. His mouth was open. No sound.

Gunlights swung. Someone fired into the sky. The birthday cake crate went over. Candles hissed out on wet concrete.

In the middle of it Lieutenant Kanzaki Reiji crouched calmly at the lip, looked down at the street for a long moment, and then turned and looked — not at the soldier, not at me — at Kaito.

"Confirmed," he said into the radio at his collar. "It's the Word. Both of them, but the loud one is active. Repeat — the Word is awake." A pause. He glanced at the kneeling soldier without sympathy. "One casualty. Civilian. Yes. It was — instructive." He stood. Brushed grit from his knee. Checked his watch. "Bring them both. Alive is the whole point. If the loud one opens his mouth, you put your hands over your ears and you do not listen to a single word he says. That's an order I will only give once."

I didn't understand it. Not then. Fifteen years old, Ryn gone over an edge, the east ending, and a man in uniform talking about Kaito's mouth like it was something he'd been sent to collect.

That night I only knew they were reaching for Kaito, and his hands, when they took hold of him, were shaking so hard I could hear his knuckles knock together.

---

They almost got us on the roof. Then the near explosion took the east stairwell.

Pressure first — ears ringing, light where the municipal screen had been. Concrete broke. Dust filled everything. Men stumbled. Gunlights swung wild.

Kaito moved first. He tore one arm free, put his forehead into a gray-green's nose, and the grip on me loosened enough that I could twist and run. West service door, wrong shoulder first. Stairs. Smoke. My mother's hospital maps — twenty years of night shifts — waiting three floors down in corridors Kanzaki's men did not know yet.

Boots behind us. A shout. A shot that took plaster instead of spine. We fell more than ran into the ground-floor wreckage where a vending bank had collapsed into a gap just wide enough for two boys.

Under that wreckage, close enough to feel him breathe, I could tell from the wet catch that his nose had begun to bleed. In the dark his blood looked black. He wiped it on the back of his hand and stared at the smear.

"Haru," he whispered. First thing since. Voice wrong — cracked, thin, half missing. "Haru. I told him to let her go. He was going to drop her — he *said* he would — I just wanted him to stop, I said *let her go* and he—" He stopped. His whole body was shaking. "Why did he open his hand—"

I wanted to tell him it wasn't his fault. The words were right there. I opened my mouth.

Nothing came out. I had seen the hand open. I had seen how evenly it opened. I could not lie to him with the conviction he needed. So I held onto him in the dark and said nothing, and he felt the nothing.

He pulled back from me half an inch. In the dark I couldn't see his face. I was grateful for that.

"Okay," Kaito said finally. Flat. Emptied. All the volume gone out of it. He wiped the blood again. "Okay. We go west. She said west."
