# Chapter 15 — Loading Line

North Market smells like wet cardboard and diesel.

The casual line is a strip of open asphalt beside a chain fence. Men without lists wait to be pointed at crates. The sun is still high enough that Gorō's warning feels like someone else's problem. Mira talks to a foreman with a red rag around his wrist. I keep my mouth shut and try to look like I know which end of a box is up.

"Half-shift," the foreman says. "Vegetable trucks. Gloves on the hook. You argue about gloves, you're done."

"We won't," Mira says.

He looks at me anyway. "He speak?"

"When he needs to."

"Then he doesn't need to." The foreman jerks his chin at a stack of crates. "You. Tall one. Those go on the second truck. Girl counts. I don't want clever. I want done before the light turns."

I lift. The wood bites my hands through cheap gloves. Mira moves down the line marking numbers on a scrap of paper like she has done this in a different town with a different name. For a while the work is almost kind — heavy enough that my throat stops inventing sentences, loud enough that the neon of the arcade feels far away.

A man in a vest cuts into our aisle on purpose. Not gray-green. Yard private again, or the same breed. He puts a hand on the crate I am carrying like he is checking the weight for fun.

"You're in my row," he says.

"Foreman put us here," Mira says.

"Foreman isn't here." He smiles without warmth. "Move."

I feel the old shape start behind my teeth — *move* aimed the other way, clean, stupid. Mira's eyes find mine over the crate. She does not step on my boot this time. She just takes the crate out of my hands, sets it down, and walks us three stacks over without making it a fight.

"See," she says, quiet, once we are loading again. "Different through."

"That wasn't different. That was you."

"Same result." She passes me the next box. "Keep going."

---

The truck fills early. The foreman cuts the extras without apology and pays in cash that looks like it has been in a pocket since before the war learned new maps. Mira counts it once, then again, then splits it where I can see.

"Enough for rice," she says. "Not enough for another room that asks nothing."

"So we keep working."

"Casual line dies later. You heard the old man." She looks at the sky — orange starting to lean. "There's talk of evening hands on the depot side if the trucks keep coming. Or we go back to Minato and take the rice she offered and stop pretending we can outrun a whole city before dark."

"Depot," I say, before I can talk myself into a bowl and a mat.

Mira studies my face. "Because you want money, or because standing still makes your mouth itch."

"Both."

"Honest. Fine." She tucks the bills away. "Depot side. You load. I talk. If someone blocks you again, we walk. No shortcuts."

We leave the fence line while the registered crews start showing paper at the gate. Nobody asks our names. That is the whole mercy of a half-shift.

---

Central Arcade, same afternoon.

The charm-shop stairs smell like plastic batteries and old smoke. Riku lets me knock. The door opens on a chain this time — a strip of undershirt and one unfriendly eye.

"If this is about the pair from last night," the man says before I get a word out, "I already told the other one I don't keep guest books."

"What other one?"

The eye narrows. "Somebody official-shaped came up my stairs an hour ago asking who paid cash this week. I said nobody. Now you." He starts to close the gap. "I rent futons. I don't collect visitors."

"Wait — I'm not official anything. I'm looking for my friends. A boy and a girl, my age. If they stayed here, I just need to know when they left."

Maybe it's my face. Maybe it's Riku on the step below me, looking like furniture that bites. The chain stays on, but the door stops moving.

"Girl paid for one night," he says. "Loud one didn't sleep — I heard him walking till the trains stopped. They left before my kettle boiled, asking about work that pays same-day and doesn't care where you're from."

"Did they say where they were going?"

"I don't give tours. I rent." The chain rattles as he steps back. "Ask the takoyaki woman downstairs. The girl talked to her on the way out — I heard it through the floor. And don't come back up these stairs today. Two visits is a coincidence. Three is a report."

The door shuts.

"Come on," Riku says on the stairs. "He answered like a man who wants you off his step. And you heard the other thing."

"Someone official is asking about cash rooms."

"Someone official is always asking about something. The question is whether it's your loud friend they're circling or just the week's paperwork." He doesn't sound worried. He sounds like he's pricing it.

The takoyaki woman downstairs is turning the last batch of the morning oil. She sees me coming and points the pick at me before I can open my mouth.

"You're the third person today walking these stairs with questions instead of money," she says. "Buy something or keep moving."

I buy four takoyaki I can't really afford. Riku takes two of them like a tax.

"A girl asked me this morning where working boys eat without a speech about papers," she says, boxing them. "I told her Minato. Not the harbor — the bar. She looked like she already knew and only wanted someone else to say it. That's everything, and I only sold it because you paid."

"The third person," I say. "Who was the second?"

"A man who didn't buy anything." She hands me the box and looks at me with the first real attention she's spent. "Whoever your friends are, they should stop being interesting. This street doesn't keep secrets, it just charges for them."

On the street Riku clicks his tongue once. "Trail's still warm. We eat where they might've eaten. I know the place."

"You know Minato?"

"Everyone who carries packages in this town knows Minato." He starts walking. "The mama there decides fast and doesn't renegotiate. Let her feed you before you ask her anything, and don't tell her more than she asks for."

"I can eat."

"We'll see."

---

Minato is two turns off the bright strip, and I would have walked past it — the sign is small and the noren is the color of the shadow it hangs in. Riku ducks through like the doorway owes him. Inside it smells the way Sugi's kitchen smelled on good nights, and that lands harder than the walk did.

The woman behind the counter doesn't greet us. She looks at Riku like an old invoice, then at me longer, and her mouth tightens.

"Second pair," she says. "Trouble comes in shifts. Sit."

Two bowls of oden arrive before I understand we've been allowed to stay. Riku eats like the bowl might be reclaimed. I want to ask my questions first, and the look she gives me over the counter makes it clear that this is not that kind of counter. So I eat, and the broth is good enough to make me ashamed of hurrying it.

She takes my bowl when it's empty and doesn't give it back.

"Now," she says. "Who are you chasing, and why should I care."

"My friend. He came through with a girl — someone on the arcade said she asked for this place by name this morning." I keep my voice ordinary the way Riku taught me. "I've been walking from the coast for a week. I just need to know if I've finally caught up."

She looks at Riku. "He with you, or are you with him."

"Cousin," Riku says.

"Sure." She snorts once. "They ate around noon. The boy held his bowl like it was the first warm thing anyone had handed him in a week. I sent them to the North Market loading line — same-day hands until dark. You're late for the bowls. Not buried."

"Are they still there?"

"How would I know from here." She tips a wipe cloth into a bucket. "Past the shotengai bend. If the casual line already spat them out, they'll invent a second plan or they'll come back for rice. If they come back with soldiers on their heels, the alley's that way and they're not my problem. Same goes for you."

The old man lifts his beer a millimeter. "Casual line dies at sundown," he says, almost kindly. "After that it's paper. If your friends wanted cash without questions, they either got on early or they're already walking toward the depot side inventing evening work."

Mama points a chopstick at him. "He already collected once today."

"Interest," Gorō says, and his laugh sounds like a gearbox.

Riku stands. "Bowls were good."

"I charged you," Mama says. She looks at me once more. "You find them, you don't shout their names across a yard. This town listens for free."

Outside, the hill's shadow has grown while we ate. The orange is already climbing the shop fronts.

"North Market," Riku says. "Then depot if the line's dead. You ask ordinary. I watch hands."

"Got it."

"And soft boy." He isn't smiling now. "Three of us asked that street about your friend today, and one of the three wasn't buying takoyaki. If the line spits your friend out at sundown, he's loose in a city that's starting to look for him. Walk faster."
