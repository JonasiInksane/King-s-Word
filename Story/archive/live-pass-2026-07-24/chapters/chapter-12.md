# Chapter 12 — Under the Bridge

We hit Ishibashi before the light dies — not because I am fast, because Riku does not believe in stopping for blisters.

The town sits where the road dips to a nearly dry riverbed. The old stone bridge is real: worn arches, market stalls packed underneath like someone decided shade was more honest than storefronts.

"Boots first," Riku says.

"I need to ask about rooms—"

"Boots first. You ask for a room in dead shoes, they price you like charity. Ask in boots and they think you might walk off to the next door." He already turns toward a stall under the second arch. "Two minutes. Then you can apologize to landlords."

The stall sells boots that have already suffered — scuffed leather, mismatched laces, one pair still chalked with someone else's size. The woman running it looks at my school shoes the way dried-goods women look at bad inventory.

"Those won't make Takamatsu," she says.

"I'm not going to—" I start, then stop, because I might be. "How much."

She names a number. Riku names a worse one. They argue without raising voices, the way people argue when both of them have done this before. I end up with boots that hurt in new places and less money than I walked in with.

"They'll hurt for two days," Riku says, already walking. "Then they'll fit. Your old ones were going to hurt the whole way and then fall apart on a hill somewhere."

"You could have said that before I paid."

"You'd have argued." He tips his chin at the stalls under the bridge. "Go ask your loud-boy questions. I've got a drop to make. Meet me by the dry piling under the third arch when you're done crying over doors. If you're not there by full dark, I assume the shed men found you again and I'm not digging twice."

"What's the drop."

"Work." He clicks his tongue once. "Not yours."

He cuts into the market crowd and is gone between squid steam and a stack of plastic crates.

---

I start with the boarding rooms above the arch shops, because "rooms that don't ask questions" is what the rumor gave me.

The first door wants papers I don't have. The second wants a deposit I can't pay. The third is a woman with wet hands and a towel over her shoulder who looks at my face and sighs like I am late for a shift I never took.

"If you're selling something, the market's downstairs," she says.

"I'm looking for my cousin. He came through yesterday with a friend of ours — they'd have asked for a room for a night. He's been sick; his voice comes out rough."

She almost closes the door. Then she doesn't. "Yesterday evening, maybe. A boy with a sanded-down voice and a girl who counted the coins out twice before she handed them over. One night, then they left at first light."

"Which way."

"Inland. Toward the pine hills — Takamatsu." She glances past me at the bridge. "And don't stand on my step looking like that. I've already had one boy today asking if a mouth can make a bag check end early. I told him luck doesn't rent rooms here."

"What boy."

"Local. Armband. Talking too loud about a bag check that went strange." She shrugs. "Market eats stories. I rent futons. If you're chasing the loud one, you're a day behind and your boots are new, which means you're either stubborn or stupid."

"Both," I say, before I can stop myself.

That almost gets a smile. "Takamatsu road starts past the north cone line."

"Thank you."

"Goodbye." She says as she shuts the door.

---

Under the bridge the market is louder than Kuroishi ever was — stall radios, fry oil, someone arguing about squid weight like it is national policy. I ask two more people anyway.

The croquette man wants to sell before he talks. I buy one; it is mostly potato and burns my fingers, and it is the best thing I have eaten since Sugi's kitchen.

"Dawn," he says, once the coin is in his box. "Two kids buying tea off the cart next to me. The boy pointed at the fare board and the girl shook her head at whatever he said about it."

"Did you hear any of it?"

"I fry croquettes at dawn, kid, I don't take minutes." He flips one in the oil. "She had the money and he had opinions. That's every couple in this market."

The sock woman two stalls down is more useful and less friendly. They asked her about buses toward the pine town, she says, and walked off north when the fare board turned out to be a joke. She noticed the girl's sack was going empty at the bottom — the way sacks go when people have been eating out of them for days and not putting anything back.

"You feeding them when you catch up?" she asks, not kindly, not unkindly.

"I don't know yet," I say, because it is the first honest answer I've given anyone today.

No one has seen them since morning.

By the time I find the dry piling under the third arch, full dark is close and my new boots have started their real work. Riku is already there, sitting on a crate like he owns the riverbed, counting small bills with his thumb.

"You look like a door hit you," he says.

"They left at dawn toward Takamatsu. The pine hills."

"Then you're still late." He tucks the bills away. "Drop's done. I get paid either way. You get blisters either way. Fair."

"What was the drop."

"A package that wasn't guns and wasn't kids. That's all you get." He stands, dusts his hands. "Takamatsu's a real town. Neon. Yards. Soldiers in pockets instead of bends. If your loud friend and his girl are hunting rooms that don't ask questions, they'll find more of them there — and more people who sell the addresses."

"Are you going that way too?"

Riku clicks his tongue once. "My work goes that way for two days. After that I might peel off. You can walk with me till then if you keep up — and if I tell you to shut up, you shut up."

"I can do that."

"We'll see." He grins, crooked tooth and all, and starts up the bank toward the north road.
