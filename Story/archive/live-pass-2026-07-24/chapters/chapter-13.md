# Chapter 13 — Survive a Tuesday

Takamatsu hits like a real town before it hits like a place you can hide in.

Pine hills first. Then freight lots. Then an elevated line screaming brakes over wet neon that looks, for one stupid second, like Sakaba-dōri if Sakaba-dōri had survived. Mira cuts us off the main road before the yards open all the way and points at a chain-link gap like she has done this in a different city already.

"East Yards," she says. "We keep our heads down when we ask questions, and we stay off Station Plaza. Too many questions there."

"I wasn't going to the plaza."

"You were looking at the brightest lights." She adjusts the sack on her shoulder. "Bright is where soldiers like to stand. Stay with me."

My throat still feels sanded. Talking costs. Not talking also costs, because the words stack up behind my teeth anyway — *move*, *open*, *let us through* — every time a truck blocks a lane or a man with a vest looks at us too long. I keep my mouth shut the way Mira asked in Kuroishi. It works until it doesn't feel like working.

A loader chaining crates near the container stacks glances up when Mira asks how to reach Central Arcade without the plaza.

"Along the fence, then the shotengai back way," he says. "Don't cut through the plaza unless you like waiting in a line." He tips his chin at the neon smear. "Arcade side rooms take cash if you don't look like trouble. Station side asks for paper. Pick one."

"Arcade side," Mira says.

We walk. Gray-green shows up in a pair near a diesel pump — not a line, a pocket — and Mira's fingers find my sleeve. We cross early without making a show of it. The city smells like fry oil and wet cardboard. An elevated car rattles past with war-feed light in the windows. I wait for the old reflex: find Haru, make a joke, pretend the war is background noise. The reflex finds nothing.

Near a pallet jack a man in a vest steps into our path like he owns the aisle.

"Papers," he says. Not gray-green. Yard private. Still a clipboard.

Mira has the empty look ready. "We're cousins from the coast. We're looking for evening work."

"Evening work has a list." He taps the board. "You're not on it."

I feel the sentence form — *let us through* — clean as the well, stupid as the well. Mira's heel comes down on my boot hard enough to hurt.

"We'll try the arcade side," she says. "Sorry for the trouble."

He stares at my face a second longer than I like, then waves us off. We go. My nose stays dry. Mira does not praise that. She just keeps walking until the yards thin into shotengai noise.

"You're lining up words again," she says, quiet.

"I'm not saying them."

"I can still see your mouth get ready." She doesn't look at me while she says it. "You said you'd walk past men like that. Do it."

"I remember."

"Good. Because that one would have been louder than a bag check, and this town already has enough stories."

---

Central Arcade is louder than Kuroishi and softer than Kōgetsu ever admitted it was. Plastic stools under awnings. Phone-charm shops. A takoyaki stand burning oil that has outlived better ideas. Mira buys two canned coffees from a cooler woman without asking if I want one and puts one in my hand.

"Drink. You look like a hinge."

"I'm fine."

"You're the color of the asphalt. Drink."

I drink. Too sweet. Better than ditch water. Mira asks the cooler woman about rooms that take cash without papers.

The woman eyes my jacket, then Mira's sack. "Station Plaza asks more questions than the arcade side. Shotengai rooms above the charm shops are cheaper and meaner. Boy doesn't talk much, does he."

"Sore throat," Mira says.

"Sure." The woman takes the coins. "Then keep him from picking fights in doorways. This town sells stories faster than coffee, and gray-green buys."

We climb the stairs above a shop selling phone charms and batteries. A man in an undershirt opens like he has already decided we are a problem.

"No rooms," he says.

"One night," Mira says. "Cash. We don't bring soldiers."

He looks at me. I try to look like furniture that does not bite. It probably fails.

"One night," he says. "She pays. And if I hear him walking the hall all night, I'm charging twice."

"You won't," Mira says, and pays before I can invent a promise I can't keep.

The room is smaller than Orii's and louder — alley neon through thin glass, the elevated line screaming every few minutes, a sink that drips like it wants attention. Mira sets the sack against the door and peels off her shoes.

"You almost did it again," she says. "In the yards. With the vest."

"I didn't."

"Your mouth got ready. I felt it in my boot." She sits. Not across the room this time — closer, like distance was a Kuroishi habit she is done performing. "This town is bigger. More doors. More people who will notice if a bag check ends wrong. Tomorrow we find work that pays. You carry and I'll talk. If your throat starts inventing a shortcut, you look at me first."

"And if looking isn't enough."

"Then I drag you." She tips her chin at the futon. "Landlord charges twice for hallway walking. So if you can't sleep, sit. Don't pace."

The neon paints the wall pink, then white, then pink. I keep seeing Haru anyway — not the plum, not the pier — just the stupid habit of turning to tell him something and finding empty air.

Outside, Takamatsu keeps breathing like the war is a rumor for other wards.

---

Morning comes gray through the alley window. The landlord's kettle starts before we are ready. Mira is already packing.

"Let's find work that pays today," she says. "No papers. I do the asking. You look useful and quiet."

"I can ask."

"You can command. That's not the same," she says, and shoulders the sack.

On the stairs the landlord's kettle radio is muttering the morning feed — weather, freight schedules, then a clipped line about a security verification still open in a coastal sector, personnel incident under review, travelers from the affected zone subject to secondary checks.

Mira doesn't stop walking. Neither do I. But her hand finds my sleeve on the last step, and she doesn't let go until we're out the door.
