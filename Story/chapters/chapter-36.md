# Chapter 36 — On Purpose

The service corridor smells like oil and wet concrete.

Mei does not slow for anyone. Habu's card is still in her fist. Riku keeps my pace. Ōhashi has the notebook under his arm like it might still matter if we hit open air with it. The six from bay three breathe hard behind us and nobody asks for a rest.

We climb a short flight into the service court. Floodlight glare from the west fence leaks over the wall and paints the wet asphalt white. Across the court, the annex approach is wrong — empty armband carts stacked against a hose reel, a white door left ajar, the faint chemical stink that does not belong to kitchens. One of Ōhashi's six swears under his breath. Riku's jaw locks and he keeps walking. He does not look at the carts twice. I do not make him.

"Don't stop," Mei says. "That side is finished. We are not."

The soft service door under the dead bulb is where she points. Habu's card goes into the reader. The light stays red.

Mei tries again. Still red.

"Outer latch," she says. "Different circuit. His desk key was never meant to open the fence side."

A gray-green steps out of the shadow by the truck bay with a rifle already up. He looks young enough to still care about doing the job right. There is blood dried on one sleeve that is not his. He has been alone long enough to hear the lobby die and decide the wire still needs a man on it.

"Back inside," he says. "Hold order. Nobody leaves through this door."

"The hold is over," Mei says. "Habu cannot give you one."

"I did not ask Habu." The rifle tip does not shake. "I asked you to go back inside."

Ōhashi's six freeze. Riku shifts his weight like he is measuring the distance to the barrel and finding it too long. Mei's hand is still on the dead reader. Nobody has a gun that will win this.

I step forward before I can invent a better plan.

"Put the rifle down," I say.

I do not shout. I do not soften it into a request. I put the sentence where it has to land — clean, finished, aimed at him — and for a beat nothing happens except the taste of old metal rising at the back of my throat.

Then he throws it.

Not sets it down. Throws it — hard, away from his body, like I told him to get it as far from his hands as the bay allows. It hits the asphalt with a flat clatter that makes one of the six jump. The guard stares at his empty hands like they belong to somebody else.

I said put the rifle down. Every syllable. That is not what his hands just did. I do not say that out loud. If Riku heard something else, arguing about it here only slows us down.

"Open that door," I say.

He turns to the latch without arguing. The bar scrapes. Cold night air pushes in from the grass beyond the fence. My nose starts to bleed. I wipe it on my sleeve and keep my feet under me.

"Face the wall," I say, quieter. "Sit down. Do not call anyone. Do not follow us."

He sits with his back to the bay concrete and looks at the wall as if the wall asked first. The bleed thickens. The corridor tips a degree. Riku's good hand finds my elbow hard enough to hurt.

"Same as him," Riku says, low, for me alone. Not a speech. Not a question. Just the fact, put where I cannot pretend I did not hear it. "I can do it too — that is what you just showed me. We will have a talk about this later."

Mei is already through the door. She does not ask what I did. She saw enough.

"Fence cut is left of the glare," she says. "Run."

We run.

Grass soaks my shoes. The floodlights make the cut in the outer wire look like a black mouth. For three strides it feels like escape is a real word again — then a truck engine turns over beyond the ditch, low and heavy, and headlamps swing across the scrub.

A big truck sits on the service track outside the cut. Civilian paint, wrong for Habu's canvas. The cab door is open. A man in a plain dark coat too warm for the night stands by the running board as if he has been waiting for exactly this gap in the wire. His face will not stay put when I look at it. Stillness first, then the rest.

I know that stillness.

Minato. Cold tea. The alley where he asked me who reshapes a crooked world.

He does not say my name. He tips his chin at the open bed.

"In," he says. Pleasant. Unhurried. "All of you who can climb. The building is going to invent a reason to shoot at this fence in about two minutes, and I would rather not be the reason."

Mei stops short. "Who are you?"

"A ride," he says. "Ask better questions when you are not standing in Habu's floodlights."

Riku looks at me. I look at the coat. The alley question is still in my head — *reshaped by whom* — and I do not have a better answer than I had then. What I have is a bleeding nose, a guard facing a wall, and six men who will die if we stand here arguing with kindness.

"We take it," I say.

We climb.

Someone is already sitting against the far rail when Ōhashi hauls himself up — seed-company cap pushed back, cracked plastic cup hooked on a finger like a joke he never got to finish. Katō Hiroshi. Bay three. The empty mattress. The man who asked Ōhashi for a cigarette and was told Tuesday.

He looks older than the bunk made him. Not a kid who wandered into a review. Twenty, maybe a little more. He meets Ōhashi's stare without flinching.

"Katō," Ōhashi says. The notebook almost slips. "You — your bunk. Your cap was on the hook. We struck you. We thought—"

"I got out earlier," Katō says. Ordinary voice. Tired. "Not a review move. Review boards do not leave your cup under the bunk." He glances once at Ōhashi's notebook, then at the coat. "I left bay three before the fence went loud. Someone already knew which cut would open if the west side blew. He said there would be more people coming through. He was right." He nods once at the coat man, who is already swinging into the cab. "Ask me the rest when we are not sitting under Habu's floodlights. Sit down before you fall out."

Ōhashi sits like his knees gave the order. One of the six laughs once, sharp and ugly with relief. Riku does not laugh. He watches Katō the way he watches doors that open too easily, then looks at me, then at the coat through the back window.

"You know him," Riku says under the engine noise. Not for the truck. For me.

"I talked to him once in Takamatsu," I say. "He sat through half an evening at Minato without drinking, then asked me what I would do if the world was crooked. I said I did not know. He laughed and walked off with two quiet men in coats. I never got a name."

"And you are climbing into his truck anyway."

"He is driving away from Habu's fence. That is all I have right now."

Mei pulls the last of Ōhashi's six in and slaps the rail twice. "Argue about who bought the diesel after we clear the lights," she says. "If either of you wants out, jump now. I am not stopping once he rolls."

Nobody jumps. The coat man does not wait for a thank-you. The truck rolls. Grass, then gravel, then the dark strip of road that skirts the floodlight pool. Shizuhara's west face slides past — glass still lit, speakers still mute, Habu still somewhere upstairs with his mouth shut.

Ōhashi leans toward Katō, notebook still crushed under his arm. "Your cap was on the hook. Your cup was under the bunk. Corporals said review move. I struck your name. Where did you actually go?"

"West," Katō says. He turns the cup once in his fingers. "When the fence lights came on I left bay three. Sitting there waiting for the next hold order felt like a good way to die. I can tell you where I walked when we stop somewhere safe. Not while Habu's building still has lights on us."

"That is not an answer," Ōhashi says. "I kept forty-two names honest for five months. You do not get to vanish and climb back in with a cup like the board never lied."

"Then write me back in when we stop," Katō says. "Or leave the strike. I am not fighting a notebook in a moving truck."

Ōhashi opens his mouth, shuts it, and holds the notebook tighter.

Mei braces a boot on the rail and looks at me, not at Riku. "At the door. The guard. You told him to put the rifle down and he did. You told him to open the latch and he did. That is the same thing the Remnant boy did on the west side tonight."

"I know," I say.

"Do you." Not a question that wants comfort. "Because if you can do that, Habu's people are going to want you the same way they wanted armbands and east letters. I did not drag C out of a sealed bay so you could get yourselves inventoried again by opening your mouth at every locked door."

Riku clicks his tongue once. "He opened a door we could not open. You can lecture him after we are not bouncing past Habu's floodlights."

"I am not lecturing," Mei says. "I am telling him the bill comes due. Remnant came for me because I was useful on paper. Your friend Kurosawa is useful because people listen when he talks. If you are the same kind of useful, do not pretend you are just a pine-road boy with a nosebleed."

My sleeve is still wet. The bleed has slowed to a sticky smear. "I did not plan it. The rifle was pointed at us. I said what I needed said."

"That is how it starts," Mei says. "Nobody plans the first time. They plan the second."

Riku's shoulder stays hard against mine. "You said out first, then find him," he says, low. "Truck is west. That part matches. Do not invent a reason to climb back over the rail for a boy who already told you to go home."

"I know what he told me."

"Then say what you are going to do when we actually stop," Riku says. "Because if your answer is still chase him until he Words you into marching east again, I want to hear it before I decide whether I am walking with you or punching you in the face and walking without you."

One of Ōhashi's six looks over, then looks away fast. The engine covers most of it. Not enough.

"I am going to get clear of this place," I say. "Then I am going to find him. Not to stand in the road and beg. Not to let him send me home with six syllables that do not even mean a town anymore. I want him to look at me when I tell him I am not leaving."

"And if he tells you to stop following him again?"

"Then I will decide whether I can break that one the way I broke home." I meet Riku's eyes. "I am not asking you to like it. I am asking you not to get off this truck without me."

Riku holds my stare for a long second. Then he knocks his knuckle once against my knee, hard enough to sting. "Fine. I am not getting off without you. But when we stop, you talk to me before you talk to him. No more quiet plans in your head while I am the idiot holding your collar."

"All right."

"Say it like you mean it."

"I will talk to you first," I say. "Before I go after him again."

Mei snorts once under her breath. "If either of you jumps off this truck to chase someone in a ditch, I am not stopping for you. Stealing Habu's key got us out of C. That does not mean I am staying behind to drag you back on."

"Nobody asked you to babysit," Riku says.

"Good. Because I am not." She faces forward again. "Driver takes us west. We get off when he stops somewhere that is not a gun range. After that you can hunt scraped voices until your feet fall off. Until then, sit."

Katō watches the ditch past the rail the way a man watches weather he already dressed for. He does not join the argument. He does not look surprised by any of it.

We clear the first bend. The fence glare falls behind. For a breath the night is only engine and wet air and bodies trying not to bounce out of the bed.

Then headlamps catch a shape in the roadside ditch — two people low in the scrub, one of them tall enough that my stomach drops before I can stop it.

I lean toward the rail.

Riku's hand clamps my wrist. "Do not. You jump and you land under a wheel, or you land in Habu's grass with no truck and no plan. Sit."

"Riku—"

"Sit."

The truck does not stop. We are past them in the time it takes to draw a breath. I see dark hair. A girl's grip on a boy's jacket. A face I have been chasing since the pine road — scraped, blood-dried, looking not at me first but at the cab, at the coat in the glass, then at me.

Kaito.

His eyes catch mine across the open bed and the moving dark. For one stretch of road there is nothing else — not Mei, not Habu, not the Word still sticky on my lip. Just him seeing me, and me seeing him, and the engine carrying us past each other before either of us can make a sound that reaches.

His mouth opens on something I cannot hear. The girl hauls him back into the ditch hard. Then we are gone around the next bend, and Shizuhara is only a white smear behind the trees, and the ditch where he was is already out of sight.
