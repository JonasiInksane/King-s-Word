# Chapter 34 — Whole Building

Mira's arms are still locked around me when the ringing in my ears starts to leave room for ordinary sound.

A radio on the lobby floor keeps asking for status that will not come. Habu's tea cup sits on his blotter, untouched. His mouth is still shut. His eyes are awake and fixed on the glass rail as if the bodies below might stand up and ask him for a kind sentence. The clerk who fetched the key sits on the mezzanine stair with empty eyes and a blank face, waiting for an order nobody is giving. Mei is still at the service door behind Habu's chair, one hand on the frame, cheeks wet, not making a sound.

I try to put weight under a new sentence — medical, east, whoever is still breathing in a gray-green coat — and nothing comes. My throat is scraped raw. Blood is drying on my chin and on Mira's sleeve. My legs do not trust the floor.

"Say it," Mira says into my shoulder. Not soft. Not kind. "Say you stopped."

"I stopped."

"Louder."

"I stopped." It costs more than a Word. "There is nothing left in my mouth for them."

She loosens enough that I can breathe, not enough that I can walk past her toward the rail again. Habu's gaze flicks to my face. He understands. He cannot answer. That is worse than if he had begged.

Mei wipes her face on her wrist and turns.

"We have to leave this office," she says. "The west shift is dead. That does not mean the whole building is empty. Medical still has a night desk. East still has a lock. C is still sealed on Habu's last order. If somebody upstairs is watching a different board, they will send people who did not hear the lobby die."

Mira looks at Habu.

"What do we do with him?"

"Leave him," Mei says. "If you shoot him, you make noise for a man who already cannot call for help. If you open his mouth again, you spend what the boy does not have." She crosses to the desk, opens the top drawer Habu's hands were twitching toward earlier, and takes a second keycard on a cloth lanyard. Habu's eyes track her. He does not move. "This opens the letter locks and the C hold doors when the board says sealed. My war-sheet card only gets me recounts. His gets me through."

"That was the job," Mira says. "Get you outside before first light. Suzumori is still waiting on a failed extract, not a tour of every wing this place sealed."

Mei holds the lanyard like it weighs more than plastic.

"Your pull failed the moment A and B went into that annex," she says. "I am not walking west with you while C is locked to their bays and Habu's kindness is still written on the speakers. There are people in that hold who waved at the curry kids two days ago. There are two tags who climb kitchen stairs when they should not. I owe morning counts to a bay that is still breathing. I am not leaving them for a clipboard that thinks inventory zero means the night went well."

Mira's jaw tightens. "I came into this building for you."

"Then you came on the wrong night," Mei says. "Or you came for the right person on a night that turned into something else. I will not be the girl you steal while the rest of the letter-wing and C stay on Habu's hold. Take your boy and go. I am going through that door."

She tips her chin at the service door. Dark stair smell comes up through it — disinfectant, boiled cloth, the medical wing we used as a shortcut when we were still only watching.

I push off the rail. Mira has to catch my belt again and hauls me back a step.

"Kurosawa. Stand still or sit down."

"You can't open C alone," I say. The scraped voice is thin. No weight under it. Just talk. "If there are still rifles on that side—"

"Then I use the card and I move quiet, the way I have moved in this building for months," Mei says. "You just showed me what happens when a mouth decides every guard on the west side is a problem. I do not need that in C. C needs a door and a person who knows which bunks still have names."

Mira steps between us and the service door.

"Mei. Listen to me. Outside there is a contact and an old woman who still thinks keys cut both ways. Inside there is a sealed wing and a building that just watched a boy empty a lobby. If you go alone and the hold is still honest, you die in a corridor that does not care you cried for the curry kids. Come west. We regroup. We come back with people who are not already bleeding out of their mouths."

"No." Mei does not raise her voice. She does not need to. "I watched them thank children into a hose room. I watched you stop him from finishing the rest. That stop does not get those kids back, and it does not unlock C. I am done being Remnant property for one night. I am going to the people I still have numbers for."

Habu makes a sound in his throat that cannot become a word. Mira's rifle tip drifts toward him and then away. She looks at me instead.

"Are you going to Word her into coming with us?"

The sentence is almost there — *come with us, leave the door*. I look at Mei's wet face and the lanyard in her fist and the dark service stair that leads toward the wing I never walked. The kids on the drains are still behind my eyes. If I put weight on Mei now, I am Habu with a scraped voice.

"No," I say.

Mira swears, ordinary and tired.

"Then say something that is not a command," she tells me. "Because if you stand there mute while she walks into a sealed hold, I am going to remember that too."

I meet Mei's eyes.

"Go," I say. "Don't die for a door. If the hold is still full of living people, get them moving before somebody invents a new review."

Mei nods once. She looks at Mira.

"Tell Suzumori I declined the road. Tell her I am not coming out the wire tonight." She looks at me once more. "And tell your boy — when his mouth comes back, he should use it on men who thank children, not on the ones trying to open their locks."

"I heard you," I say.

She takes Habu's keycard, checks her war-sheet card is still inside her shirt, and pulls the service door wider. Cold air from medical moves the papers on Habu's desk. Habu watches her the way he watched the lobby — attentive, useless, kind only in the shape of his shoulders.

"Mei," Mira says one more time.

Mei does not turn around.

"I am not your extract anymore," she says, and goes down into the dark.

---

The service door swings shut on her footsteps. For a few seconds I can still hear them on the metal stair. Then the stair takes the sound and I can't hear her anymore, and the glass office is only Habu, the empty-eyed clerk, Mira, and the smell of blood that has started to turn ordinary.

Mira hauls my arm over her shoulder.

"We're leaving," she says. "You said you stopped. That means we walk out while we still can."

"What about Habu?"

"We leave him. I am not spending a round on a man who already cannot talk, and you are not inventing a sentence for him either." She tips the rifle at the clerk on the stair. "Stand up. Walk us to the west service corridor, then sit facing the wall. Do not call anyone. Do not follow."

The clerk stands. He leads like a man carrying a tray. We leave Habu in his glass with his tea cup and his sealed mouth and his view of the lobby he filled with kindness and rifles. He does not get a goodbye. He does not deserve one.

The mezzanine stair is sticky. Below, clerks without guns are still behind desks. One of them is praying under his breath. Another is staring at a dead gray-green like the coat might sit up. Nobody raises a voice at us. Habu's west guards are still on the tile. The building feels huge and hollow at the same time — whole only because the lights are still on and the speakers are still waiting for a soft man who cannot speak.

We cut through the kitchen junction. The painted lines where armbands stood are empty. The serving counter still has the smell of copper and old curry. Mira does not let me look long. My legs try to fold at the dish-room door; she drags me past it.

"You can hate me for this later," she says. "When you can stand without leaning on me."

"I don't hate you."

"Good. Then keep your feet moving."

The west service corridor is darker than it was when we came in loud. Floodlight glare from the fence still leaks through high windows. Somewhere far behind us a lock clicks — metal on metal, a sealed hold giving way. Mira's head turns toward the sound the same time mine does.

"That's her," I say. "She got Habu's card to work."

I stop. Mira's grip on my belt goes hard.

"Don't," she says.

"She's opening C."

"And you are not going back through a building you already emptied just to watch her do it." She pulls. For a second I do not move. The next sentence tries to rise again — not for rifles this time, for the dark behind us, for the girl with the lanyard, for whoever is waiting on the other side of that lock. Mira puts her forehead against mine hard enough that it hurts. "You told me you stopped. Twice. If you turn around now, that was not stopping. That was a pause."

"All right," I say.

We move.

At the service door where the night clerk used to smoke, the latch is still loose. Outside air hits like cold water — wet grass, diesel, the oil smell of the truck that brought us in. Mira half-carries me over the sill. Behind us Shizuhara keeps its lights on like nothing in the lobby happened. Ahead, the cut in the fence is a black gap in the glare.

We hit the grass, and I follow where she points.

At the wire I look back once.

Through a high corridor window I can see a strip of the C approach — a painted letter on a door, a card reader blinking from red to waiting green. A small figure with a tablet and a lanyard stands there long enough to press Habu's key to the lock. The door starts to move. I do not see who is on the other side. I do not get faces. Mira's hand finds the back of my neck and turns my head forward before I can invent a reason to stay.

"Eyes front," she says. "We are getting out of here."

Somewhere behind us, in a building that just watched its lobby die, a sealed door is standing open.
