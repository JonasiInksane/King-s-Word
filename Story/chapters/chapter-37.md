# Chapter 37 — Reed City

The truck does not stop for a long time after Shizuhara falls behind us.

Nobody asks the coat man to turn around and go back for Kaito. Riku keeps his hand near my wrist without holding it — he still thinks I might jump off the truck. Mei watches the road and the side streets like she is already planning how we get out of the next bad place. Ōhashi has the notebook open on his knee and has not written a word since Katō told him to wait until we stopped somewhere safe. The six from bay three sleep in pieces, heads knocking the canvas whenever the tires hit a hole.

Katō does not pretend to sleep. When the floodlight glare of Shizuhara is finally gone behind a ridge, he looks at Ōhashi once — acknowledgment that the struck name in the notebook is still waiting.

"When we stop," Ōhashi says, quiet, "I am putting your name back. Living. If you disappear again, I will write that too."

"Fair," Katō says.

The road widens. The dark stops being scrub and starts being outskirts — warehouse roofs, a canal that smells like wet rope instead of fence oil, neon that is not Habu's white glare. An elevated line runs a ring of light around something big enough that I have to sit up to see the end of it.

Mei leans toward the cab window. "Where is this?"

The coat man's voice comes back pleasant, unhurried, the same tone he used at the wire. "Ashiro. Reed Road city, if the old maps still bother with poetry. Canal wards, night markets, boarding blocks that do not ask for a relocation number if you pay cash and keep your voice down. Thin gray-green presence this far inland from the line. You can breathe here for a while if you do not make yourselves interesting."

"How long is a while?" Riku asks.

"Longer than a night on the run," the coat man says. "Not forever. I am not selling you a home. I am putting you somewhere that is not Habu's inventory."

The truck takes a ramp down into a ward that is still awake. Lanterns hang over reed-stall alleys. Steam lifts off noodle carts. A canal runs black between concrete banks, and somebody's radio is playing a song too cheerful for the hour. Kids my age — ordinary kids, not armbands — shout across a bridge about a lost bicycle. No floodlights. No soft hold speech. For a second my chest does not know what to do with the noise.

We stop in a loading pocket behind a boarding house with a painted reed on the door. The coat man kills the engine, climbs down, and opens the bed gate like a host, not a jailer.

"Out," he says. "All of you who are staying."

We climb down. My legs have gone stiff. The air smells like canal water and somebody burning incense in a stairwell. Habu's disinfectant is finally out of my nose.

The coat man counts us with his eyes the way he counted at the wire — making sure nobody is still in the bed. Then he takes a folded envelope from inside his coat and holds it out to Mei, not to me.

"Room keys for the third floor. Two rooms. Coin for food and a few days of looking ordinary. The landlady thinks you are warehouse hands who missed a transfer. Do not correct her with a better story."

Mei takes the envelope. She does not thank him yet. "And you?"

"I have other work." He glances at Katō, who has not climbed down. "He rides with me."

Ōhashi steps forward so fast the notebook almost hits the coat man's chest. "No. He is bay three. He is on my book. You do not get to invent another review move in a city I have never seen."

Katō swings his legs over the rail and drops to the asphalt light on his feet. He looks at Ōhashi, then at the rest of us, then at the coat.

"I am not going into those rooms," Katō says. Plain. No joke. "He got me out when the west side went loud. I owe him the ride, not your notebook. Write me living if it helps you sleep. I will not be on the third floor in the morning."

"That is not—" Ōhashi starts.

"It is what is happening," Katō says. He nods at me once, almost friendly. "Keep the pine-road Tuesday joke. I never collected on it."

He climbs back into the bed and sits against the rail the way he was sitting when we found him. The coat man closes the gate without looking like a man who stole anyone.

"Aoyama," he says, and my surname in his mouth still feels like a draft under a door. "Eat. Sleep. Do not follow quiet coats into alleys as a hobby — I already told you that once. This time I mean stay in the ward and be boring."

"You still have not given me a name," I say.

He smiles without showing teeth. "Names are for people who want to be found. Go upstairs."

He gets in the cab. The truck rolls. Katō's seed-cap is the last shape I see against the canvas, and then the loading pocket is only us, the reed door, and a city that does not know Habu's name.

Ōhashi stands with the notebook open and nowhere to put the pencil.

"He was mine," he says. Not loud. Not for the street. "I struck him and I found him and he left again."

"He left on his feet," Mei says. She is already counting the envelope. "That is more than A and B got. Come on. Before the landlady decides warehouse hands do not arrive at this hour."

The boarding house smells like boiled rice and old wood. The landlady is a woman with a scarf and no patience for speeches. She takes one look at our C shirts and the dried blood on my sleeve, sniffs once, and points at the stairs.

"Third floor. Bath is down the hall. If you fight, you leave. If you bring soldiers, you leave. Rent is in the envelope or you leave. Questions?"

"No, ma'am," Riku says, before anyone else can invent one.

Two rooms. Thin mattresses. A window that looks over the canal and the night market lanterns. One of Ōhashi's six sits on the floor and laughs until it turns into something that is not laughter, and nobody tells him to stop. Mei sets Habu's keycard on the windowsill like she does not know what else to do with a thing that opened doors and cannot open a city.

Riku pulls me into the corner by the window while the others claim mattresses.

"We are off Habu's ground," he says. "That was the first part. Tomorrow, before you go looking for Kurosawa, you talk to me. That was the deal."

"I know."

"Do you." He tips his chin at the canal. "Because I can already see you measuring which bridge he would take if he walked into this ward. Sit on it for one night. Eat something that is not institutional rice. If you run out that door before morning, I am not promising I will catch you gently."

Mei joins us without asking if the corner is private. She has washed her face. The blood line near her hairline is still there.

"I am not your Remnant contact," she says. "I am not your babysitter either. I got you out of C because I owed morning counts and because Habu's kill room was real. In the morning I am finding day work so this envelope lasts. If you two want to hunt a scraped voice through a city this size, you do it without expecting me to hold a map."

"You could leave," I say.

She looks at me for a long second. "I could. I also watched A and B walk into vents while I held a tablet and could not stop it. I am not ready to be alone with that yet. That is not the same as signing up for your chase."

"Fair," Riku says.

I look out at Ashiro — reed stalls, canal light, an elevated train sliding past like the Loop used to in a life that still had a birthday cake on a roof. Kaito is somewhere behind us on a road that does not care we made it here. The coat man is gone with Katō. Habu is still mouthed in a glass office we will never see again if we are lucky.

"We stay," I say. "Tonight. We eat. We sleep. In the morning we talk — you first," I tell Riku, "before I invent a plan that puts our names on a desk again."

Riku clicks his tongue once. "Finally. Soft boy learns a schedule."

Mei snorts, almost a real sound. "Do not call him that where the landlady can hear. She will think we are trouble."

"We are trouble," one of the six says from the mattress, eyes already closing.

"Not tonight," Mei says. "Tonight we are warehouse hands who missed a transfer. Act like it."

We go downstairs and buy noodles from a cart that does not ask for papers. The broth burns my mouth. It is the best thing I have tasted since Minato. Riku eats like someone who has decided hunger is an argument he can win. Mei pays from the envelope and keeps the change counted. Ōhashi sits with the notebook closed for the first time since bay three and watches the canal as if names might float past.

"You are staring at the water like it owes you a headcount," Riku tells him.

"Katō owed me a Tuesday," Ōhashi says. "The board owed me honesty. This city does not owe me anything, and somehow that is worse." He taps the closed notebook once. "In the morning I am going to write the six who came, and the two who stayed in C, and Katō living with a question mark. Then I am going to find out if Ashiro has a stationery shop, because this pencil is a stub."

Mei almost smiles. "That is the most ordinary plan anyone has said all night. Keep it."

One of the six asks if there are gray-greens on the bridges. Mei shakes her head.

"Not the way Habu ran them. Coat man was right about thin presence — I counted one patrol car on the elevated and it was going somewhere else in a hurry. Do not take that as forever. Take it as tonight."

Across the water, lanterns rock on their wires. Somebody laughs too loud. A couple argues about a bicycle. The war app in a stranger's pocket buzzes once and gets ignored, the way I used to ignore it on birthday nights I thought I could keep. An elevated train slides past with its windows full of people going home from shifts that still exist. Nobody in those windows is walking into a vent review. Nobody is waiting for Habu's thank-you voice.

Riku knocks my bowl with a knuckle. "You are somewhere else again."

"I am here," I say. "I am just not used to a place where nobody is inventing a hold order."

"Then sleep before your brain invents one for you," Riku says. "Morning comes either way."

We carry the empty bowls back toward the reed door. Upstairs, one of the six has claimed the mattress by the wall. Another is washing blood out of a sleeve in the shared bath. Mei has put Habu's keycard on the sill and is folding the envelope smaller so it will not show in her pocket.

Riku checks the latch twice, then looks at me.

"Mattress," he says. "Not the window. If you stand there all night measuring bridges, I am tying your ankle to the bedpost."

"I will sleep," I say.

"Say it like you mean it."

"I will sleep."

I lie down. The canal noise comes through the glass. For a while it is only water and carts and somebody laughing too loud in the market, and that is what I listen to until my eyes finally close.
