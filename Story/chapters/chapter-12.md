# Chapter 12 — Bus Fare

Orii is already at the counter when we come down. She points at a stack of rice sacks by the door without looking up from her pencil.

"Haul those to the back before you leave. Then you're paid up."

We haul. Mira takes the rhythm of it; I take the weight. My nose has stopped bleeding. My throat still feels scraped. When the last sack is stacked Orii sets two onigiri on the counter and a folded paper with a bus time written on it in the same blunt hand as her inventory.

"Ishibashi run leaves at nine from the depot if it hasn't been requisitioned," she says. "If it has, you walk — same road, only dustier." She looks at Mira, not me. "Your cousin should keep his head down at the ticket window. Clipboards have been asking about mouths since yesterday."

Mira bows like a coast girl who knows how to leave a debt clean. "Thank you."

Orii has already gone back to her pencil. We take the onigiri and go.

---

The bus is still there. Gray, rattling, half full of people who look like they have been moving west for weeks. Mira buys two tickets with money from the bottom of the sack — I see how thin the fold is when she opens it — and we sit near the back with our bags at our feet.

Nobody asks where we are from. The war app buzzes once in somebody's pocket and everybody pretends not to hear it. Through the window Kuroishi slides away: blackened shrine gate, well square, Orii's awning.

Mira leans her head against the glass and closes her eyes — resting, not sleeping. I watch the road go by and keep my mouth shut.

An hour in, a pair of soldiers works the aisle from the front, checking tickets and faces. The woman across from us hands hers over with a smile that costs her. When they get to us Mira has both tickets ready and a story about cousins and a family errand already shaped in her mouth.

"Ishibashi," she says. "Our aunt's people are expecting us."

The taller one takes the tickets and does not stamp them. He looks at me longer than he looked at her — at the dust on my knees, at a boy who doesn't smile when smiled at. Then back to Mira.

"Your aunt's people. Which house?"

It is a small question. It is exactly the size of question that sinks people.

"Off the bridge square. The Moris — they take in mending." She says it with the mild boredom of a girl who has answered for her family a hundred times. "If they're not at the house, they're at the stall."

"There's a mending stall on the bridge square?" He says it half to the bus, almost friendly. Behind us a seat creaks, and an old man's voice arrives, helpful, terrible: "Square's all fish these days. Mending would be new."

The soldier's eyes come back to Mira. The whole bus is very interested in its windows.

"New since spring," Mira says, unhurried. "Half of everything is new since spring — that's why they need hands." She lets one breath of annoyance show, exactly one. "You're welcome to walk us there off the bus. My aunt will feed you for the trouble, but she'll also tell you about her knees, and that costs an afternoon."

It is good. It might even have been enough. But he is still holding the tickets, and now he tips his chin at me.

"You," he says, mild. "Say something."

Mira's shoulder touches mine — not a shove, a reminder that she talks first. Except he asked me, in front of the whole bus, and *he's shy* only works on a man who hasn't dug in.

The sentence is already there, assembled before I ask for it — *Stamp the tickets and move on* — four words, clean, no exits. I make my mouth do the harder thing instead.

"We're going for the work," I say. My voice comes out the way it comes out these days. His eyebrows move at the scrape of it. "Sir."

"What happened to your voice?"

"Fever in the spring." The lie Mira built for me, ordinary in my own mouth for the first time. "It never came back right."

He looks at me for a long beat with the bus jolting under all of us. Then he stamps both tickets and moves on, and the old man behind us tells his window that mending is a good trade after all, as if he had been on our side the whole time.

Mira does not let her breath out until the pair reaches the front. She doesn't say anything either. She just passes me the water like it's about the heat, and I drink, and my pulse comes down slow off the sentence I didn't say.

I keep my mouth shut the rest of the way to Ishibashi.

---

Ishibashi is a stone bridge over a nearly dry riverbed and a market tucked under the arches — squid oil, hot stone, diesel from a truck idling where the bus lets us off. The FDC presence is thin paper theater: a desk under a tarp, a woman stamping day-passes, nobody with a rifle in a hurry.

Mira counts the money left in the sack while we stand in the shade of the bridge.

"Enough for food today," she says. "Not enough for the Takamatsu bus and a roof."

"How far is Takamatsu on foot?"

"Two days if we push. Three if we don't." She looks at the market. "Or we earn the fare here and ride tomorrow."

"Earn how?"

"Same way coast people always earn." She tips her chin at the stalls under the arches — crates, ice, a man shouting for hands to unload a truck. "You can haul, and people trust my face when I ask. We don't use anything else."

The *anything else* sits between us. She is not asking. She is drawing a line I already know and making sure I hear it in daylight.

"I talk first," I say.

"You haul first. Talking is my job until your voice stops making people look twice."

---

The truck man is thick through the middle and red from the sun. I haul crates for him until sundown and he pays in cash — not much, but enough to put the Takamatsu fare back in the sack. My shoulders burn the useful way. Once he asks where I'm from. I say west, and he laughs like that counts as an answer, then goes back to his count.

Mira spent the afternoon on trays at a squid stall. She comes back with grease on her wrists and the one thing worth knowing: a boy who runs errands for the depot says the Takamatsu bus still leaves at dawn if nobody has requisitioned it overnight. She starts to tell me who else she talked to — a stall woman, a clerk near the stamps — and stops when she sees I'm only listening for the bus.

At the edge of the market she buys two bowls of something hot with the truck money, and we eat under the bridge where the arches keep the wind off. While we eat she counts what is left in the sack against the fare and the stamps.

"So we sleep near the arches," Mira says. "Cheaper than a room, but cold. Up before the bus if we want seats."

"How long to Takamatsu from here?"

"Half a day if the bus isn't stopped. Longer if they hold the road." She taps the sack. "We've got fare and almost nothing else. Once we're there we need work the first afternoon, or we'll spend the last of it on food and go hungry."

"What kind of work?"

"Yards or markets — whatever doesn't ask for a family register." She looks at me over the bowl. "And you keep your head down at the window. If they ask you to talk, I talk. Same as the bus today."

A man from the ice truck walks past with a crate, recognizes me from the unload, and slows. "You heading out already? We could use you again tomorrow — same rate, maybe better if the squid boat comes in."

I almost say something ordinary. In Nagihama I would have. Here I just nod once and look at my bowl until he shrugs and moves on.

Mira watches him go. "He was offering money."

"We've got enough for the tickets."

"Enough for tickets isn't the same as enough for a week." She says it without heat, then softer: "You used to learn people's names by supper. That one had a face you could have used tomorrow if the bus got canceled."

"If the bus gets canceled, we walk."

"We walk," she agrees, and does not push the name. She finishes her bowl, wipes her wrists on her skirt, and stands. "We sleep under the arches tonight and get to the depot before first light. If they stamp us through, Takamatsu by afternoon — and then we find a roof that doesn't care where west is."

"And if they don't stamp us?"

"Then we find another road." She tips her chin at the sack. "Come on. Cold's easier if we claim a dry spot before the market empties."

---

We claim a dry strip under the arches before the market empties and sleep with the sack between us. Cold comes up through the stone. Nobody asks our names.

Before first light Mira shakes my shoulder. Yesterday's pay is still counted into two tickets' worth and a little food. The dawn bus is on the board. At the window she buys both seats to Takamatsu, the clerk stamps the slips without looking up twice, and we climb on. No aisle soldiers this time. Just market people, a radio playing something bright, and the road starting to climb.

For the first hour we don't talk. Then Mira does, quiet enough that the seat ahead can't use it.

"Once we're in, we drop the aunt story," she says. "It nearly fell apart yesterday over a mending stall. Cities ask for addresses. If we give them one that isn't real, somebody checks it by supper."

"So what do we say?"

"As little as possible. We don't go through Station Plaza if we can help it — that place is full of gray-greens. We get off with the yard workers and walk east. East Yards hire by the day and don't care whose cousin you are as long as you can lift."

"You've been there?"

"I've heard enough to not get us arrested the first afternoon." She tips the sack with her foot. "We've got almost nothing left after the tickets. Work before dark, or we're sleeping in a ditch with bigger rats than Ishibashi's."

I watch the hills go by. "And if they ask me to talk?"

"Then you talk like a boy who wants a wage, not a boy who wants an argument." She glances at my mouth, then away. "Ordinary words. Just ask for the wage and stop there. Same rule as the crates."

"I know the rule."

"Knowing it and keeping it when a clipboard leans in are different jobs." She says it without heat. "You're getting better at being quiet. Don't start telling people what to do just because quiet feels like it isn't enough."

I almost tell her the quiet is the only thing that has felt like mine since the shrine yard. I don't. The bus hits a patch of bad road and the whole cabin rattles, and when it settles she is looking at the window again.

"Why Takamatsu?" I ask. "Not the next town. Not farther west."

"Because it's big enough to get lost in and still find work." She is quiet a moment. "And because the road west of it gets thinner, and thin roads have fewer places to put a boy who can't go home."

That is as much answer as she gives and I let it stand.

Late afternoon the land opens. Takamatsu comes up under us — harbor cranes, smokestacks, streets running until they blur, wet neon even in daylight, fry oil and pine and brake-squeal. Nobody on the bus looks impressed. They have been moving toward this for weeks.

We get off with the yard shift at a stop before Station Plaza — Mira's hand on my sleeve once, already steering. Behind us the main depot is a mess of cones and waiting people. Ahead, East Yards: chain-link, diesel, men shouting for hands before the afternoon load leaves.

"There," Mira says. "Ask for day work. I'll find us a roof that takes cash and doesn't keep a register book."

"Split up?"

"For an hour. Meet back at that water tower when the whistle blows." She nods at a rusted tower over the yards. "If I'm late, wait. If you're late, I come looking — and I don't want to come looking because your mouth got bored."

"It won't."

"Good." She almost smiles. It doesn't reach her eyes. "Water tower when the whistle blows. Go."

A foreman at the gate is already shouting for hands that can lift without asking questions. I put my name nowhere and take a place in the line before someone else takes it first.
