# Chapter 42 — Taking Time

Four days into the paper shop and Endo trusts me enough to send me out on deliveries instead of just watching me stack, which is either a promotion or a way to get me out of his hair during the slow hours. Either way, it means I'm carrying a roll of festival lanterns across three wards by midmorning with nobody watching my shoulders for a tell, and it's the closest thing to peace I've had since the truck pulled away from Habu's mezzanine with Haruki's face in the gap of the canvas.

I ask about him anyway. Not loud, not with a description that sounds like a wanted notice — Mira drilled that into me the first night and I haven't forgotten it — just a question dropped sideways into whatever conversation is already happening. A dockworker unloading crates near the ferry stair. A woman selling roasted chestnuts who's worked that corner nine years and knows every face that's ever bought from her twice.

"Group of kids came through maybe a week back," I say to the chestnut woman, like it's idle. "One of them tall, dark hair, might've had a rough voice on him."

"Half this city came through a week back," she says, not unkind, just tired. "Reed Festival brings in every cousin and stranger with a boarding-house cousin. You looking for family?"

"Something like that."

"Ask at the labor board, not me. I sell chestnuts. I don't keep a register." She hands me a paper cone anyway, warm, without charging for it, which might be the nicest thing anyone's done for me all week and also tells me exactly nothing.

It's like that every time. A shrug, a maybe, a *lot of tall boys in this city, kid.* Nothing that points anywhere. I used to think patience was something other people had because they hadn't lost anything yet. Turns out it's just this — asking the same quiet question forty times and getting forty different kinds of nothing, and going back the next day to ask again anyway.

Back at the shop, Tetsu is stacking the delivery cart for the afternoon run and gives me the east-ward route without being asked twice, which I've learned means he trusts me not to get lost or steal the cart.

"You're out there asking people things," he says. It's not a question either. "Endo noticed. Says you talk to every stall owner on your route like you're memorizing them."

"Looking for someone," I say. "A friend. Got separated coming into the city."

"Separated." He says the word like he's turning it over for a false bottom. "That's a soft way to put whatever actually happened."

"It's the true way. Just not the whole of it."

Tetsu shrugs, the kind of shrug that means he's decided not to ask twice either. "Ashiro's not as big as it looks from the bridge. People wash up in the same three or four places — the labor board, the ferry stair, the market. Keep asking. Cities like this one eventually run out of ways to hide a person, especially with the festival dragging half of them out into the open at once." He hands me the cart handle. "East ward. Don't let the fire marshal's cousin talk you into a discount on frames. He does that to every new face."

---

Suzumori's ankle is good enough now that she only leans on the crutch going downstairs, and she's decided this means she's earned the right to come to the market with me and Mira instead of waiting in the room like something that needs minding.

"I am not luggage," she says, when Mira suggests she stay behind while we go for ointment and rice. "I have walked farther than either of you on worse legs. I am coming to look at faces, because four eyes miss less than two, and because I am tired of that window."

"Nobody said luggage," Mira says. "I said rest another day. There's a difference."

"There is no difference when it's the same window and the same wall for the fourth day running." Suzumori is already reaching for her crutch. "I sat behind shutters for longer than you've been alive, girl, waiting for men in nice watches to decide what happened to me next. I am not doing that again in a room I'm paying rent on. If my ankle gives out, you may say you told me so exactly once."

Mira looks at me like she wants backup and isn't going to get it, because I already know better than to referee this one.

"Fine," she says. "Once."

We go together. The market at midday is packed shoulder to shoulder along the canal rail, stalls selling everything from dried fish to festival string lights, and it's loud in a way that feels almost normal — not FDC-loud, not gunfire-loud, just a hundred people arguing over the price of onions. Mira haggles a wrap of ointment down two coins while Suzumori leans on her crutch and watches the crowd the way she watches everything, unhurried, like she's reading names off it.

That's when the boy goes for her coin purse.

He's quick about it — younger than me, maybe fourteen, already three steps into the crowd with the little cloth pouch in his fist before Suzumori even finishes turning to see what pulled at her belt. I move before I decide to move, which is the whole problem with my body lately; it still thinks like the pier, like the roof, like a place where the fastest answer is always the right one.

"Stop—" comes up in my throat on its own, already shaped, already carrying weight I haven't decided to give it, and copper floods the back of my tongue before the word finishes forming, sharp and metallic and familiar as a bruise. One more syllable and that boy's legs lock wherever he's standing, in the middle of a crowded market, in front of forty strangers who would remember a boy who freezes mid-stride for the rest of their lives.

I swallow it instead. Whole. It goes down like a stone and leaves my throat raw, and I close the last few steps on my own legs and grab the boy by the back of his collar with my actual hand.

He yelps and twists, and I twist him right back until the purse falls out of his fist onto the cobbles, and then I let go, because he's fourteen and skinny and already looking at me like I'm about to do something worse than shout.

"Go," I say. Just that. No weight under it, nothing but a boy telling another boy to get lost. "Before she decides to press it."

He goes. Fast, weaving through legs and stall poles until he's just another back in the crowd, and I stand there with my heart going too hard and my throat raw from the word I swallowed instead of said.

Suzumori picks up her own purse from the cobbles without any drama at all, like this happens to her weekly, which for all I know it does.

"You almost said something," she says. Not a question.

"I didn't."

"I know you didn't. I asked because I watched you decide not to, which is a different thing than not thinking of it at all." She weighs the purse in her palm, checking the coins are still inside. "That was the first time I've seen you almost use your voice on somebody and stop yourself."

"He's a kid," I say. "Stealing a purse. That's not — that's not the kind of thing that needs my voice on it. My hands worked fine."

"Your hands have always worked fine," Mira says, arriving a beat late with the ointment, taking in the scene in one pass the way she does. "That was never the argument."

---

We eat lunch on the canal steps because none of us wants to carry the ointment and the rice all the way home before the bread goes stale. Suzumori peels a hard-boiled egg with more concentration than the task requires, which is her way of thinking without announcing that she's thinking.

"You could have stopped him with one word from ten feet away," she says eventually. "No running. No grabbing. No collar in your fist. Just a word, and he freezes, and you walk over at your own pace and pick the purse up like nothing in the market ever moved."

"I know."

"Why didn't you?"

I turn the empty paper chestnut cone over in my hands, looking for an answer that isn't just a reflex dressed up as a reason.

"Because I've used that voice on things that mattered a lot more than a purse, and it costs me every time," I say slowly. "Because if I start using it on a fourteen-year-old with sticky fingers, I don't know where the line moves to next. Because Mira held my jaw shut in a ditch outside Shizuhara so I wouldn't kill a truck full of people, and I don't want to find out what happens to a purse-thief on a day nobody's there to hold anything."

Suzumori doesn't say *good boy* or anything close to it, which I'm grateful for, because I think I'd have thrown the egg at her.

"Stopping yourself isn't the same as being slow," she says instead. "People confuse those. A slow boy doesn't notice it coming. You felt it and chose not to say it. That took you too long to learn, and you're still learning it."

"It isn't," I agree. "None of this feels finished."

"No," she says. "It won't, for a while yet."

Mira has gone quiet beside me, turning her own cup in her hands, and when she finally speaks it isn't about the thief or the market or the coin at all.

"You've been asking about him for four days," she says. "Quiet questions. You'll get a lead eventually — this city isn't that big, and the festival will pull people to the same pond." She looks at me directly. "So say it plainly. When you find him — what are you going to say?"

I open my mouth. Nothing comes. Not a command. Not even an ordinary sentence.

Across the canal a clerk's face will not stay put for the length of one glance — stillness first, then the rest — and when I blink hard he is just a tired man stamping receipts. Ashiro keeps doing that to me. Or I keep doing it to Ashiro. Either way the festival night is not a clean vacation from the coat, and my body knows it even when I try to spend a whole afternoon pretending otherwise.

"I don't know yet," I say.

Mira nods once, like that is an answer she can work with for tonight.
