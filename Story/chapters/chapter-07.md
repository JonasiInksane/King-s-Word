# Chapter 7 — Practice

The gulls were the first thing I tried, because gulls don't file reports.

North end of the sea wall, an hour before the boats go out, nobody but me and forty birds fighting over the co-op's gut bucket. I picked the big one on the piling, got my voice down into the register where the weight lives — I can feel it now, the way you feel a lifted crate settle onto your spine — and told it: "Fly away."

Nothing. It looked at me with one flat yellow eye and went back to screaming at the other gulls.

I tried again, closer, cleaner. *Fly away. Leave. Go.* Coin taste rising faint at the back of my throat each time, weight going out of me — and nothing coming back. Forty gulls, and not one of them cared what I said, and on the walk to work it came together like a splice under Jun's thumb: the soldier on the roof heard me *and knew what the words meant.* The one on the pier heard me and couldn't find the meaning — pronouns, no handles — and it slid off.

It's not a shout. It's not volume at all. It's a sentence going into a mind that *understands* it, and the understanding is the door the weight walks through.

Which means it only works on people.

I stand on the sea wall with the gut bucket screaming behind me and let it arrive properly. The only thing in the world my mouth works on is people. There's no practicing on cans off a fence.

I go to work and tie splices and don't say anything with weight on it for three days.

The nights are different. Most nights I end up on the north wall again.

Practice looks like this: a boy on cold stone past midnight, building sentences out loud with no weight on them, the way you dry-fire a rifle. I pick a job — *make a man put down a knife, make a man step out of a doorway, make a man forget he saw me* — and then I draft the sentence and attack my own draft. *Put the knife down* — which knife, what if he's holding two things, does *down* mean drop or set. *Step aside* — aside which way, what if aside is off a pier. Every sentence has exits, and everything I love about how I've talked my whole life — fast, hot, first thing out of my mouth — is exactly what leaves the exits open. So I sand them. Shorter. Fewer doors. Say it, hear the gap, close the gap, say it again. Some nights I get one sentence smooth in two hours, and my throat aches like I've been shouting even though nothing I said all night was louder than the tide.

Twice I put a hair of weight on one, just to feel the coin taste rise, aimed at nothing and nobody — and even that spent something. I could feel it come out of me: a faint pressure behind the eyes, gone by morning, but real.

The second time, a lantern comes up the wall path. Old Harada, checking his skiff lines the way he does when his hips won't let him sleep. I sit still and let him find me being a boy staring at the sea, which is a thing this town's boys are allowed to be at night, and he grunts something about the swell and goes back down. I tell myself he saw nothing.

The town notices things too. That comes clear on Friday.

---

I know what the gulls proved. Every real test has to be a person. I starting creating tests, and I know exactly what I am doing — the version where I stumble into it is a comfort I haven't earned.

I build them the way Haru would. That is the part that makes my skin crawl — I can hear the shape of his thinking in mine, careful, step by step: *a real test needs a result you can't explain away.* Telling a co-op man "take the other end" proves nothing; he would have taken the other end anyway. For a week I do those anyway — little deniable nothings, "hand me the hook," "mind the ice" — and get nothing but maybes and the faint coin taste, spending pieces of myself to learn nothing.

A real test needs someone doing a thing they would never otherwise do.

Wednesday, on the mending frames, Jun is fighting the co-op's worst net — the one everybody hates, the one with the tar-stiff panel that takes two men and a grudge — and doing it alone, because the other netman is out and Jun is constitutionally incapable of leaving a job for the tide. His hands are torn up. He has waved off my help twice, cheerful, *you've got your own frame, boss counts my blisters not yours.* And the sentence assembles itself behind my teeth before I've asked it to. Clean. Perfect. Four words, no exits: *Let me help you.*

It would work. That's the thing. It isn't a test I might fail — I can feel the shape of it, feel that it is smooth all the way around, and it would go into the kindest man on this pier and move his *no* out of the way like furniture, and he would never know anything had happened. He would just — change his mind. People change their minds all the time.

I stand there with it loaded and look at him — at the radish fans in the lunch cloth, at the thumb he taught mine to be smarter than — and I swallow it. Physically. It goes down like a stone.

Not because it is cruel. Because it isn't. Because it is *help*, and it is still me reaching into my friend's head and rearranging the furniture, and if I let myself start with the kind ones I already know exactly where that ends. Every rep is a person. Jun doesn't get to be a rep.

I help him anyway, the ordinary way, by ignoring his waving-off and grabbing the tar panel, and he calls me a mule and splits the lunch, and I sit there eating Hanae's rice with my unspent sentence dissolving in my mouth like a coin I almost paid with.

I have already drawn the line: nobody I love. Nobody kind. It doesn't occur to me yet that a line like that has another edge to it, and the other edge is a permission.

So I look for someone who isn't either.

The buyer from the city comes Thursdays. Suit trousers and rubber boots, drives down from the prefecture road, and the whole co-op hates him the same way you hate weather — he shorts the posted price, every week, and every week the foreman argues and loses, because the buyer's truck is the only truck, and everyone's crates rot the same speed. Nobody loves him. Nobody calls him kind. He is perfect for a test.

Thursday, he's mid-shortchange — foreman's neck going red, Jun's mouth doing its flat patient line — and I'm stacking behind the scale, close enough. Low, under the argument, aimed at the back of his head like a thrown hook:

"Pay the posted price. Don't argue."

The coin taste comes up hard this time, a proper mouthful of it, and my nose prickles high up behind the bridge, and the buyer stops mid-sentence.

Stops. Blinks at the chalkboard. And pays the posted price — counts it out flat, no argument, while the foreman stands there with his rebuttal dying in his mouth and the whole shed goes quiet at the miracle. The buyer looks confused doing it. That is the detail that sticks. He watches his own hands count the money like a man watching rain fall out of a clear sky, mutters something about fixing the numbers next week, and drives off.

I know what it was before anyone else does. It was clean. Name the act, close the exits. *Pay the posted price. Don't argue.* No pronouns, no gaps, nothing for the machine to turn over and drop. Two weeks of whispering in the dark and I have finally played a whole note, and it has gone through a grown man like current through wire.

I make it to the ice room before the nosebleed properly starts.

The co-op talks about it for two days after that. Miracle, they say. Even misers wake up human once.

---

Here is what it costs, since I'm the only one who knows.

The blood, first — not a trickle, this time. I sit on a crate in the ice room with my head forward, pinching, and it runs through my fingers for longer than it should, bright on the wet floor, and when it finally stops, my throat feels the way it felt after the roof: scraped, paid out, like the voice is a rope and something heavy just went down it too fast.

Then the pulse in the skull — behind the eyes, a slow flat drumbeat that lasts till evening.

Then the part with no name. That night, lying in the dark across from Haru's breathing, I keep my mouth shut on purpose. Not practicing. Not even dry-firing. Just listening to my own throat scrape when I swallow, and wondering what the weight is taking out of me when it leaves — because it goes into people, and I am also a person, and I don't know what talking is doing to whoever's left in here after I use it.

And underneath all of it — under the blood and the drum and the fear, holding them up like a piling — the true cost, the one I would never say at Sugi's table:

I feel good.

When the buyer's hands start counting. When the shed goes quiet. Two weeks of being a scraped-out refugee kid who checks doorways, and for one second on that dock I am the reason the world goes *right* instead of wrong — the miser pays, the foreman wins, Jun's crates make money — and something in my chest lifts like a gull getting the wind, and it has not fully come down since, and I hate it, and I go looking for it again every time I practice.

That's the truth, both halves of it: it works when it's clean, and part of me is glad, and I can't tell anymore which of those is the dangerous one.

---

Mira knows something. I keep turning it over and I can't find who told her — which means nobody did, which means she is just watching that closely.

Friday she is at Sugi's gate when I come back from the wall crew. She falls in step. For one lane's length she says nothing. Then:

"Third nosebleed this week. Ice room Monday. The wall yesterday. And don't bother lying — Harada saw you, and Harada tells my aunt everything. It's her whole retirement."

"Dry summer," I say.

"It's the wettest June in nine years." Mild. Watching the lane ahead. "The buyer paid the posted price on Thursday. First time in two years. Jun says a saint did it. The foreman is telling people his arguing finally landed."

"Maybe it did."

"Maybe." We walk. Her voice stays the same size the whole way, which is somehow worse than an accusation. "My aunt gets deliveries from three towns up the coast. Drivers talk when they think nobody's listening. There's a story going around from Kōgetsu — about the night it fell. A checkpoint sergeant walked away from his own post in the middle of the evacuation, rifle and all, because somebody *told him to go home*. The way they tell it, he did. Three days' walk. Turned up at his mother's door still holding the rifle."

She stops at the bathhouse corner, where her road splits off mine.

"It's probably nothing," she says. "Evacuations grow stories. But the Federation doesn't think it's nothing. There's a man at the harbor office this week who isn't there for the registry. He doesn't stamp anything. He just listens."

The lane is very quiet. Down at Sugi's end, the plum tree moves.

"Why tell me?"

"Because you get nosebleeds. Because you're terrible at looking innocent. And because Sugi's floor has enough grief on it already." She shifts her aunt's parcels up her hip — ordinary as anything, seventeen years old, talking about rising water like tide charts. "Whatever you're doing at night on the north wall — Harada, remember — do it where the town can't see you. Or better: stop."

She goes her road. I stand at the corner a while.

I don't stop.

Not because of the lift in the chest, whatever else that lift is doing to me. Because that night I finally write the sentence out — the one I've been circling since the roof, the reason I whisper into the dark until my nose bleeds. On the roof I had one throw and I made it with a dirty sentence. *Let her go* — words we'd been tossing around the arcade all night, worn so smooth they could mean anything, and the machine took the meaning I didn't want.

Clean, I know the sentence now. Built the careful way, Haru's way, no gaps, no exits.

*Bring her onto the roof and set her down.*

Not *let her go* — that opens a hand over empty air. This names the place, the act, the end of it. That's all it needed. One clean sentence and Ryn would have been alive.

So I practice. Because the world is full of piers and trucks and lists, and the next time it hands me half a second, I am not wasting it on a dirty sentence.
