# Chapter 4 — Two Weeks

Two weeks in Nagihama and the salt gets into everything — the futons, the hems of our borrowed clothes, even the quiet between me and Kaito.

Sugi puts us to work because work is how she keeps a house standing. I mend nets on the drying frames until my fingers smell like tar. Kaito hauls water from the well and stacks wood and takes whatever job does not require him to speak. His voice comes back in scraps — a word here, a sentence there — but it still sounds scraped.

Mira comes by most days. She brings rice when the sack runs low. She fixes the back shutter with a spare hinge from her aunt's shed. She sits on the step with Sugi and talks about the shrine gym filling up with mats, or about a fisherman who came in with Haiga dust on his boots and would not say which ward he had left.

Once I catch her watching Kaito from the doorway while he splits kindling. When she notices me she says, "Make him drink something warm. His color's wrong," and goes to fill the kettle herself.

"I'm fine," Kaito says without looking up.

"You're the color of old rice," Mira says. "Drink anyway."

He takes the cup.

---

On a Thursday the town gets a gray-green truck.

It parks at the west pier like it owns the boards. A clipboard. Two soldiers. A lieutenant who is not Kanzaki — younger, louder, sweat in the wrong places — reading names off a list while people in the shrine grounds stare at their own hands.

Sugi makes us stay inside.

"You don't have papers that match this town yet," she says. "And I don't need my plum tree decorated with questions."

"What are they doing?" Kaito asks from the window slit.

"Counting," Sugi says. "Federation always counts before it takes."

We watch until the truck leaves with three men who were sitting under the shrine eaves an hour before. Kaito's knuckles are white on the sill. He does not say anything else.

Mira comes after dark with miso and news.

"They're gone for tonight," she says, setting the jar on the counter. "My aunt says assessment of refugee flow. Temporary. For their safety." She makes a face at the last part. "They'll be back on a schedule. Tuesdays, maybe. Pier side."

"Tuesdays," I repeat.

"Pier side," she says again, and looks at Kaito — not like she wants anything from him, only because he is still standing by the window. "You should keep your head down. Both of you."

"We will," I say.

Kaito's mouth twitches like he might argue, then he doesn't. He takes the miso carefully and says, "Thanks," in that scraped voice.

"Don't thank me for miso," Mira says. "Thank me if I find eggs tomorrow. The stall's been empty since the truck started parking there."

"Eggs," Kaito says, almost like he's testing whether his voice still works for ordinary words.

"If the stall has any." She looks at Sugi. "I'll come by after the morning line."

---

The next evening the house feels too small and the pier feels too watched, so we walk the sea wall.

Wind comes sideways off the flats. Kaito has his hands in the pockets of a jacket that used to be someone's uncle's. For a while we just walk.

"You're still not saying it," he says finally. Not loud.

"Saying what?"

"That it wasn't my fault." He stops and looks at the horizon where the east still glows wrong. "I can feel you not saying it. Every time I open my mouth. I told him to let her go, Haru. I said the words. And then he did."

I have practiced this answer for two weeks and it still will not come out clean.

"I saw his hand open," I say. "I don't know how to make that into something that wasn't you."

He laughs once, short and ugly. "Yeah. That's what I thought."

"I'm not blaming you."

"You're not forgiving me either." He says it without heat, which is worse. "It's fine. I wouldn't either."

"That isn't—"

"I'm not sure how long I'm staying," he says. "One more night. Maybe more. I just need you to hear that you're still my best friend. Even if you can't say the other thing."

My throat hurts. "You're still my best friend too."

He knocks his shoulder into mine, careful. "Good. Then stop looking at me like I'm about to disappear when you blink."

Mira finds us on the way back with a paper bag that smells like fried croquettes from the cart by the depot.

"Sugi-san said you'd gone walking," she says. "These were going cold. Waste is a sin in a house that feeds refugees." She holds the bag out. When Kaito doesn't take it, she puts it in my hands and falls into step with us. "Pier's quiet tonight. Truck's gone. You can breathe for a day."

"You keep track of the truck?" I ask.

"Everyone keeps track of the truck. It's the only schedule this town has left." She glances at Kaito. "Your voice sounds better."

"Does it."

"A little. Don't thank me. I didn't do anything except bring towels and bother you about water."

"You bother everyone about water," Kaito says.

"Someone has to." She smiles, then points at the bag. "Eat those before Sugi-san decides I'm feeding you wrong."

At the gate she squeezes my elbow once, friendly, tells Kaito to sleep with the window cracked for the salt air, and goes down the lane toward her aunt's light.

Inside, Sugi has left rice under a cloth. Kaito eats standing up.

"Tuesday," he says, almost to himself. "She said the pier. Tuesdays."

"We're staying inside on Tuesdays," I say.

He looks at me over the rim of the bowl. For a second something sharper moves behind his eyes — not grief, not the old grin.

"Yeah," he says. "Sure, Haru."

He finishes the rice, washes the bowl, and lies down on his futon facing the wall.
