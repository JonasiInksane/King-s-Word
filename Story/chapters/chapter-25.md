# Chapter 25 — Soft Lobby

The truck drives most of the day and picks up four more tags on the way.

A woman who argued with a requisition officer. Two brothers off a farm that couldn't prove it was theirs anymore. A boy of maybe thirteen who cries for the first hour with his whole face and then stops all at once, which is worse. Nobody in the truck talks much. The seed-cap man sleeps sitting up, or does a very good impression of a man who can.

Late afternoon, the canvas goes bright, then shadowed, then bright again — tree line, cut, tree line — and then the truck slows for a gate and I get my first look at where we are going through the gap above the tailgate.

It doesn't look like a prison. It looks like a school that won an award — three stories of pale concrete and long windows set in mown grass, with a flagpole and a paved forecourt and planted saplings still tied to their stakes. Then the truck turns and the rest of it comes into view: the double fence you don't see from the road, the wire's slow curve inward at the top, and a low windowless wing running back from the main building like the handle on a pan.

A signboard faces the gate, white on official blue: **SHIZUHARA CIVIL PROTECTION AND RELOCATION CENTER.**

"Relocation," Riku reads, flat. "That's a lot of fence for a travel agency."

---

The lobby is glass and floor wax.

They walk the seven of us in through doors that open by themselves, into a space with the proportions of a bank — high ceiling, a long counter, recycled air that smells of disinfectant with something boiled underneath it. Behind the counter, clerks in civilian gray. Above them, a mezzanine office with glass walls, so whoever works up there can see the whole lobby without standing up.

A man is waiting for us in the middle of the floor, and I take him for an administrator until I see the soldiers' posture change around him.

He is fifty-something and built like a headmaster, in uniform but soft-shouldered, reading glasses pushed up into gray hair. The tape on his chest says HABU. He waits until we are lined up — not barked into line; guided, one hand on a shoulder here and there, the way you seat guests — and then he speaks in a voice pitched for a smaller room, so that everyone leans in a little to hear him. I understand within one sentence that the leaning-in is the point.

"Good evening. I'm Colonel Habu. This building is my responsibility, which means that as of an hour ago, so are you." He looks down the line, and his eyes are attentive and unhurried, and they stop on each face just long enough to be felt. "You've had a frightening day. Some of you are angry, and I'd like to say that's fair. The road has been unkind and the paperwork has been worse. Here is what I can offer: a bed, hot food, a doctor if you need one, and a process. The process is slow, and I won't insult you by pretending otherwise, but it does end, and it ends better for people who let it work." A small smile, self-deprecating, real-looking. "Rules are posted in every corridor. There are only four. You'll find none of them are cruel."

The crying boy — the thirteen-year-old — has started again, quietly. Habu notices before the guards do. He crosses to him, crouches to the boy's height, and speaks too low for me to hear, and whatever he says makes the kid nod and scrub his face with his sleeve. Habu straightens, rests a hand briefly on the boy's head, and turns to the intake counter.

"He hasn't eaten since morning," he tells the clerk, in exactly the voice he used for the rest of it. "Add him to inventory before dinner, please, so he eats under his own number."

The clerk types. Habu said *inventory* the same way he said *hot food* — soft, practical, like a boy is a number you add so dinner can find him. The bed and the doctor and the four polite rules are still in the room. That word is what they are for.

Riku has gone very still beside me. He heard it too.

---

Processing takes the rest of the evening, one of us at a time, in a row of small rooms off the lobby.

Fingers on a glass plate. A photograph face-on and side-on. Height, weight, a doctor with cold hands who looks at Riku's forearm for a long moment, writes something, and wraps it properly for the first time since the pine road — which Riku accepts with the specific bad grace of a man being done a favor by a building he intends to hate.

Then the booth.

It is the size of a bus-stop shelter, padded inside like a recording studio, with a stool and a microphone and a card taped at eye level. The technician is a young woman who has said her instructions so many times the words have worn smooth.

"Sit. Face the microphone. State your family name, given name, and district of registration, then read the sentence on the card in your normal speaking voice. Don't whisper, don't shout, don't rush."

The card says: *I understand the rules of this facility and I agree to cooperate with its staff.*

I look at the microphone, and the microphone looks back, and a thing I have no name for turns over slowly in my stomach. Every desk on this road wanted my papers. This is the first one that wants my *voice* — wants it kept, wants it on file, the way Sato kept stamps. I think of a booth like this one somewhere with Kaito on the stool, and the thought is so bad I have to put it down before it shows on my face.

"Is there a reason it's recorded?" I ask. Normal, curious, nothing leaning on it.

"Voice is harder to fake than paper." She doesn't look up from her list. "Read the card, please."

I read the card in the flattest, most ordinary voice I own — the one for gates and clerks, with nothing under it, nothing leaning on anyone. The machine draws my sentence as a small green mountain range on its screen, thinks about it, and files it away. No light goes red. Nobody comes running. The technician is already looking at her list for the next name.

The not-happening should feel like relief. Instead it walks out of the booth with me: somewhere in this building is a machine that listens to sentences for a living, and someone taught it what to listen *for*.

At the forms desk, a tired clerk works down a checklist with Riku while I wait my turn on the bench.

"Prior civil service?" the clerk asks. "Corps registration, defense auxiliary — you're the right age band for the volunteer program."

"No," Riku says.

One word, and I have heard Riku say no a hundred ways — mocking, cheerful, weaponized. This is none of them. It is flat and closed like a shutter coming down, and the clerk glances up at it, and even he, at the end of a long shift, notices there is a room behind the word that nobody is invited into. He marks the box and moves on. Riku's jaw stays set for three more questions.

I don't ask. The road taught me that much — he'll sell it when he's ready.

---

They walk us to C wing after dinner, down a corridor with a painted line on the floor, and on the way we cross the building's other life.

A door opens somewhere ahead and a line of kids comes through the junction at right angles to us — twenty of them, maybe, thirteen to seventeen, in clean clothes with cloth armbands, walking two by two behind a guard the way a school class walks behind a teacher. They look fed. They look *bright* — that is the word my head reaches for and I don't like it. A girl near the front waves at us, actually waves, and calls out that dinner is curry on Thursdays, and their guard smiles at her indulgently and tells her to keep the line.

They pass out of sight down their corridor. Ours keeps going.

"Volunteers," says the guard walking us, in answer to nobody. "A and B block. Good kids. Behave and the review board notices, that's all I'll say." He says it warmly, the way you repeat something you have decided to believe.

Riku looks straight ahead at nothing. His jaw is set the same way it was at the forms desk.

---

C wing is bunks in bays of eight, a wash room that smells of chlorine over rust, and a wall where the four rules are posted exactly as promised. None of them are cruel. All of them are about lines, counts, and doors.

We have been in the bay ten minutes when the mayor arrives.

He is mid-twenties or so, with hair somehow still pomaded in a building that confiscates belts, and he introduces himself as Ōhashi before anyone asks — first to Riku, who ignores him, then to me, who doesn't, which he takes as a lifelong friendship being founded.

"Aoyama, was it? Good bay, this one. I had them move a snorer out last month — I know the day-shift corporal, we have an understanding." He produces a school notebook, fat with pencil, and taps it. "Intake numbers, bunk assignments, who's due at review. I keep track of everybody in C. Somebody has to. Buildings like this, the paper forgets people, but *I* don't forget people." He says it loudly enough for the nearest three bays to benefit from knowing it.

It costs me two questions and a respectful silence to learn the shape of him: he has been here five months, he is owed favors by half of C wing and collects them in public, and under the vanity he is genuinely useful — he knows which guard trades cigarettes for quiet, which clerk loses forms on purpose out of spite, and what day the review board actually sits, as opposed to the day the corridor rules say it does.

"And the east wing?" I ask it the boring way, the way I'd ask about bus schedules — half attention, folding my blanket while I talk.

"East wing's specials. War paperwork, flagged cases. Different food, different guards — the guards down there don't chat." He lowers his voice to the register of a man giving away stock for free and enjoying it. "They keep the count separate too. I've seen the wall board at the junction when they change shifts. C is forty-one, you two make forty-three. A and B together run about two hundred. East wing never has a number on the board. Just a letter." He frowns, for the first time looking his age instead of like a mayor. "Which is strange, when you think about it, because the whole building runs on numbers. I asked a corporal once and got corridor duty for a week."

He stops. Straightens his hair. Looks like a man who just heard himself talk too much to a stranger.

Lights go to half at the second bell. Somewhere beyond the wash-room wall, a door the size of a garage opens — I feel it in the floor more than hear it — and an engine idles in and settles. Late for deliveries. The kitchen is on the other side of the building; Ōhashi's tour covered that too.

I lie on a bunk that smells of other people's summers and listen. The engine idles a long time. Then gates, then gears, then gone — heavier leaving than it came, or I am imagining that, lying in the dark with a wired-on number where my name should be.

"Ōhashi," I say quietly, across the bay. He isn't asleep either. Vanity sleeps badly in buildings with wall boards. "The night trucks. Where do they go?"

There is a long pause, and when he answers, for the first time all evening he doesn't sound like the mayor of anything.

"I don't sell that one," he says. "Go to sleep."
