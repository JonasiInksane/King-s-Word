# Chapter 26 — Volunteers

Morning in C wing starts with a bell that does not care who slept.

They put us on corridor detail before breakfast — mop buckets, a strip of waxed floor from the bay doors to the junction where the painted line splits toward A and B. The day-shift corporal is a woman with a clipboard and a voice that has already decided we are late. She wants the floor dry before the A-block line comes through. She wants quiet. She wants her name kept off any complaint about wet tile.

"You drip on my junction, you polish it again," she says. "You talk while you work, you polish it again. Clear?"

"Clear," I say.

Riku takes the mop without answering. His wrapped forearm is stiff; he switches hands when she isn't looking. Ōhashi appears halfway through the strip with his notebook open, counting heads as if the mop water were a meeting he convened.

"Forty-three still," he announces, loud enough for the corporal. "Nobody wandered overnight. Good bay, Aoyama. I told you."

The corporal does not thank him. She marks something and walks the junction. Ōhashi looks pleased anyway.

I put my head down and push wax. The recycled air tastes like boiled cabbage and bleach. Somewhere past the junction a line of kids laughs — real laugh, not the careful kind — and a guard tells them to keep moving, fondly, the way you tell a class not to run.

Riku's mop slows for half a second. Then he starts again.

---

Breakfast is rice and something gray that claims to be fish. We eat in a hall with A and B on one side and C on the other, a painted stripe down the middle nobody crosses without a reason. The armband kids sit brighter than the food. Clean sleeves. Cloth bands with neat stitches. A boy about fourteen argues cheerfully with his neighbor about Thursday curry and whether the review board likes people who smile.

"Good kids," a kitchen guard says to nobody in particular, wiping a tray. "You C lot could learn. Behave, get noticed, get options."

Riku sets his chopsticks down hard enough that the tray jumps. He picks them up again before anyone writes it down as a problem. I watch his jaw do the same thing it did at the forms desk and in the corridor last night. I don't ask across the table. The hall is full of ears.

Ōhashi slides onto the bench beside me with a cup of tea he somehow has and we do not.

"Don't stare at A-block," he says, cheerful. "Makes the guards think you're ambitious. Ambition in C gets you moved to a bay with snorers. Trust me."

"I'm not ambitious."

"Good. Ambition is for people with armbands." He taps the notebook. "I keep C honest. You keep mopping. Everybody stays counted."

"The truck last night," I say, quiet. "You still don't sell that?"

His smile thins. "I still don't. Drink your water, Aoyama. Tea is for friends with seniority."

---

After breakfast they send three of us — me, Riku, and a seed-cap man from the intake truck — to the service court behind the kitchen to haul empty crates to a loading bay that smells like old ice and diesel. The bay door is shut. Daylight. No night truck. Just a clerk with a stamp and a boy from B-block stacking trays with an armband and a smile that never quite turns off.

"You C?" the B-boy asks me, not unkind. "First week?"

"Second day."

"Stick the corridor work. Review board likes clean floors. My cousin got noticed that way — he's on kitchen now. Hot food twice." He says it like a tip you pay forward. "Volunteers get first pick of the soft jobs. You should put your name in if they open the list."

Riku is behind me with a crate. He goes still — the kind of still that means he heard a word he hates.

"We're not on that list," I say.

"Yet," the boy says, and goes back to stacking.

The clerk stamps our work chit and tells us to bring the next load faster. I use the quiet voice on him — shoulders down, ordinary, the tone that makes people pause when they should not pause — and ask if the bay opens at night for deliveries.

He almost answers. His mouth starts the shape of a yes. Then he blinks, looks at his stamp, and says, "Kitchen schedule isn't your business. Move the crates."

It almost worked. My stomach goes cold the way it did in the booth.

Riku watches me sideways as we walk back with empty hands. He does not ask what I tried. He clicks his tongue once, short, and keeps walking.

---

They give C-wing an hour in the yard after the midday count — a rectangle of mown grass inside the inner fence, and the blank wall of the east wing with nothing looking back. A and B come out in a different slot; we overlap for ten minutes at the gate while they come in and we head for the door.

Armbands. Laughing. A girl waves again, the same one from last night. Her guard smiles.

Riku stops walking.

Not dramatic. Just stopped, in the middle of the line, so the boy behind him bumps his shoulder and swears. A yard guard starts toward us. I put a hand on Riku's sleeve.

"Move," I say. "Later."

He moves. Inside, in the wash room where the chlorine covers most talk, he runs water over his hands longer than hands need and stares at the drain.

"Those bands," he says. Not looking at me. "We had those."

"Your town?"

"Ridge town — smaller than Kuroishi." He shuts the water off, then keeps talking like he already regrets starting. "When the quota hit, they pulled kids into civil-defense and sewed bands on us. Called it volunteer so they didn't have to write our names down. We walked the perimeter on asset sweeps. Neighbor kids on the line — a runner won't hurt a neighbor's kid. That was what they sold us. They put rifles in our hands that month."

I wait. After the yard, he can't keep it shut.

"A boy ran my stretch," Riku says. "He was fourteen. Somebody had flagged him for being too close to an asset. A soldier shouted to stop him and I fired." He dries his hands on his shirt, rough. "Later they decided the flag didn't hold. One afternoon of theirs. He doesn't get any more afternoons."

He does not say the boy's name. I do not ask for it.

"That's why you said no at the forms desk," I say.

He looks at me then, crooked tooth not showing. "They don't chase volunteers who fold the band and walk. You were never here — that was the other line. Now this place puts the bands on kids and calls them good, feeds them curry talk, and tells C to learn from it." He laughs once, no humor. "I'm not learning. And if some clerk opens a volunteer list under my number, I am going to make a problem, and you are standing too close to be an accident." He tips his chin at the door. "You wanted to know. Now you know. Come on — corporal wants the junction dry before A-block afternoon."

---

Afternoon puts us back on the junction.

We are on the second wax pass when a man in civilian gray from the mezzanine — not Habu; younger, with a tablet — stops our corporal and points at Riku's wrap, then at me, then at the tablet.

"C intake from the pine-road scoop," he says. "Age band fits the volunteer expansion. Colonel wants soft moves where possible — offer the track before review. Kitchen and yard open slots." He looks at Riku with the polite interest of someone filling a form. "Nakahara. You declined prior service at intake. That was a forms answer. This is a real offer. A-block has bunks. Hot food. The review board sees volunteers first."

Riku's hands are on the mop. The knuckles go white.

"No," he says.

Same flat shutter as the forms desk. The mezzanine man raises his eyebrows.

"You haven't heard the terms?"

"I heard volunteer," Riku says. "That's the whole term."

The corporal shifts her weight. She wants her floor finished. She also wants her name off this. The mezzanine man looks at me.

"Aoyama. You're younger. Same offer. Your friend can stay in C if he's attached to the mop."

I feel the careful voice rising — the one that almost opened the bay clerk. I stop it before it leaves my mouth. This man already listens too easily.

"We'll stay in C," I say. Ordinary. Boring. Nothing under it. "We're not looking for a track."

"That's a short-sighted answer." He marks the tablet anyway. "Refusal noted. Refusals get reviewed same as acceptances — just slower, and with fewer friends in the kitchen." He glances at Riku's forearm wrap. "And Nakahara — next time a yard guard tells you to keep the line moving, you keep it moving. Standing still in a volunteer gate looks like a statement."

He leaves. The corporal exhales through her nose.

"Finish the strip," she says. "Both of you. And if either of you makes me interesting to the mezzanine again, you polish until second bell."

We finish the strip. Wax. Quiet. Riku does not speak until we are back in the bay with the four polite rules on the wall.

Ōhashi is there with the notebook open, frowning at a page.

"Problem," he says, without his mayor voice. "Midday count on the junction board said C forty-two. I have forty-three names. Someone is wrong, and it isn't me."

"Who's missing on their side?" I ask.

"They don't list names on the board. Just the number." He taps the pencil hard enough to dent the paper. "If their number drops and mine doesn't, either a clerk can't add, or somebody walked a body through a door that doesn't show on C's sheet." He looks at me, then at Riku, then shuts the notebook. "I don't sell trucks. I sell counts. And today their count is short one."

Riku sits on his bunk and unwraps the end of the bandage to check the bruise. Easier to look at than the board.

"They're counting wrong," he says. "Or they're counting something else."

Outside the wash-room wall, far off, a bay door rolls — daylight this time, a delivery that belongs on a schedule. Still, my shoulders go tight until the engine stays and men shout about ice, ordinary and loud.

Ōhashi stands, hair already being corrected with his palm.

"Tomorrow's morning count," he says. "I'm walking the junction when they write it. Aoyama, you want to be useful, you come. Bring the quiet one if he can keep his feet moving when the armbands go past."

"I'll come," I say.

Riku lies back and puts his good arm over his eyes.

"I'll come," he says to the underside of his elbow. "If anybody offers me a band again, I'm going to need a witness that I said no the boring way."
