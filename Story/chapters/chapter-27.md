# Chapter 27 — Count

We walk the junction at first bell, before the wax crew, because Ōhashi wants to see the board when the numbers go up wet.

The wall board hangs where the painted lines cross — A left, B right, C back the way we came, and a blank strip toward the east wing with no arrow anyone bothers to repaint. A guard with a tablet stands under it, stylus tapping, while a second guard reads off a clipboard in a bored rhythm. Forty-one. Forty-two. He stops, frowns, starts over.

Ōhashi has his notebook open against his forearm. Riku stands half a step behind me, feet planted, eyes on the east strip as if it might grow a number if he stares hard enough.

"C," the clipboard guard says. "Forty-two."

"Write forty-two," the tablet guard answers, and lifts the marker.

"Forty-three," says a voice from the east-side corridor.

Not loud. Not polite. The kind of correction you give when the wrong total offends you personally.

A girl steps into the junction light before either guard has turned all the way. Seventeen or eighteen, taller than most of the A-block kids, short practical dark hair, war-issue shirt worn like she picked it. A pale scar runs across the knuckles of her left hand when she reaches for the tablet — not hidden, not shown off. She takes it out of the guard's hand the way you take a ladle from someone burning soup, taps twice, and finishes the tally herself.

"C is forty-three," she says. "You skipped the late intake from the pine road — two tags, one bay. They're on the night sheet. A is ninety-six, not ninety-four; you missed the kitchen runners coming back through B." She holds the tablet so both guards can see the screen. "East wing stays a letter. Don't invent a number for it because the column looks lonely."

The tablet guard's mouth opens. Closes. The clipboard guard laughs once, short, like he has lost this argument before.

"Serizawa," he says. "You're supposed to be on the east side of the line."

"I am on the east side of the line." She tips her chin at the blank strip. "Your board is on my way to breakfast. If you hang bad counts in the road, I fix them. That's not a hobby. Bad counts move people."

She writes C-43 on the board in a hand that does not shake, sets the marker in the tray, and only then looks at the three of us from C as if we were furniture that had started talking.

Ōhashi recovers first. His mayor smile comes back on a delay.

"I had forty-three," he says, tapping his notebook. "I've been saying—"

"I don't care what you've been saying," she says. "I care what the board shows when the shift changes." She glances at Riku's wrap, then at my tag. "Pine-road scoop. You're the ones the mezzanine tried to band yesterday."

"How do you know that?" Riku says.

"Corridor duty has ears. Also the mezzanine man complains in a voice built for glass offices." She almost smiles. It is not soft. "I'm Serizawa Mei. You can keep standing in the junction looking grateful, or you can move before this becomes a meeting."

The tablet guard clears his throat. "Serizawa. Back to east. Now. And next time you touch my tablet I write you up."

"Write carefully," Mei says. "Your nines look like fours."

She is already walking. The east corridor takes her.

---

Ōhashi wants to follow her with thanks. Riku wants to go back to the bay. I want someone who cares about wrong numbers to explain why east is only a letter.

"I'm going to be late for mop," I tell the corporal when she finds us still at the junction. I use the ordinary voice — not the one that almost opened the bay clerk. "We were checking the count. It's fixed."

She looks at the board, sees C-43, and decides we are less interesting than wet tile. "Junction. Now. Serizawa isn't your friend."

"Understood."

We wax. Ōhashi narrates his own correctness for twenty meters and then remembers he has a day-shift corporal to flatter elsewhere. That leaves me and Riku with a stretch of floor and the sound of A-block lining up somewhere we can't see.

"She's not a volunteer," Riku says, quiet, pushing wax.

"East wing."

"Different clothes. Different mouth. Volunteers don't correct guards in public — not the ones who still flinch." He glances at the east strip as we pass it. "I don't like her. I like that she hates a wrong number. Wrong numbers are how bands get sewn."

"You don't know that."

"I know enough." He puts his head down. "If you chase her for truck stories, do it without me smiling. I have a refusal on a tablet already."

---

They put C on supply-run to the east junction after midday — not into the wing, just to the painted stop line with a cart of boiled towels and soap. A guard who does not chat checks our tags, takes the cart handle, and tells us to wait. Waiting means looking at a door with a letter on it and no window.

Mei comes out through that door with two empty crates and a list, stops when she sees us, and sighs like we are a chore she did not schedule.

"You're the grateful ones," she says. "Still here."

"We brought towels," I say.

"I can see towels." She dumps the crates on our cart without asking and holds out the list. "Sign that you handed them across the line. If you write the wrong bay I'll walk them back to you myself, and you'll redo the corridor."

I sign. Riku does not. He looks at her face.

"You always steal tablets," he asks, "or only in the morning?"

"I don't steal. I finish." She tips the list at him. "You're the one who said no to the band. Corridor said the quiet boy said no too. That's two refusals. Mezzanine notices pairs."

"Good," Riku says.

"It isn't good. It's visible." She looks at me. "You. The one who asked a bay clerk about night deliveries yesterday until he almost answered. Don't do that at this door. East guards don't do almost. They write."

My stomach goes cold. "Who told you that?"

"The clerk has a mouth when he's off the stamp. He thought it was funny." She is not laughing. "I fix counts so people don't get moved for ghost numbers. I don't fix boys who lean on strangers for truck routes. What do you want from me that towels don't cover?"

I keep the careful voice out of it. "The board. East is a letter. Night trucks leave from somewhere. Ōhashi won't say where. I want to know if the short count yesterday was a clerk error or a door."

Mei studies me for a long second. Riku shifts his weight — not leaving, not helping.

"Yesterday's short count was a clerk error," she says. "I checked the night sheet. Your two tags were late-posted. Nobody walked out of C." She pauses. "That isn't the same as nobody walking out of the building."

"The trucks," I say.

"I don't sell that one either." Same flat no as Ōhashi, sharper. "Ask me about counts. Ask me about which guard can't add. Ask me why A-block smiles on Thursdays. Don't ask me for bay doors on a towel run."

"Then what do you want?" Riku says, blunt. "You corrected the board in front of us. That wasn't free."

Mei almost looks amused. "I want you to stop making the junction interesting. Every time C refuses a band and then loiters under the board, someone upstairs adds a note. Notes become reviews. Reviews become moves." She nods at the letter-door. "I live on the other side of that. When C gets messy, east gets quieter, and quieter is not safer."

"So we owe you boring," I say.

"You owe me a clean board and your feet on your side of the line." She takes the signed list back. "If the morning count is wrong again, I'll be there. If you bring me a wrong count on purpose to get a conversation, I'll give you corridor duty and I won't make it a joke."

"Serizawa," the silent guard says from the door.

"Coming." She picks up one empty crate as if she still needs a reason to turn. Then, over her shoulder, not quite looking at us: "Pine-road boys. If the mezzanine offers the band again, say no the same way twice. Different nos read like a negotiation."

She goes. The letter-door shuts. The guard points us back toward C with the cart.

---

Ōhashi intercepts us at the C turn, notebook already open.

"Well?" he says. "Did the east girl admit I was right?"

"She fixed the board," I say. "She said yesterday was a late post, not a missing body."

He brightens, then catches himself and smooths his hair. "Of course it was a late post. I don't miss bodies." He lowers his voice. "She's Serizawa. East. War paper. Don't make her a project, Aoyama. East projects end with fewer friends and the same trucks."

"I'm not making a project."

"You're making a face like a project." He taps the notebook. "Morning count tomorrow. I'm there. You're there if you can be dull. Bring Nakahara if he promises not to stop walking when the armbands pass."

Riku clicks his tongue once.

"I'll walk," he says. "I'm not waving at her."

"Nobody asked you to wave," Ōhashi says, and sails off toward a corporal who might still be flattered.

In the bay, Riku sits on his bunk and rewraps the forearm without asking for help. I sit across from him.

"She'll be at the board again tomorrow," he says.

"So will I."

He ties the wrap off with his teeth and his good hand. "Fine. If she starts offering bands, I'm out of the junction. I'll sit through a count. I'm not putting one on."

"She didn't offer."

"Not this morning." He lies back. "Give them a day."

Second bell. Lights halfway down. Far off, past the wash-room wall, a bay door rolls and an engine settles into idle — night schedule this time, not ice shouting. I listen until the gears take it away. Heavier leaving, or the same trick my ears played the first night.

Riku's voice comes out of the dim, dry.

"If the board goes short again," he says, "and she isn't there to call it a clerk error — we don't take Ōhashi's notebook for it. We check the bunks. See who's gone."

"Alright."

"Sleep, Aoyama. First bell doesn't wait."
