# Chapter 28 — Bunks

First bell. We are at the junction before the wax water is warm.

Ōhashi has the notebook open against his forearm the way other people hold a shield. Riku stands half a step behind me, feet planted, eyes on the east strip. The blank letter waits. Mei does not walk out of it.

The tablet guard lifts his marker. The clipboard guard reads in the same bored rhythm as yesterday.

"C," he says. "Forty-two."

Ōhashi's pencil stops.

"Write forty-two," the tablet guard answers, and writes it. Wet black. No correction from the east corridor. No Serizawa taking the tablet out of anyone's hand. A-block gets ninety-six. B gets its number. East stays a letter because nobody invents one for it today, and nobody is here to stop them if they tried.

"That's wrong," Ōhashi says, too loud for a junction with ears. "I have forty-three. I checked at lights. I—"

"Board says forty-two," the tablet guard says, not looking at him. "If your notebook wants a fight, take it to the corporal. Move."

Riku does not look at me. He does not have to. Last night's deal sits between us without words: if the board goes short and she isn't here, we do not take the notebook for it. We check the bunks.

"Ōhashi," I say. "Bay. Now."

He wants to argue with the marker. Vanity always wants the public win. Then he sees our faces and shuts the notebook on his thumb like a man closing a door on his own fingers.

"Fine. Fine. We'll prove it. I don't miss bodies."

---

C-wing smells like chlorine and sleep and the gray fish from yesterday. Eight bunks to a bay. Ours is the third along the wash-room wall. Men sit up as we come through — the day-shift corporal's whistle has already started the corridor line, and half the bay is still pulling on shirts.

"Stay," Ōhashi says, mayor voice cracking. "Nobody leave until I say. I am counting."

He walks the aisle with the notebook open. Name. Bunk. Face. The seed-cap man from the crate run is not on his mattress. The blanket is folded the way Habu's polite rules like blankets folded. The tag hook above the pillow is empty. The space under the bunk where he kept his cracked plastic cup is empty too.

Ōhashi's pencil finds the line and stops.

"Katō," he says. Quiet now. "Katō Hiroshi. Intake from the pine road same truck as you two. Bay three. He was here at second bell. I saw him. He asked me for a cigarette and I told him Tuesday."

Riku crouches and puts his good hand on the mattress as if the bed might still be warm. It isn't.

"He's gone," Riku says.

"Transfer," someone from the next bunk offers, helpful the way people are helpful when they want the counting to stop. "Review board pulls overnight sometimes. Kitchen said—"

"Kitchen doesn't pull people," Ōhashi snaps. Then he hears himself and smooths his hair with a palm that shakes. "I don't miss bodies. The board is wrong. He is on a detail. He is in the wash room. He—"

We check the wash room. Two sinks. One clogged drain. No Katō. We check the corridor strip. The corporal's line is already moving toward breakfast. No seed cap. No cracked cup. No man who complained about crate weight and then laughed at his own complaining.

The day-shift corporal finds us standing in the bay like we have invented a new way to be late.

"Junction," she says. "Now. Or I put all three of you on polish until second bell."

"Katō Hiroshi," Ōhashi says. He holds the notebook out like evidence in a play. "Bay three. Board says forty-two. I have him written. Where is he?"

She looks at the empty bunk. She looks at the notebook. She looks at us the way she looks at wet tile — a problem with a procedure attached.

"Overnight move," she says. "Review track. You don't get a speech about it, Ōhashi. C doesn't get speeches. He was due. They took him. Board is right. Your notebook is behind."

"Due for what?" I ask. Ordinary voice. Shoulders down. Nothing under it. Mei's warning sits in my teeth: east guards write; bay clerks gossip; do not lean.

"Due for review," she says, and the word has the same warm shine the kitchen guard puts on *volunteer*. "Behave, get noticed, get options. Some options happen at night. You want to argue with the board, argue with Habu's office. You want to mop, mop. Empty bunks don't wait for feelings."

Riku's jaw does the forms-desk thing. He does not speak. If he speaks, the word *volunteer* will come out, and the junction will hear it.

Ōhashi opens his mouth. Closes it. For the first time since the lobby, he looks like a man who has been here five months and just learned the building can forget a name he refused to forget.

"Write him gone, then," he says, and his voice is small. "If you're going to be right, be right in my book too."

"I don't write your book," the corporal says. "Junction. All of you."

---

Breakfast is the same rice and the same gray claim of fish. Katō's tray space on our bench stays open until a boy from bay five sits in it without asking. Ōhashi does not eat. He draws a line through a page and keeps staring at the line as if it might apologize.

"Review track," he mutters once, not to us. "They don't pull review at night. Review sits on Thursdays. I know which day. I *know*."

"Eat," Riku says.

"I don't want—"

"Eat anyway. Empty stomachs make loud mistakes."

Ōhashi eats two bites and stops. Across the painted stripe, A-block laughs about Thursday curry. An armband girl waves at nobody in particular.

I keep my eyes on my chopsticks. The careful voice wants to stand up and ask the room where overnight moves go. I leave it sitting. Mei already knows I almost opened a clerk. I am not giving the hall another story.

---

Corridor wax. The corporal puts us on the strip that faces the east blank as if she wants us to look at the letter while we work. Riku pushes water. I push water. Ōhashi does not appear with tea. Vanity is somewhere rewriting a page that used to make him feel necessary.

Midmorning, a mezzanine man in civilian gray — not the band-offer one; older, thinner — walks the junction with a tablet and stops long enough to glance at our tags. He does not offer a band. He marks something and leaves. Visible, Mei said. Pairs get noticed. Being noticed without a band is still being noticed.

"Don't look at him," Riku says.

"I wasn't."

"You were about to ask him something with your face." He does not grin. "Keep the face boring. We already have one empty bunk."

---

Mei finds us at the east supply line after midday, not at the morning board.

They send C with the towel cart again. Same painted stop. Same letter-door with no window. Same silent guard. Mei comes through with a crate of boiled cloth and a mouth already set for an argument she has been having somewhere else.

"You're late," she says, before we can speak. "East held me for a recount on the war sheet. Your junction wrote forty-two without me. I heard it from a runner who can't add."

"Katō Hiroshi," I say. "Bay three. Empty bunk. Corporal called it an overnight review move."

Mei sets the crate down harder than cloth needs.

"Show me the board number when you get back," she says. "If C is forty-two on the wall and forty-two in the bay, the wall isn't lying today." She looks at Riku. "You checked beds?"

"We checked," Riku says.

"Good." Not praise. Confirmation. "Don't check again in front of the corporal. She writes boys who invent missing people, and then those boys get a review of their own."

"He isn't invented," Ōhashi says. He has appeared at the C turn with the notebook shut tight under his arm, hair wrong for the first time. "I had him. Pine-road intake. Seed cap. He owed me a cigarette joke on Tuesday."

Mei studies Ōhashi the way she studies bad tallies — as a thing that will move people if left alone.

"Then stop saying his name in the junction," she says. "Say it in your bay if you have to. Public names make public problems, and public problems on my side of the line get people pulled for recounts they do not come back from." She tips the list at me. "Sign for the towels. And listen carefully, pine-road: I told you yesterday that a clerk error is not the same as nobody walking out of the building. Today somebody walked. That does not mean you get to lean on bay doors and clerks for a map. It means you keep your feet on your side and your mouth ordinary."

"Where do overnight review moves go?" I ask.

She almost answers. I see the shape of it — not kindness, irritation at a stupid question with a real hole under it. Then the silent guard shifts his weight, and Mei's eyes flick to him, and the almost dies.

"I don't sell routes," she says. "I sell clean boards. Your board is clean now. Your bay is short one. Those can both be true." She takes the signed list. "If you chase Katō through the service court in daylight, you will get corridor duty and I will not help you out of it. If you are going to be stupid, be stupid when fewer people are watching."

Riku clicks his tongue once.

"That's not a no," he says.

"It isn't a map either," Mei says. "Move the cart. East is done looking at you for today."

She goes. The letter-door shuts. The silent guard points us back toward C.

Ōhashi walks beside me for ten meters without the mayor voice.

"Tuesday," he says. "He was going to laugh at the cigarette. I don't — I don't lose Tuesdays."

"We know," I say.

"I keep track of everybody in C." He opens the notebook, looks at the struck line, and closes it again. "Somebody has to. If the board can be right while my book is wrong, then the board is counting a different building than the one I sleep in."

Riku glances at him, then at me.

"Second bell," Riku says. "We listen again. And if the bay door opens, we don't ask a clerk where the truck goes. We look."

Ōhashi swallows. Vanity wants to forbid it. The struck name wants something else.

"If you get caught," he says, "I never heard this. If you don't get caught — you tell me if you see him. I am not rewriting Tuesday for a story."

"Alright," I say.

---

We finish the corridor strip. Wax. Quiet. The afternoon count keeps C at forty-two on the wall. Katō's bunk stays empty. Nobody invents him back into the number. Somewhere past the junction an armband line laughs, and a guard tells them fondly not to run.

Second bell is still an hour out when the outer lights slam on.

Not the soft lobby strips. The hard white ones on the west fence — the ones that stay dark unless someone upstairs decides the wire needs to be seen. They throw a hard square of glare across the junction floor through the high window. Boots hit the painted line at a run. A corporal goes past with a rifle up, not a clipboard. He does not look at us. He looks at the west doors like they owe him an answer.

"Perimeter," someone shouts from the mezzanine stair. "West fence. No C in the service court. East locks now."

Ōhashi's notebook is already open. His pencil hangs over the page and does nothing useful.

"That isn't a night truck," Riku says. "Night trucks don't put rifles in the junction."

"I know."

Mei comes through the letter-door before the silent guard can finish locking it. She has no towel cart. Her hands are white on the frame.

"Pine-road," she says, fast, for us and not for the junction. "Do not go to the bay. Do not stand under the board inventing reasons. East is locking because the west wire has company, and when the wire has company every empty bunk becomes a story they write wrong." She looks once at Ōhashi's open notebook. "Keep his name in the bay. Not in the corridor."

"Who is on the fence?" I ask.

"If I knew a name worth saying, I still wouldn't say it with a rifle in the hall." The silent guard's hand finds her shoulder. She shakes it off once, then lets him have it. "Stay on your side of the line. Do not give them a reason to walk anyone else out of C while the fence is lit — you already have one empty bunk they can write any story on."

"Katō isn't—" Riku starts.

"I know what Katō isn't," Mei says. "I also know what a doubled perimeter does to review schedules. Keep his name out of the junction. Do not give them a second problem while they are looking for a first."

The letter-door shuts. The lock turns. Through the high window the west lights stay hard and white. Another pair of boots runs the junction. Somewhere outside a motor turns over that is not the canvas-truck idle we have been listening for — too light, too close to the fence line, cut off like someone killed the engine on purpose.

Ōhashi shuts the notebook, then opens it again to the C list. His hair is wrong. For once he does not fix it.

"Bay is closed," Riku says. "Fence is someone else's fight. So what—"

"So we use their eyes being west," Ōhashi cuts in. He taps the notebook hard enough that the pencil jumps. "Every corporal in this hall is looking at the wire. Nobody is watching who still has a mattress. We check every bunk in C against my book before upstairs invents another review and calls it tidy." He looks at me, then at Riku. "You two know bay three. I take one through four. You take five through eight. Wake them. Match names. If anyone is gone besides Katō, I want it in pencil before a guard writes it in marker."

"And if a corporal comes back mid-count?" Riku asks.

"Then we were doing a hold check like good inventory," Ōhashi says, and there is no mayor smile in it. "I am tired of learning my bay is short from a wall. Move."

---

We move.

Bay three first — our own. Seven heads. Katō's hook still holds his seed-company cap. His cup is still under the bunk. Nobody took the small things a review move usually packs. Riku swears once under his breath and marks nothing; there is nothing to mark except the empty mattress.

"He didn't pack," I say.

"I know," Riku says. "Wake the next bay."

Five has eight. Six has eight, one of them arguing until Ōhashi's voice from the corridor shuts him up. Seven has seven and a boy who tries to claim the eighth bunk is on kitchen detail; we walk him to the wash-room door and find the eighth asleep behind it with a towel over his face. Ōhashi writes the correction without looking pleased. Eight is full.

We meet back at the junction under the high window. The west glare is still on. A second set of floodlights has kicked farther along the wire. The mezzanine voice comes soft through the stairwell:

"All blocks hold. Inventory stays put until the west is clear."

Ōhashi holds the open notebook between us. One struck line. Forty-two living names. No second gap.

"C is short one," he says. "Only Katō. Cap and cup still here. That is not a man who walked to a review with a bag." He closes the book. "I cannot take this to the board while they have rifles in the hall. I can keep it until morning, and if the wall tries to call forty-one, I have paper that says otherwise."

"He left his cap," Riku says. "People who get walked to a real review take their cup. Somebody moved him, or he walked himself toward the noise on the west side. Either way I'm not waiting in bay three for a corporal to explain it tomorrow."

"Mei told us to stay off the bay," I say.

"I'm not going to open the bay door." The motor turns over once more outside — short, close, then dead. Riku looks at the bright window, then at me. "There's a stair by the kitchen that looks down on the service court. We used it when we hauled towels. From up there we can see if a truck is loading, or if the guards are shooting at the fence. That's all I want — eyes on it. Then we come back."

"And if they catch us in the west corridor?"

"Then we say Ōhashi sent us to check the kitchen hold, which is almost true." He glances at Ōhashi. "You going to deny that if they ask?"

Ōhashi's mouth opens, then shuts. He shoves the notebook under his arm.

"If you get caught, you got lost on a hold check," he says. "If you see Katō, you come tell me before you tell a corporal. I am staying in bay three. Somebody has to be here when they count heads again."

"Alright," I say.

Riku is already moving toward the west corridor the corporals just emptied. Bad arm tucked in. Good hand empty.

"Ordinary faces," he says without looking back. "Don't touch any door we can't explain. We look, and we leave."

We take the west corridor at a walk that tries to be boring. The floodlight glare gets brighter with every turn. Behind us Ōhashi stays under the junction board with his pencil and his list. Ahead, the kitchen stair smells like boiled fish and cold air from a door that should be shut.

Riku hits the first step.

"Come on, Aoyama," he says. "Before someone remembers we belong in C."

I go up after him.
