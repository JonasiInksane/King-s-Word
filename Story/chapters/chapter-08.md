# Chapter 8 — Drop It

Orihara's shop backs onto a drying yard, and the drying yard backs onto a net shed, and the net shed is where the lane keeps the things it doesn't explain to visitors.

I found out by accident, the way I find out everything — through arithmetic. Three weeks of doing Orihara's ledger and the numbers kept having a shadow: rice that sold without selling, a column of small sums marked only with single characters, payments in and out that balanced perfectly and connected to nothing. Good books hide things badly from people who like books. When I finally asked, Orihara looked at me for a long moment over her glasses and then, instead of answering, introduced me to Sato.

Sato Kimi is fifty, built like a bollard, and spent thirty years as the harbor office's records clerk before her knees retired her. What she does now, in the net shed, with a locked tin box and a beautiful old hand-stamp collection, is keep the town's second set of paper. A boy from Haiga whose records burned gets a work chit that says he's somebody's cousin. A family the registry would split across two shelters becomes one household with one address. Nothing sold, nothing forged from air — mostly it's real stamps applied with merciful timing, and a ledger of who owes whom dinner. The clerk who replaced her at the harbor office grew up two doors down. It's not a resistance. It's a lane taking care of its paper the way it takes care of its nets.

"You can carry for me," Sato said, that first day, sizing me up like a crate. "You look honest and boring. That's the whole uniform."

So Tuesdays and Fridays I carry — envelopes between the shed and the shop, sums in my head where no soldier can read them. I told Sugi. She grunted like I'd told her the tide was in. I did not tell Kaito, and I want the ledger to show why: he has enough weight in his pockets, and I liked having one thing that was mine, one small brave thing I was doing with my boring honest face while he lifted ice and got quieter. Pride, filed as protection. It won't be the last time I make that filing error.

---

The Friday it goes wrong, Kaito is only there because of a shelf.

Orihara bought another one — her retirement plan is apparently shelving — and Kaito hauls it through the drying yard at the exact wrong hour, and I'm at the shed door with an envelope, and that is the entire coincidence. Two soldiers come up the seaward lane on foot. Not the pier detail — younger, unfamiliar, the kind the registry week has been rotating through town in pairs. Later, Sugi will say they were poking gates on their own initiative, hunting for something worth a commendation. What matters is the geography, so here it is: the drying yard is a rectangle of gravel between three walls, net frames down the middle like empty looms. One gate to the lane, where the soldiers are. The shed at the back, door open, Sato inside with the tin box open on the bench, and me on the threshold holding an envelope, and Kaito and the shelf halfway across the yard between everyone.

"Yard check," the taller one calls. Bored. Routine. Then he sees the shed — the box, the stamps laid out in their rows, Sato's ledger open — and stops being bored, and it happens the way a wave happens: nothing you can point to, then everything moving.

"Step away from the bench." His rifle comes off the shoulder. His partner's too, a half-beat late, the younger of the two, and that half-beat matters in a minute. "Hands where I can see them. Grandmother — away from the box."

Sato does something I will respect until I die and cursed at the time: she steps *in front of* the box. Thirty years a records clerk. She knows exactly what a tin of loose stamps means at a registry verification, and exactly which names are in the ledger, and she puts her body where the paper is like it's the most natural filing decision in the world.

"This is net-mending storage," she says. "You're holding rifles in a widow's drying yard."

"On the ground, all of it—" and he moves toward her, rifle first. It's aimed at a fifty-year-old woman's chest from four steps. His finger is inside the guard. He is twenty-two, maybe, and hunting a commendation, and his voice has gone tight and high because nothing about a granny in a net shed was in this morning's briefing, and scared young men with rifles are the loudest fact in any yard they stand in.

I am at the threshold, holding an envelope of the exact thing he's looking for.

I do the thing I do. I open my mouth to apologize to the situation — some blur of *sorry, she's my aunt, we're mending nets* — and I get one word into it when the shelf hits the gravel.

Kaito, setting it down. Freeing his hands. And walking — not running — into the space between the rifle and Sato, wide of the net frames, angling so the muzzle has to choose between them, and his voice, when it comes, isn't loud at all.

"Drop it."

Two words. Aimed like a thrown hook.

I saw it happen four steps away in good afternoon light, so this is witness testimony, not a story. The soldier's fingers opened. All of them, at once, evenly — the rifle just left his hands, like the ground had asked for it back — and his face hadn't changed yet, he was still wearing the shout, because no part of *him* had chosen it. The rifle hit the gravel. And the yard, for one held breath, tried to decide whether that had happened.

The partner decided first. The young one, the half-beat one — he saw his sergeant's weapon fall for no reason in the middle of a stand-off, and his body did the thing scared bodies do around a trigger.

"Step back," Kaito said, at the first one, second command, coin-quick — and the disarmed soldier obeyed, instantly, the machine walking him backward, and backward was where his partner was standing. Eighty kilos of commanded man stepped into the younger one's rifle arm.

The shot went into the yard like the end of the world.

Wild — up and sideways, wrenched by the collision — and it would have hit nothing, it should have hit nothing, the yard is three walls and open sky. Sato was mid-turn, reaching to close the tin box. The round took her through the upper arm and slapped her against the shed frame, and her blood hit the stamps, and the sound she made was not a scream, it was a huge affronted *oh* — a records clerk's disbelief at a filing error the size of a bullet.

After that, pieces. The young soldier frozen, staring at what his rifle had done, actually holding it away from his own body like it had bitten him. The older one on the gravel where the second command had left him, eyes coming back to himself, looking at his empty hands with an expression I recognized from a rooftop and will recognize in my sleep forever. Kaito with blood starting down his upper lip, already moving to Sato. Me, finally useful in the only way I know — pressure, here, both hands, like my mother taught me on a kitchen towel when I was nine, *arms bleed loud but the loud ones you can hold shut.*

And through the gate, drawn by the shot, the person the lane sends when things break: Mira, at a dead run, with her aunt's first-aid kit already under her arm.

She took over the arm like she'd done it before. She didn't ask what happened. She looked once at the rifle on the gravel, once at the older soldier still on his knees in the wrong part of the yard, once at Kaito's bleeding nose — a triangle, three points, I watched her eyes draw it — and then she said, in her tide-chart voice: "You two were never here. Go through the shop. Sato fell on a net hook. Everyone in this yard likes that story better than any other story. *Go.*"

The soldiers didn't stop us. That's its own entry: they didn't stop us, because the older one couldn't explain his empty hands without a story he didn't have, and the younger one had just shot a grandmother in a drying yard. They had more to bury than we did. By evening the lane's official truth was a discharge accident during a routine check, apologies conveyed, the box and its stamps long gone into some other shed by hands I didn't see. The lane closes over its own like water.

Sato will keep the arm. That sentence took until midnight to earn.

---

He comes and finds me on the sea wall after, past the bend, where the stone still holds the day's heat. For a while we sit in the dark and listen to the tide work, and I count what I actually saw against everything I've spent a summer refusing to add up, and the column finally totals itself whether I like it or not.

"You saw," Kaito says.

"I saw."

"Say what you saw." Not a challenge. He needs the witness. His voice is scraped down to the boards and there's dried blood he missed at his jaw.

"You said *drop it* and his hands opened before his face knew. You said *step back* and his legs did it. The same as—" and I make myself finish it, because the record deserves at least one honest sentence out of me this summer, "—the same as the roof."

He breathes out like something's been cut off him. And then it comes, all of it, quiet and fast — the practicing in the dark, the gulls, the pier and why the pier failed, the buyer, the nosebleeds, the coin taste, the clean sentences and how he builds them. He talks for longer than I've heard him talk since Kōgetsu. I don't interrupt. I file. Some of it is terrifying and some of it is just my best friend describing, in his own words, with his own scraped voice, the loneliest month of work anyone I know has ever done.

"It's getting cleaner," he says finally. "That's what today was. Two commands in ten seconds and they both landed. A month ago that soldier's hand wouldn't have opened all the way." His thumb runs across his fingertips, back and forth. "And Sato's arm still happened. Clean isn't the same as safe. I aimed everything right and the yard still broke sideways, because it isn't only me in the yard. I can't—" He stops the sentence half a beat early, the way he does. Puts his hand flat on the stone. Then, smaller: "Help me."

"Kaito—"

"Not — I don't need you to fix it. I need somebody outside my own head. You count things. You see the order things happen in — you just proved it, you gave me the roof back in the right order and it didn't kill me to hear it. Help me figure out the rules. What it needs, what it costs, where the edges are. Because I'm going to keep being in yards, Haru. This summer isn't going to stop putting me in yards, and the next wild shot lands somewhere worse than an arm."

The tide works on the stones. Here is where the record has to be exact, because this is the hinge of the whole account.

He didn't ask me for the six syllables. He's never asked again, since the wall in the dusk — he asked for something I could actually give, and I understood, sitting there, that this was mercy for me and not for him. He'd measured what my silence could carry and cut the request to fit it.

"I saw the hand open on the roof," I say. It's the closest I've ever come. "I've been seeing it all summer. I couldn't make it mean anything that didn't terrify me, so I filed it under dry air and I let you carry it alone. That's my entry. That's what I did." My throat hurts. "I'll help. I don't know what help looks like yet. But I'm done pretending, and I'm not leaving you alone with it — the rules, the costs, any of it. We figure out your mouth the way we'd have studied for finals. Properly. Both of us."

Kaito looks at the black water for a while.

"She'd laugh," he says. "Study group."

"She'd run it," I say, and it's the first time either of us has touched the subject of her lightly, and it holds — barely, like early ice, but it holds.

We walk back up the lane past Orihara's dark shop. In her front room a lamp is still burning where Mira is sitting up with Sato. Neither of us says anything about the third point of the triangle her eyes drew in the yard — the girl who arrived at a gunshot with a kit already packed, who invented the cover story in one breath, who has watched Kaito more carefully all summer than I've watched anyone in my life.

I file it under *good neighbor.* The file is getting heavy. I keep it anyway.
