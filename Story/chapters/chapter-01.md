# Chapter 1 — The Plan

Sugi's kitchen smells like pickled plum and charcoal. We still have ash on our shoes. She takes the hideous arcade cat out of my arms — birthday sticker still stuck to the plastic, one milky eye staring — and sets it on the shelf by the window. Then she puts a cup in front of me and doesn't ask what happened. I wrap both hands around it.

"I'll tell you all of it," I say. "From before — so you know how we got here."

She nods once and sits.

It started on a Tuesday morning on the Rail Loop, four days before Ryn's birthday, with a train pass falling out of a salaryman's bag.

I caught it without meaning to — plastic, warm from his pocket — held it out, and said, "Sorry," which made no sense, because I hadn't done anything wrong. He blinked at me like I'd apologized for the weather. Heat was already coming up off the platform tiles. My phone buzzed against my thigh: two ugly tones from the war app the government would not let me uninstall. Haiga on fire again somewhere east. I turned the screen face-down.

"You apologized to a pass," said a voice behind me. "An inanimate rectangle."

"I apologized to the man."

"You defused a fight that didn't exist." Kurosawa Kaito dropped onto the bench beside me before I'd turned around. Wet hair, scraped knuckles, thin white nick through his left eyebrow when he grinned. He was wearing yesterday's dark jacket over today's uniform, and he was already too loud for the morning. "One day you'll apologize mid-swing and the guy will just leave."

"That's the plan."

"It's a personality disorder, Haru."

"It's manners."

"Same disease." He nudged my knee with his. "Train's here. Unless you're apologizing to that too."

The doors chimed. We got on just ahead of them. Two women across the car glanced at Kaito, bristled, then softened in that odd way people did around him — like he had already won an argument nobody started. Through the window the city unrolled: river, elevated highway, the Daigo Building screen that was supposed to show weather and increasingly showed the war. FRONT STABLE. EASTERN WARDS: CONTAINED. Names of the newly missing scrolled underneath. People looked at their knees instead.

"Ryn's off today," Kaito said, already changing the subject the way he did whenever the screen came up. "Pickle stall with Grandma."

"She hates the pickle thing."

"She loves the pickle thing. She just hates that she loves it." He stretched; his sleeve rode up and I caught a bruise on his forearm — greenish, a few days old, the shape of someone's grip. I didn't ask. "You bringing your mom's thing Saturday?"

"Her *thing* is a homemade cake," I said, "and you will show it respect."

"I will show it reverence," he said, hand over his heart like he was swearing into court. "Candles?"

"I have candles."

"Good. If you forget candles I will steal some from the shrine and then you'll have to apologize to a god."

"Please don't steal from a shrine."

"See? Personality disorder." A third-year two seats down snorted into his phone. The car felt a fraction easier after that, and none of us could have said why.

---

We came up the school hill late enough that the track already smelled baked. The rooftop chain hung on its broken lock like always — formality, not security. Everybody climbed it anyway.

First period, Doi-sensei shoved through the door with papers clutched to his chest, glasses askew, tie already losing. "Sit down, sit down — I have exactly enough handouts, which means if any of you have multiplied since Friday we are in trouble."

Kaito was still standing in the aisle, arguing soccer with a third-year who'd wandered in from the hall. The older kid had half a head on him and wanted the room to watch.

"Hey," Kaito said — not loud, just final. "Back off. Go back to your class."

The third-year's weight left his forward foot before his face caught up. His shoulders dropped. He blinked at his own shoes, confused, and walked out like the hallway had been his idea all along.

Nobody else seemed to notice. Loud boy tells louder boy to leave, louder boy leaves — ordinary high school sentence.

I saw the body answer a beat early. I saw Kaito touch two fingers under his nose and come away with a pinprick of red. He wiped it on the jacket like it bored him.

Dry AC and summer heat, I told myself. That was the easy story.

"Kurosawa," Doi said. "Sit *down*."

Kaito sat late, eye-roll included. Doi exhaled like he had won one round before coffee. Ordinary again. I still kept the early step and the pinprick, unnamed, the same way I kept the war app face-down in my bag.

---

After last bell we pushed into Fullmoon Mart for the air conditioning and the fried-chicken heat lamp. Amano Ryn was behind the register — sleeves shoved up, apron double-knotted, ink on her fingers from the price tags, cowlick fighting the clip, silver stud flashing when she turned.

"Don't," she said.

"We haven't said anything," Kaito said.

"You're both wearing the face. The 'let's do something stupid' face. Kaito's wearing it because he's Kaito, and you're wearing it because you'll agree to anything he wants and then feel responsible when it goes wrong." She rang up a canned coffee without looking at the customer. "It's her birthday Saturday, isn't it. That's the face. You're planning something."

"It's *your* birthday Saturday," Kaito said, delighted, elbows on the counter under the NO LEANING sign. "You can't plan your own surprise."

"I can absolutely plan my own surprise. I plan everything. This store would burn down without me."

"Then tell us what you want," I said.

"I want you two not to look guilty in my store." She pointed at Kaito's elbows. "And I want him off my counter before the manager comes back from the stockroom and fires me for hosting livestock."

"Livestock," Kaito repeated, wounded. "I am a guest."

"You are a problem with a jacket."

Door chime. A kid in a too-big hoodie headed for the drinks wall with an egg sandwich already half up the front of his shirt. Ryn's body changed before either of us said anything.

"Hey."

He ran.

She was over the counter before the word finished — one hand flat, pivot clean, gum display skittering — and out into the street while the store's welcome chime was still cheerful. We hit the pavement half a block later. The sandwich was crushed on the corner. The kid was gone. Ryn bent double, furious and laughing, pale scar on her right knee where the skirt had ridden up.

"An egg sandwich," she wheezed. "He risked a criminal record for a crushed egg sandwich. He panicked — did you see his face — he didn't even want it—" The laugh cut. Something older moved behind her eyes. "He had the Haiga dust on his shoes. That gray stuff from the east. You can't wash it out."

Three phones buzzed with the war app's two tones. Nobody reached for theirs.

Kaito picked up the sandwich, inspected the crush, and ate it in two bites. "Evidence."

"That was evidence for the police, you animal—"

"It's gone now. Very thorough cleanup."

She shoved him a full step. He let her. I looked at the ground, because looking at both of them properly would have been too much for a Tuesday.

"Saturday," Ryn said, already walking again, taking the sidewalk like she owned it. "Since you're both incapable of surprising me. What's the plan?"

"Sakaba-dōri," Kaito said. "Arcade, ramen, karaoke—"

"The 'K' is dead on the tower sign."

"The dead 'K' is tradition."

"Rooftop," I said.

They both looked at me.

"The school rooftop," I said. "Midnight. You can see the whole city from up there. We bring the cake up, and you turn sixteen where you can see all the lights at once."

Ryn stopped walking. For a second she just looked at me, neon sliding across the stud and the cowlick. Then she smiled — the rare sincere one — and said, "Yeah. Okay. Let's do the roof."

Beside me Kaito went still. No joke. No volume. Just looking at her like the smile had knocked the air out of him, and he wasn't going to admit that to either of us.

Then he laughed too loud and hooked an arm around each of our shoulders. "Midnight, then. All three of us. I'm telling you right now — nothing is going to ruin it. Nothing."
