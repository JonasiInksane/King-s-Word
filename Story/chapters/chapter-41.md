# Chapter 41 — Lantern Paper

The paper shop wants hands before it wants names, which suits me fine.

I find it because a boy my age is standing outside it at dawn shouting into the alley that his uncle needs two backs and doesn't care whose, and I get there first by half a step ahead of a dockworker twice my size. The owner is a narrow man named Endo who talks the entire time he's deciding whether to hire me, which turns out to mean he decides in about four seconds and just keeps talking anyway.

"You're not from here," he says, already turning to lead me inside. "Don't care. You're not from here, you don't owe anybody in this ward a grudge, which means you'll actually move the boxes instead of standing around arguing about whose turn it is. That's rarer than you'd think. Watch the stacks — that one's rice paper, that one's the heavy stock for the frames, and none of it likes rain, which is a problem because half of Ashiro's roof leaks in August."

"What's the rush?" I ask, because the whole floor of the shop is stacked to the rafters with rolled paper and the man behind me is already unloading a second cart.

"The Reed Festival," Endo says, like I'm slow for not knowing. "Two weeks. Every lantern on every bridge in this city needs new paper because last year's is sun-rotted and the fire marshal finally noticed. I have the contract for the east wards. I have three days to fill an order that should take a week, and my nephew has decided this is the month to fall in love and be useless." He shoves a roll of paper into my arms that's heavier than it looks. "Frames go upstairs. Paper goes on the racks by color, not by size, I don't care what your instinct tells you. Two coins an hour, paid tonight, not tomorrow, because I don't trust boys I met at dawn to still be here tomorrow."

"I'll be here tomorrow."

"Everyone says that." He's already turned away, shouting at the cart man. "Upstairs. Go."

The work is the kind my body remembers even when my head doesn't want to — stacking, hauling, the particular ache across the shoulders that comes from carrying something too wide to hold close. It's not the pier and it's not the ice-truck yards from a life that feels like someone else's now, but it's close enough that my hands know what to do without me managing them, and for a few hours that's the whole relief of it. Nobody in the shop asks where I learned to carry a load balanced right. Nobody asks about the fading bruise along my jaw or the way I flinch half a beat too long at a dropped crate downstairs before I place the sound. Endo just keeps talking — about the festival committee, about his nephew's terrible taste in girls, about the fire marshal's cousin who runs the lantern-frame stall and has been overcharging the whole district for six years running — and none of it requires an answer, which is its own kind of mercy.

By noon I've learned three things that have nothing to do with paper: the festival is on the fifteenth, the fireworks go up over the reed pond on the east side because the water throws the light back twice, and half the city treats the whole thing like the only good thing that's happened to Ashiro in a year.

Around midmorning the cart man — a heavyset guy named Tetsu who has clearly worked for Endo long enough to have opinions about him — leans against the doorframe on his break and watches me stack the last of the rice paper.

"You're doing the corners wrong," he says. "Not wrong-wrong. Just slow. Stack from the middle out, not the edge in, or you'll be here till the fifteenth yourself."

I switch the way he shows me and the stack goes twice as fast, which he seems to take as a personal victory.

"Where'd you learn to carry like that?" he asks. "That's not paper-shop carrying. That's dock carrying."

"Pier, up north. Before the line moved."

"Before the line moved" is apparently a phrase everyone in this city understands without needing more of it, because he just nods like I've explained a whole life in four words and goes back to stacking without asking anything else. It's the easiest conversation I've had in weeks, mostly because it isn't really a conversation — just two people agreeing on how to move a stack of paper without either of us owing the other a story.

"You're quiet for a boy who talks with his shoulders that loud," Endo says, handing me a rice ball wrapped in paper he definitely didn't pay for out of his own pocket. "Eat. You've earned lunch, at least, even if you haven't earned trust yet."

I eat on the loading step with my legs hanging over the canal, and it's the first food since the truck left Shizuhara that I actually taste instead of just swallow — the rice a little too salty, the plum in the middle sharp enough to make my eyes water, ordinary in a way that feels like an accusation and a relief at the same time. Somewhere behind me Endo is arguing with the cart man about the price of glue.

---

Mira finds me at the end of the shift with Suzumori's borrowed crutch tucked under one arm like she's been carrying it since Shizuhara instead of buying it an hour ago.

"You smell like paste," she says.

"I smell like money," I say, and hold up the coins Endo counted into my palm without shorting a single one, which he made a point of narrating out loud so I'd notice. "Three days of work, maybe more if his nephew stays useless."

"That covers the room through the week." She doesn't smile exactly, but something in her face unclenches a notch, the way it does when a number finally goes the right direction. "Suzumori found a woman selling grilled fish two streets over who does not ask why an old woman needs a crutch. We're eating something that isn't noodles tonight."

The fish stall turns out to be run by a woman with forearms like she's spent thirty years hauling nets, who calls everyone *customer* instead of a name and clearly could not care less about our story as long as our coins are real. The fish is smoky and a little burnt at the tail and better than anything I remember eating in Kōgetsu, which shouldn't be possible and is anyway. Suzumori eats with the crutch propped against her chair like a spear she's decided to retire for the evening.

"Your boy smells like a paper shop," she tells Mira, around a mouthful.

"He got hired at one," Mira says.

"I noticed the smell before the sentence. It's a strong smell." Suzumori tilts her head at me. "Did you learn anything today besides how to carry a box?"

"The festival's on the fifteenth," I say. "Fireworks over some pond on the east side. The whole city's making paper for it like it's the only thing that matters this year."

"It is the only thing that matters this year," the fish woman says, without looking up from her grill, which makes all three of us go quiet in the way you do when a stranger overhears more of your table than you meant to let out. She flips a fillet with the flat of her knife. "First one since the checkpoints went up on the coast road. Half this city lost someone to the line east. The other half is pretending they didn't so they can still go stand by the water and watch the sky do something pretty for one night. You should go. Boy like you looks like he forgot what pretty looks like."

"Maybe," I say, because it's easier than explaining that I don't know if I'll still be standing anywhere in this city on the fifteenth, hunting a coat and a truck instead of watching fireworks.

"Maybe is a coward's answer," Suzumori says, but there's no real heat in it. "You've been in this city one day and you're already deciding which parts of it you don't get to enjoy. That's not caution. That's just punishing yourself with extra steps."

"I'm not punishing myself."

"You are, a little," Mira says, quieter, not unkind. "You ate lunch today and you're already apologizing for it with your face."

"I didn't apologize for anything."

"You don't have to say it for it to be there," she says, and doesn't push past that, which is somehow worse than if she had.

We walk back with full stomachs, which is a sentence I would not have believed a week ago. Suzumori swings the crutch more than she needs it, testing the rhythm of not favoring the bad ankle, and at one point she overcorrects on a loose cobble and nearly puts herself into a fruit cart, catching the awning pole with one hand and swearing in a way that startles a laugh out of me before I can stop it — short, surprised, gone almost as fast as it came, the way Mira's laugh went last night. It still counts. My chest still remembers how to do it.

"Laugh at an old woman falling," Suzumori says, straightening with as much dignity as a woman leaning on a stolen crutch can manage. "That's the boy I expected to meet on a pine road with a mouth like his."

"You didn't fall."

"I nearly gave a fruit cart my whole dignity. Same thing." But she's not really annoyed — there's something almost pleased in it, like she'd rather I laughed than flinched.

---

Back at the boarding house, Endo's paper roll list is still stuffed in my jacket pocket from the afternoon, and when I pull it out to check tomorrow's start time, a second sheet comes out with it — a festival notice, cheap printed paper, the kind Endo said his shop was stocking for every lamppost in the east ward. Reed lanterns pictured along a bridge. A date in block characters. FIFTEENTH NIGHT — LANTERNS ON THE WATER, FIREWORKS OVER THE REED POND, ALL WARDS WELCOME.

Mira takes it out of my hand and reads it twice, the way she reads everything that might matter later.

"Two weeks," she says.

"That's what Endo said."

"That's a lot of city standing in one place on one night." She folds the notice along its crease, careful, like it's worth keeping instead of scrap. "A lot of faces coming out from wherever they've been hiding all year to stand by the same water at the same hour."

"You think he'd be there?" I say. Haruki, I mean, though neither of us says his name out loud, because saying it out loud still feels like it might jinx whatever thin luck got us this far.

"I think a city that empties itself onto one bank of water is either the safest place in Ashiro to look for somebody, or the worst place to be found standing still," Mira says. "I don't know which yet. I know we have two weeks to decide, and paper in my hand that says exactly when we'll need to."

Suzumori looks at the notice over Mira's shoulder, quiet for once, and doesn't say anything about coats or careful mouths. She just taps the printed date once with one finger, the way a person marks something down so they won't have the excuse of forgetting it later.

"Fifteenth," she says. "Write it somewhere that isn't just your head, boy. Heads forget things they're scared of."

I put the notice on the windowsill next to what's left of the envelope, where I'll see it every morning whether I want to or not.
