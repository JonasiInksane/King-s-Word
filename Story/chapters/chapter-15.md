# Chapter 15 — Loading Line

North Market smells like wet cardboard and diesel.

The casual line is a strip of open asphalt beside a chain fence. Men without lists wait to be pointed at crates. The sun is still high enough that Gorō's warning feels like someone else's problem. Mira talks to a foreman with a red rag around his wrist. I keep my mouth shut and try to look like I know which end of a box is up.

"Half-shift," the foreman says. "Vegetable trucks. Gloves on the hook. You argue about gloves, you're done."

"We won't," Mira says.

He looks at me anyway. "He speak?"

"When he needs to."

"Then he doesn't need to." The foreman jerks his chin at a stack of crates. "You. Tall one. Those go on the second truck. Girl counts. I don't want clever. I want done before the light turns."

I lift. The wood bites my hands through cheap gloves. Mira moves down the line marking numbers on a scrap of paper like she has done this in a different town with a different name. For a while the work is almost kind — heavy enough that my throat stops inventing sentences, loud enough that the neon of the arcade feels far away.

A man in a vest cuts into our aisle on purpose. Not gray-green. Yard private again, or the same breed. He puts a hand on the crate I am carrying like he is checking the weight for fun.

"You're in my row," he says.

"Foreman put us here," Mira says.

"Foreman isn't here." He smiles without warmth. "Move."

I feel the old shape start behind my teeth — *move* aimed the other way, clean, stupid. Mira's eyes find mine over the crate. She does not step on my boot this time. She just takes the crate out of my hands, sets it down, and walks us three stacks over without making it a fight.

"See," she says, quiet, once we are loading again. "Different through."

"That wasn't different. That was you."

"Same result." She passes me the next box. "Keep going."

---

The truck fills early. The foreman cuts the extras without apology and pays in cash that looks like it has been in a pocket since before the war learned new maps. Mira counts it once, then again, then splits it where I can see.

"Enough for rice," she says. "Not enough for another room that asks nothing."

"So we keep working."

"Casual line dies later. You heard the old man." She looks at the sky — orange starting to lean. "There's talk of evening hands on the depot side if the trucks keep coming. Or we go back to Minato and take the rice she offered and stop pretending we can outrun a whole city before dark."

"Depot," I say, before I can talk myself into a bowl and a mat.

Mira studies my face. "Because you want money, or because standing still makes your mouth itch."

"Both."

"Honest. Fine." She tucks the bills away. "Depot side. You load. I talk. If someone blocks you again, we walk. No shortcuts."

We leave the fence line while the registered crews start showing paper at the gate. Nobody asks our names. That is the whole mercy of a half-shift.

---

Central Arcade, same afternoon.

The charm-shop stairs smell like plastic batteries and old smoke. Riku lets me knock. A man in an undershirt opens like he has already decided we are a problem.

"No rooms," he says.

"I'm not asking for us," I say. "I'm looking for friends who stayed here. Yesterday evening, maybe. Paid cash. Left in the morning."

He looks at Riku. Riku looks like furniture that bites.

"Yesterday," the man says. "Girl paid for one night. Loud one didn't sleep — I heard him walking. They left before my kettle boiled. Asked about work that pays the same day and doesn't care where you're from."

"Did they say where they were going?"

"I don't give tours. I rent." He starts to close the door, then stops halfway. "If they were hungry, someone on this street always points at Minato. If they were stupid, North Market. If they wanted paper trouble, Station Plaza. Pick which kind of friends you have."

The door shuts.

"Come on," Riku says on the stairs. "He answered like a man who wants you off his step."

The narrower door over the takoyaki stand opens with chopsticks still in a woman's hand.

"If you're selling something, downstairs," she says.

"Not selling," I say. "Looking for two people who asked about a place that feeds working boys without a speech about papers. This morning, maybe."

She looks past me at the deeper neon. "I told a girl Minato. Not the harbor — the bar. She looked like she already knew and only wanted someone else to say it."

"How long ago?"

"Before the oil burned. Morning." The chopsticks twitch. "If you're chasing them, you're late for lunch and early for excuses."

She shuts the door harder than the first one.

On the street Riku clicks his tongue once. "Trail's still warm. We eat where they might've eaten. I know the place."

"You know Minato?"

"Everyone who carries packages in this town knows Minato." He starts walking. "Rules when we get there: you don't confess, you don't rush the mama, and if she tells you to eat first, you eat first. Boys who argue with broth get the alley."

"I can eat."

"We'll see."

---

Minato is two turns off the bright strip, under a pine-named hill's shadow. Noren dark with old smoke. Inside, the air is broth and charcoal. The corner stool has an old man and one unfinished beer. The woman behind the counter looks up as we come in and prices us in one pass — then her mouth tightens like she has already done this once today.

She sets down two bowls of oden before we have sat.

"Second pair," she says. "Trouble comes in shifts. Sit."

Riku sits like he has done this before. I sit because my knees remember the road. The broth is hot enough that talking would be stupid anyway. I wait until she decides my bowl looks honest.

"Now," she says. "Who are you chasing, and why should I care."

"Friends," I say. "They would've come through earlier. Tall boy, my age. Girl with him. Someone on the arcade said she asked for Minato this morning."

She looks at Riku. "He with you, or are you with him."

"Cousin," Riku says.

"Sure." She snorts once. "They ate around noon. Girl paid. Boy didn't talk much. I sent them to the North Market loading line — same-day hands until dark. You're late for the bowls. Not buried."

"Are they still there?"

"How would I know from here." She tips a wipe cloth into a bucket. "Past the shotengai bend. If the casual line already spat them out, they'll invent a second plan or they'll come back for rice. If they come back with soldiers on their heels, the alley's that way and they're not my problem. Same goes for you."

The old man lifts his beer a millimeter. "Casual line dies at sundown," he says, almost kindly. "After that it's paper. If your friends wanted cash without questions, they either got on early or they're already walking toward the depot side inventing evening work."

Mama points a chopstick at him. "He already collected once today."

"Interest," Gorō says, and his laugh sounds like a gearbox.

Riku stands. "Bowls were good."

"I charged you," Mama says. She looks at me once more. "You find them, you don't shout their names across a yard. This town listens for free."

Outside, the pine-shadow street is cooler. Neon leaks pink at the turn. The sky is starting to lean toward the orange Gorō likes to tax.

"North Market," Riku says. "Then depot if the line's dead. You ask ordinary. I watch hands."

"Got it."

He glances at my stance, clicks his tongue once, and starts toward the shotengai bend. "Come on. Day's not done."
