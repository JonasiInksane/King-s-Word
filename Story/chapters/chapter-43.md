# Chapter 43 — Stall Turn

Suzumori wants pickles from a stall two lanes past the fish woman's — the good kind, she says, not the ones that leave your mouth tasting like you bit a fence — and Mira left before I woke up with nothing but a note pinned under the festival notice on the sill: *checking something, don't wait on me.* So it's just me and a fistful of coins Endo doesn't need back until noon, walking a market I've mostly learned the shape of by now.

Ten days to the fifteenth. Endo's shop has stopped being about rolled paper and started being about frames, which means half the ward smells like glue and cut reed whenever the wind comes off the workshops. I like the smell more than I expected to. It means people are building toward something instead of just surviving whatever's already happened to them.

The lane Suzumori sent me down is narrower than the main strip, more locals than festival lookers, brine barrels lined up under a low tin roof with jars stacked three deep behind them. A man's voice up ahead is arguing with a customer about the price of pickled plum like his whole reputation rides on two coins, which in this city it probably does.

I get in line behind an old woman with a basket and wait, because waiting in lines is one of the smaller, dumber skills this city has taught me — how to just stand somewhere and not need it to move faster.

The stall boy has his back to the lane, both hands deep in a barrel, hauling up a net bag of daikon that's been soaking since dawn by the smell of it. Tall. Dark hair a little longer than it should be for someone doing this kind of work, pushed back off his forehead with a wet hand. Sleeves rolled past the elbow. Forearms that didn't use to look like that — thicker through the wrist, a new white line across one knuckle healed wrong, like he grabbed something sharp and kept working anyway.

"Two coins for the plum, that's it," he says to whoever's ahead of me, not turning around yet. "I don't set the price. I just work here today."

The old woman pays without arguing and moves off. It's my turn.

He turns around.

For one whole second my body doesn't know what to do with what my eyes just gave it. The market keeps making its noise — the plum vendor arguing, a cart wheel sticking on a cobble, gulls fighting over a dropped skewer — and none of it touches me, because Haruki is standing on the other side of a barrel of daikon with his mouth already open on whatever he was about to say to the next customer, and the next customer is me.

His mouth stays open. Nothing comes out of it.

"Haruki." It comes up my throat with weight already stacked behind it before I've decided to give it any, hot and sudden, and copper floods the back of my tongue for half a breath — not a command, nothing shaped enough to land on anyone, just his own name trying to leave my mouth like a shout instead of a word. I swallow it down to something barely louder than the barrel between us. "Haruki."

"Kaito." His voice does the opposite of mine — goes quiet instead of loud, careful instead of hard, the way it always does right before he says something he's thought all the way through. Except I don't think he's thought anything through yet either. His eyes go to my jaw, my hands, the space behind me like he's counting who's close enough to hear, and only then back to my face. "You're—" He stops. Starts again. "You're here."

"I live here." It comes out stupid and I know it's stupid the second it's out, but it's true, and true is apparently all I've got left in me right now.

Behind me somebody clears their throat — a woman with two kids tugging her sleeves, waiting for pickles like the world hasn't just tipped sideways on her side of the lane. Haruki's eyes flick to her and something in his shoulders drops half an inch, deliberate, like he's setting a lid back on a pot before it boils over in front of a stranger.

"What can I get you?" he says. To me. In the voice he'd use for anyone.

"Daikon," I say, because it's the first pickled thing my mouth can find. "And whatever she was buying. The plum."

He weighs it without looking at the scale twice, wraps it in paper already gone translucent with brine, and takes my coins with fingers shaking just enough that one drops and rolls under the barrel. Neither of us goes for it.

"You're not hurt," I say, low, while he's still bent half toward the dropped coin. It's not the question I mean to ask. It's the one that gets out first.

"Not right now." He straightens. "You?"

"Not right now."

The woman behind me shifts her weight, not patient anymore. Haruki serves her without missing the wrap, without his voice changing at all — daikon, two jars of plum, change counted out loud so she doesn't have to ask twice. I stand there holding my own paper parcel like it's the only reason I'm allowed to still be in this lane, and I watch him be normal at somebody while every other part of him is clearly not.

When she's gone there's a gap — three, four seconds where the next person in line is still deciding what they want — and neither of us wastes it on anything soft.

"Where?" I say. "Just tell me where you are, who you're with, I need—"

"I can't talk here." Fast, flat, his glance going past my shoulder again. "The man who runs this stall went to get more barrels. He'll be back before I finish explaining anything, and I am not having this conversation where six strangers can hear your name."

"So when?"

"Tonight." He wipes his hands on an apron someone lent him, plain gray, two sizes too big, and for a second he just looks at me — really looks, not the scanning-the-lane look, the other one, the one I haven't seen since a truck full of headlights put half a mile of dark road between us. "I close this stall myself tonight. The man's leaving me to lock the barrels and count the till because he trusts me now, which took less time than it should have. Come back after the lanterns go up over the lane — not before, or you'll be standing here with witnesses. I'll be alone by then."

"Alone." I latch onto the word harder than I mean to. "Riku's not going to be standing over your shoulder?"

"Riku knows I'm covering somebody's stall for the hour. He doesn't know who walked up in the line." Something moves behind Haruki's eyes, fast, gone before I can read all of it. "None of them know you're here. I took this because the warehouse foreman owed the stall man a favor and I said I'd cover so the crates got unloaded on time. It wasn't supposed to turn into anything."

"You didn't tell them where you were going."

"I'm telling you I didn't expect the customer," he says, and there's an edge in it now, thin but real. "I don't plan my whole morning around who might be standing in a pickle line. Do you tell Mira everywhere you walk?"

I don't answer that, because the honest answer is *not since about four minutes ago*, and saying it out loud feels like handing him something I haven't decided to give yet.

Another customer steps up — a kid, maybe ten, counting coins twice like he's not sure he has enough. Haruki turns to him without missing a beat, patient, gets the order right on the first try. I watch him do the whole ordinary transaction like it costs him nothing, and I read it wrong, the way I read most things about him lately — I decide it means he's fine, that whatever's under the careful voice is small enough to manage between customers.

It isn't. I see it the second the kid walks off — the way Haruki's hand stays flat on the counter a beat too long, like he's using the wood to keep himself standing instead of just resting there.

"You're not fine," I say, before I can stop myself.

"No," he says. Plain. No argument in it. "I'm really not. But I have about four more minutes before that man comes back with his barrels, and I'm not going to spend them falling apart in front of you where anyone walking past can watch it happen. Tonight. That's what I have to give you today."

"Fine." The word comes out harder than it should. "Tonight, when the lanterns go up. This stall."

"This stall," he says. "Don't bring Mira. Don't bring Suzumori, if she's still with you—"

"She is."

"—not because I don't want to see them. Because I don't know what I'm going to say yet, and I would rather say the wrong thing to just you first than say it in front of a room."

That lands somewhere close to the exact thing Mira asked me on the canal steps a few days back, the question I still don't have an answer for, and hearing it come out of Haruki's mouth instead — *I don't know what I'm going to say yet* — makes it feel less like a hole in me and more like something we're standing in together for once.

"Okay," I say. "Just me."

Down the lane, a man's voice calls out, already annoyed, already back — "Boy! Barrels don't unload themselves, and neither does my till" — and Haruki's face closes back into the ordinary stall-boy mask so fast I almost don't believe the last few minutes happened at all.

"Go," he says, quiet, not looking at me now, hands already moving to look busy. "Before he sees your face twice in one morning and starts asking who you are."

I go. I make myself walk instead of run, out into the wider lane where nobody's paying attention to a boy with a parcel of pickled daikon, and three turns away my hands start shaking hard enough that I stop and set the parcel down on a low wall, just to breathe.

Back at the room, Suzumori is sitting up on her mattress with her bad ankle propped on a folded blanket, and she takes one look at me before I've even got the door shut.

"You look like you saw a ghost buy vegetables," she says.

"Just tired." I hold out the parcel. "Daikon. And the plum, since it was there."

She unwraps a corner and eats a slice standing on principle, not because she's hungry yet. "Your hands were shaking when you walked in. Was it him? The coat."

It's the easiest door she could have opened for me, and I walk through it before I can talk myself out of it. "Thought I saw him again, by the barrels. Wasn't. Same as the tea stand." The lie sits wrong in my mouth, heavier than the truth would have been, but it's not nothing — I did think my whole life had turned a corner in that lane, just not the corner I'm telling her.

"You keep seeing him in men who are not him," Suzumori says. "That's what a bad debt does. It goes looking for the man who's owed it in every stranger's coat." She studies me a beat longer than I like. "Eat something. You look like a boy who forgot he has a body."

I eat because it's easier than arguing. She doesn't ask again, but I catch her watching my hands twice more before I leave for Endo's, and both times I make them still on purpose.

The shop is glue smell and stacked frames all afternoon, and I get two corners wrong badly enough that Tetsu redoes them without comment, which is somehow worse than if he'd said something. "You're somewhere else today," is all he offers, and doesn't push past it when I say I didn't sleep great. Endo pays me out at dusk without shorting a coin and tells me to be back at dawn. I say I will. Right now the only thing I'm actually planning past tonight is tonight.

Mira comes back to the room after dark, quiet in a way that isn't unusual for her lately — whatever she's been checking, she doesn't hand it over easy, and I've learned not to make her. She eats a little of the pickles Suzumori has been guarding, says the plum's better than the last stall's, and doesn't ask why my leg won't stop bouncing under the table.

When the lanterns along the canal start going up — the first strings catch light through the window, one by one — I stand up and say I'm going to walk for a while.

"Now?" Mira looks up from the coins she's been recounting for the fourth time tonight. "I'll come."

"I want to be by myself for a bit." It's not a lie, even if it isn't the whole truth either. "Head's loud. I need the water without anybody talking at me."

She studies me the way Suzumori did this morning, and for a second I think she's going to argue it, come anyway, plant herself beside me whether I want the company or not. Instead she just nods, once, slow.

"Don't be gone past midnight," she says. "And don't go looking at every coat you pass. You'll wear yourself out before you find the real one."

"I won't look at coats," I say. True enough. "I'll be back."

She goes back to the coins. I go down the stairs two at a time, past the landlady's dark window, into the lane that smells like canal water and somebody's dinner. The pickle stall is three turns from here.
