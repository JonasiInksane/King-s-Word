# Chapter 29 — Loading

Getting to the service court after second bell takes work.

Morning count writes C forty-two again. Mei is at the board this time — east recount done. She confirms the number with the marker and leaves before Ōhashi can start about his notebook. She does not look at us. We wax. We keep Katō's name out of the junction.

Ōhashi finds us at the mop buckets before midday with his hair fixed and his voice almost steady again.

"Kitchen needs empty ice crates hauled to the loading bay before second bell," he says. "Clerk asked for two C hands. I told him I had two who already know the court and won't ask stupid questions about night schedules." He holds my gaze a second too long. "You will not ask stupid questions. You will stack crates. If a door opens after the light drops, you finish stacking and walk back to C. Clear?"

"Clear," I say.

Riku tips his chin once. No grin. The wrap on his forearm is cleaner today. He still switches hands when the corporal isn't looking.

"If anyone asks," Ōhashi adds, quieter, "I sent you. That's all."

He leaves before either of us answers.

---

The bay door is shut when we arrive. Daylight. The clerk with the stamp is the same one who almost answered me about night deliveries. He looks at my face and snorts, like he remembers and still thinks it was funny.

"You two," he says. "Crates. Stack against the left wall. Don't lean on my counter. Don't ask me about schedules. Serizawa already told half the corridor you like asking."

"We're stacking," I say, shoulders down, ordinary.

He stamps a chit. Riku takes the first empty crate without looking at him. We work. Ice melt soaks the cuffs of my pants. The bay door stays shut. Somewhere inside the kitchen a radio plays a cheerful song over the smell of boiled fish. An armband boy from B passes with a tray cart and tells us the review board likes people who smile.

Riku's crate hits the stack harder than it needs to.

"Don't," I say.

"I didn't."

"You were about to."

He clicks his tongue and takes the next crate.

We finish the left wall by late afternoon. The clerk checks the stack, marks the chit, and tells us to wait for a second load from inside — kitchen is slow, he says, kitchen is always slow — and waiting means sit on the cold concrete and do not wander. That keeps us in sight of the bay door when the light starts to thin. Nobody comes from C to collect us. The day-shift corporal's whistle blows somewhere far behind the building and dies out.

The clerk locks his stamp drawer and leaves through the kitchen. "Second bell in an hour," he says over his shoulder. "If you're still here after, you're someone else's problem. Don't touch the door."

We sit. The concrete is cold through my pants. A kitchen worker wheels a bin past the inner door and does not look at us. Riku unwraps the end of his bandage, checks the bruise, rewraps it with his teeth and his good hand. He does not talk about Katō. He does not talk about bands. I watch the shut bay door until my eyes water from not blinking, then force myself to look at the crate stack instead.

---

Second bell reaches the court thin through the concrete. Lights over the kitchen eaves drop halfway. For a minute nothing happens. Then the bay door rolls — the same shake in the floor as the first night, stronger up close — and cold air and diesel push in.

A truck backs into the light. Not a kitchen ice truck. Canvas sides. Two gray-greens on the ground with clipboards. A third in the cab with the engine at idle. Behind them, from a corridor I have not been allowed to walk, a line comes out.

Some of them wear armbands. Some wear C tags. A girl about fourteen still has the bright look the A-block kids wear at breakfast, but her eyes do not match it. A boy with a seed cap pulled low — not Katō; younger; different face — holds a cracked cup against his chest. The gray-greens check tags and names without reading them loud enough for us to keep.

"Load," a gray-green says, bored. "Two benches. Bags under. No talking once you're up."

They climb into the canvas. Boots on the truck bed. Someone drops a bag and is told to pick it up without being told a name. One clipboard man marks a tablet. The other counts heads the way the junction does, except this count will not go on the wall board in the morning for anyone to argue over.

I stay low. Standing would put me in the light. Riku's hand finds my sleeve and holds it flat against the crate stack. We watch from the shadow under the kitchen eaves. My legs want to cramp. I do not shift.

Katō is not in the first line. He is in the second.

Same seed cap. Same cracked plastic cup, empty now. Same way of setting his shoulders when someone tells him to move. A gray-green takes the cup out of his hand and drops it in a bin by the door. Katō watches it land. Then he climbs. The tablet man marks once.

I want to speak — stop, wait, leave him — and I do not. Mei already warned me what happens when I lean on people here. I keep my shoulders down and my mouth shut.

The truck's gate chain goes up. The bay door starts its roll down. The engine takes the weight of the load.

"Pine-road," someone says behind us, quiet and sharp.

Mei.

She is not supposed to be in the kitchen court. Dark jacket over the war-issue shirt, scarred hand on a towel-cart handle she brought as cover. She does not look at the truck. She looks at us.

"Walk," she says. "Now. Not toward the door. Toward C. If you turn your heads I will invent a reason for the corporal to polish you until second bell."

We walk. The truck's gears find the service road. Heavier leaving this time — I am sure of it. I keep my face pointed at the painted line back to C.

---

Halfway to the C turn, a yard guard with a night stick and a tired face steps out of a side door and blocks the corridor.

"You," he says. "C tags. Why are you on the kitchen court after second bell?"

Mei answers before I can invent anything. "East ran short on soap, and I had towels left over from the afternoon run. I pulled two C hands who already knew the cart route. We're walking them back now."

The guard looks at our wet cuffs — ice melt, honest enough — then at Mei's cart. One towel. Not much of an overrun.

"Names?" he says.

I give him Aoyama. Riku gives him Nakahara. Mei gives him Serizawa. The guard writes on a folded card with a pencil stub.

"Kitchen court after bell gets noted," he says. "Mezzanine likes notes. Next time east runs out of soap, Serizawa, you pull east hands, not C. C already has refusals on a tablet. Don't make me add a night walk."

"Understood," Mei says.

He lets us past. The card goes into his pocket. Mei does not look at either of us until we reach the painted stop at the east line.

"You saw?" she says.

"Katō," I say. "On the truck."

"I don't want the name." She tips the nearly empty cart at the letter-door. "I want you in your bay with your mouths shut. Overnight moves leave by the service road. Anybody here a month has heard the engine. Hearing it is not the same as standing in the light watching who climbs."

"Where do they go?" Riku asks.

Mei looks at him for a long second. The knuckles whiten on the cart handle.

"Even if I knew, I wouldn't tell you with a guard writing your names two corridors back." She does not smile. "When C makes noise, east gets quieter. You made noise after second bell. Go sleep. If the board is short again tomorrow, I will be at the junction. If you are at the bay door again, I will not invent towels."

She pushes through the letter-door. It shuts. The silent east guard on the other side does not open it for us.

---

Ōhashi is awake in bay three when we get back. He sits on Katō's empty mattress with the notebook open to the line he drew through Tuesday.

"Well?" he says.

Riku sits on his own bunk and starts rewrapping his forearm again. I sit across and keep my voice low.

"We saw the truck," I say. "They loaded kids — some with tags, some with armbands. Katō climbed on with them. A guard took his cup and threw it in a bin. Canvas sides, out the service road. We still don't know where it goes."

Ōhashi shuts the notebook. His face goes flat.

"Review track," he says. "That's what she called it this morning."

"That's what she called it," Riku says. "Doesn't mean the truck cares."

Ōhashi nods once. The pomade is losing. He looks tired.

"The yard guard stopped us on the way back," I add. "He wrote our names for being in the kitchen court after second bell. Mei told him it was towels. The mezzanine will see that card."

"Of course they will." Ōhashi stands, tries to smooth his hair, fails, and leaves it. "I sent you. If anyone asks, I sent you for crates and you got delayed." He glances at the empty hook. "I still won't tell you where the trucks go. But Katō was on one, and my notebook had him in this bay, and I am tired of pretending those two facts fit."

He goes to his bunk. Lights are already halfway down. Far off, an engine might still be moving past the fence, or it might already be gone. I listen until there is only the wash-room drip and someone in bay four snoring.

Riku's voice comes out of the dim.

"Tomorrow we mop," he says. "We don't run to the bay. We don't ask clerks. We watch the board. If the number drops again, we check beds. If Serizawa tells us to keep our heads down, we keep our heads down."

"Alright."

"Sleep, Aoyama. First bell doesn't wait."
