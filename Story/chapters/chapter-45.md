# Chapter 45 — Same Table

Riku is sitting up on his mattress with his knees against his chest when I get the door shut behind me, which tells me he was already awake, which tells me some part of him knew tonight wasn't going to end the ordinary way.

"You said you were walking off a headache." Not a question. His voice is flat in the dark, careful the way it gets right before it isn't. "That was two hours ago."

"I found him." I don't try to soften it, because softening it now would just be another version of the lie I already told walking out the door. "Kaito. He was standing at the till of a pickle stall three lanes from here, and I've been talking to him since the lanterns went up."

Across the room Mei sits up too. She wasn't asleep either — nobody in this room sleeps light enough to miss a door — and she doesn't say anything yet, just watches me the way she watches a short count on a board, waiting to see where the number lands.

"You went alone." Riku's voice hasn't moved, but something under it has. "After you told me — after Shizuhara, after the pine road, you told me you wouldn't go near him without me standing next to you."

"I know what I said."

"Then say it again so I know you heard yourself."

"I heard myself." My hands are doing the thing they do, smoothing a wrinkle out of my own sleeve that isn't there. "I didn't go looking for him tonight, Riku. I was covering somebody's stall for an hour and he walked up in the line to buy pickled daikon. I didn't choose the moment. I chose what I did after it."

"Which was not to come find me."

"Which was talk to him for five minutes in front of the vendor and then go back tonight, alone, because he asked me to and because I wanted one conversation with him that wasn't performed for an audience first." I make myself hold his eyes instead of the floor. "That part I chose. You're right to be angry about that part."

Riku doesn't say anything for a moment. Mei is the one who breaks it.

"Is he still in this city?"

"Yes. With Mira and an old woman named Suzumori, in a rented room somewhere in the canal wards. We meet again at the stall at dusk. He'll have a place by then."

"Habu's building," Mei says. Not loud. "He's the reason A and B are all dead. He's the reason I spent a night watching a tablet I couldn't put down because it was the only thing keeping my hands from shaking." She says it the way she reads a bad count out loud — correcting it, not performing grief over it. "I want to hear that from him before I decide what I think about a pickle stall reunion."

"You'll get the chance." I sit down on the floor between the two mattresses because standing feels like something I have to justify. "That's what tonight was actually about. He wants everyone in one room. Tomorrow, or the day after — whenever he finds somewhere. You, Mei, Ōhashi. Mira and Suzumori on his side. Everybody says the true things instead of feeding it to each other one careful sentence at a time until somebody feels lied to."

"His words or yours?"

"His, mostly." I almost smile at that, and hate that I almost do. "He's better at naming what's wrong with a plan than I am lately."

Riku rubs both hands down his face and holds them there a second before dropping them. "You understand what you're asking me to do. Sit in a room with the boy who put a rifle-stock bruise on my shoulder the same morning a checkpoint decided to look twice at us. Be civil about it. For your sake."

"I'm not asking you to be civil. I'm asking you to be honest, in the same room, where he can hear it and answer it instead of it just living in you."

"That's worse," Riku says, but there's no real refusal in it, just the sound of a man deciding to do the harder thing anyway. "Fine. One room. I get to say what I came to say, and nobody in that room gets to tell me it isn't the time."

"Nobody's going to tell you that."

Mei lies back down but keeps her eyes open on the ceiling. "I'll come. Not for him. For Habu, and for whatever he's decided to do with the rest of the people who were in that building." A pause. "And because if I don't hear it now, I'll keep wondering the whole time we're in this city, and wondering is worse than knowing."

---

At dusk I go back to the pickle stall the way we said, and I bring Riku with me because that was the other half of the deal — no more secret meetings, no more walking into Kaito alone. Mei stays with Ōhashi and the six; she said if the place is real she will come to the room, not stand in a market lane waiting to be introduced like a package.

Kaito is already there, buying nothing, standing where the barrels block the view from the main path. When he sees Riku his shoulders go tight, then he makes himself leave them down.

"Nakahara," Kaito says. His voice is careful in a way I've only heard him use with strangers he's decided not to underestimate. "I owe you a busted shoulder and everything that came after it. I'm not here to fix that in a pickle lane. I'm here to tell you where."

"Where," Riku repeats.

"Paper shop, east ward. Endo's. My boss trusts me enough now to lend me the back room after closing, no questions, as long as nothing gets set on fire near the good stock." A ghost of something almost like a joke moves across his face and doesn't land anywhere. "Tonight, once the lanterns are lit. Bring whoever's coming. I'll bring mine."

Riku looks at me, then at Kaito. "You walk us there. I am not following a scrap of directions into a building your people picked."

"Fine," Kaito says. "I walk you there. Mira and Suzumori will already be inside."

We collect Mei and Ōhashi on the way. The six stay with the landlady, who's fed stranger arrangements than seven kids arguing across town and doesn't ask why. Ōhashi tells the oldest of them where the stationery shop is if anyone needs him urgently, which is Ōhashi's whole way of saying *be careful* without saying it.

---

Endo's shop at night smells like glue and cut reed, rolls of unfinished lantern paper stacked to the rafters on either side, half-built frames leaning in a row like something waiting to be given a shape. One oil lamp on a crate throws just enough light to make everyone's face look older than it is.

Mira is already there when Kaito pushes the storeroom door open, and Suzumori is seated on an upturned crate with her stick across her knees like she's decided this is where she'll be presiding from. Kaito steps aside so the rest of us can file in behind him. Nobody says hello. There isn't a version of hello that fits.

"Sit if you want," Kaito says. "Nobody's going to make anyone stand through this out of manners."

Riku doesn't sit. "The pine road," he says, straight at Kaito, no run-up. "You put a soldier's rifle down like it was nothing, and then you turned around and told my friend to go home in a voice that took his legs out from under him for a mile. I got hit trying to get between the two of you. That bruise is why we walked slower than we should have. Slower is why the checkpoint that morning had time to actually look at us instead of waving us through. Slower is why we both ended up on Habu's intake line."

"I know," Kaito says. He doesn't look away from it. "I've turned that road over a lot of nights since. I'm not going to tell you it would've gone differently if I'd said something kinder. I don't know that it would have. I know I didn't ask what it would do to you before I said it, and that's on me whether the checkpoint finds you slow or fast."

"That's not an apology."

"No," Kaito says. "I'm short on those lately. The truth is what I had."

Riku's jaw works. He sits, finally, on the edge of a paper crate, like the honesty took more out of him than a fight would have.

Mei doesn't wait for a gap. "Shizuhara," she says. "I was there. Vents. Lobby. Habu. Mira said abort. You stayed. I want to know why *you* stayed — not the schedule. You."

"Because I watched them walk into that shed and I could not make myself leave," Kaito says. "Because walking out felt like agreeing with Habu. Because after Ryn I keep throwing myself at the next bad thing until something stops me. That is not a clean reason. It is the one I had. When it was over I killed every rifle between me and Habu. I did not count them. I am not going to pretend I did."

"You left him alive."

"Mira stopped me." His hand finds Mira's without looking for it, just briefly, and lets go. "I wanted him dead more than I've wanted almost anything. She told me that would be for me, not for anyone he already hurt, and she wasn't wrong, and I hated her for being right for about a day."

"He's still breathing somewhere," Mei says. "Mouthed shut or not, that's a man who knows exactly what happened, sitting in a chair, waiting for whoever finds him to ask the wrong question." She looks at Mira. "Your people left that loose."

"My people didn't leave anything," Mira says, even. "I left it, because the alternative was watching him become someone I didn't recognize over a body that couldn't argue back. I'll live with that choice being wrong before I'll live with the other one."

Suzumori taps her stick once against the crate leg, not sharp, just present. "You are all determined to relitigate a building none of you can go back and fix. Say the useful part instead. What do you do about the coat."

That's the word that turns the room.

"The coat helped us," Ōhashi says, careful, like a man laying a card he's not sure of. "He handed us an envelope with rent money in it and dropped us in this city without asking anything back. Katō rode off with him on his own two legs. Whatever he is, on our side of it he looked like a man clearing a debt."

"On my side of it," Kaito says, and his voice goes flatter, "he stood on a mezzanine and watched children walk into a gassed room and didn't move, and then he loaded my best friend into the back of a truck without asking either of us if that was all right, and drove off into the dark like he'd bought something. I don't care how kind the envelope was. A man doesn't get credit for the rescue if he was close enough to stop the thing he rescued you from."

"I don't know what he saw on that mezzanine," I say. "I was not there for that part. You were. I know what he did once he had us on the truck — he got us out of the wire and into Ashiro. That kept six people and me and Riku and Mei and Ōhashi off Habu's next board. Both of those things can be true at once."

"Convenient, that the same man is a savior on your morning and a bystander on mine."

"Maybe he's both," I say. "I don't like that answer either. It's the only one that fits what we've each actually seen."

Nobody has a better one. The lamp hisses. Somewhere out past the shutters a cart wheel sticks on a cobble and somebody swears at it, ordinary and far away.

"We are not going to solve that man tonight," Suzumori says, into the quiet that follows. "We are not going to solve Habu, or the pine road, or which of you gets to keep the worse guilt. You have all been carrying this since separate roads met a truck, and carrying it together in one storeroom for an hour has not made any of it lighter. It has only made it louder, in stereo, which I could have told you before we started."

"So what were we supposed to do?" Riku says. "Sit quiet and let it fester?"

"No. You were supposed to say it once, out loud, where the other side could hear it land — which you have now done." Suzumori looks around the room, slow, like she's counting faces the way Sato once counted stamps. "Now you are supposed to do the harder thing, which is decide you are not going to fill every hour left in this city arguing about who bled worse."

Mira reaches into her jacket and sets a folded sheet of paper on the crate between the lamp and Suzumori's knee — cheap printed paper, a picture of reed lanterns on a bridge, block characters gone soft at the fold from being carried too long.

"The fifteenth," she says. "Fireworks over the reed pond. All wards welcome. That notice has been sitting on our windowsill for days like a joke we hadn't decided whether to laugh at."

"You want a festival," Mei says, and it isn't quite a question, and it isn't quite mockery either.

"I want one night where nobody in this room has to be careful," Kaito says. "Not pine road, not vents, not the coat — just lanterns and food that costs coins instead of a story, and everybody standing by the same water at the same hour like it's normal, because it's the closest thing to normal any of us are going to get handed for free."

"We don't get nights off," Riku says, but it comes out tired instead of hard, which is its own kind of answer.

"No," Kaito says. "We're taking one anyway."

Ōhashi looks down at his notebook, at the page he hasn't written on all evening, and for once doesn't reach for the pencil. "I'd like that written down as the first plan tonight nobody had to argue into existence."

"Fifteenth," Suzumori says, tapping the paper once with one finger. "All of you. In one place, for one evening, doing nothing more dangerous than watching the sky do something pretty."

"All of us," I say, looking at Riku, then at Mei, then at Kaito last, because some agreements need the person who started this to say it plainly before it's real. "The fifteenth. We go."

"We go," Kaito says.

Riku looks like he wants to argue purely on principle, and doesn't. "Fine. But if anyone so much as raises a voice near that pond, I'm dragging both of you home by the collar, and I don't care whose legs decide to fight me on it."

"Fair," Kaito says, and for the first time all night, something in his face almost settles.
