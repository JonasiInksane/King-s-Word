# Chapter 18 — The Listener at the Bar

The night market gives us nothing useful.

We ask quietly, the way Riku said — a cardigan, an old tram driver, walking north. One man swears he saw a gray-green truck take someone toward the hill. A woman selling roasted corn says an old man slipped that same truck at the pine road and kept going. Neither story has a name on it. Neither brings Gorō back to his stool.

By the second evening the ask has gone cold, and we are back at Minato eating like the work still matters, when a man sits in the corner and does not drink.

I notice him the way you notice a chair that wasn't there yesterday — not because he's loud, because the room has rearranged around a stillness. Mid-thirties, maybe older. Short hair with nothing to say. A plain dark coat too warm for the season. He has a cup of tea going cold in front of him and both hands around it like the tea is the point, and he watches the counter the way Gorō used to watch the beer: as if the glass were a window.

Machi serves him once and doesn't chat. Riku glances over, dismisses him, goes back to arguing with a dock hand about scrap prices. I keep half an eye on the coat because something about the face won't stay put even while I'm looking at it, and that bothers me more than a staring man should.

Later, when the dock hand leaves and the counter is quieter, Riku knocks my bowl with a knuckle. "You're somewhere else."

"Gorō isn't coming back because somebody is going to fix this," I say, low, for him. "Nobody is coming to fix any of this. The world isn't failing. It's wrong."

Riku looks at me a second. "Eat anyway." He doesn't argue. He doesn't make a speech. That is as much answer as I get, and I take it.

The coat man doesn't speak all evening. He is still there when the counter thins. When Machi starts wiping down, he stands, leaves exact change for the tea, and goes out through the curtain without nodding at anyone.

Riku watches the curtain settle. "That one give you a feeling?"

"I don't know."

"Then leave it. Feelings about quiet men in warm coats are how boys get walked to desks." He tips his chin at the back room. "Just go to sleep. We have scrap work at dawn."

I almost do, but then I remember Gorō's voice — *start standing near the places that hide* — and I think about a man who sat through a whole evening of working boys talking about registration and an empty stool and never once asked a question, and I push through the curtain after him.

---

The street outside is damp from a short rain. The bath house has closed. The tool sharpener's shutter is down. The coat man is halfway to the corner, not hurrying, and when I call out — "Wait" — he stops as if he had been waiting for exactly that word.

He turns. Up close the face is ordinary in a way that feels deliberate. Warm enough to trust. Easy to sit beside. I still can't hold it; my eyes keep sliding off toward the collar, the buttons, the wet street behind him.

"You left without finishing your tea," I say, which is a stupid opening and I know it.

"Tea goes cold. Conversations don't always." His voice is pleasant, unhurried. "You were the one talking about the world being wrong. At the counter. To the boy with the scraped knuckles."

I said that — quiet, to Riku, not for the room — and I thought the coat was far enough away. Apparently not.

"I was talking to my friend."

"You were talking where ears reach." He smiles without showing teeth. "I have a soft spot for boys who notice the shape of the damage. Most people only notice the part that hits them." He tips his head toward the alley beside the bath house, out of the streetlamp. "Walk with me a minute. I don't bite, and I don't carry a clipboard."

Riku would tell me to go back inside. Gorō's empty stool tells me sitting still hasn't fixed anything. I join him.

The alley smells of steam and old soap. He stops under the overhang where the light gives up and leans his shoulder to the wall like a man with all night.

"So," he says. "The world is wrong. What would you do about it, if you could?"

"Find my friend, finish something I started saying to him, and go home."

"That's a personal repair. I asked about the world." He looks genuinely curious, not mocking. "You've seen enough to know polite stories don't hold — registration sheets, old men taken for talking, a coast that burns and calls it verification. If the whole arrangement is crooked — not broken, crooked — who puts it right?"

"I don't know."

"Good. The ones who know too fast are selling something." He laughs then — head back, loud enough that it bounces off the alley wall, a jolly sound that doesn't match the coat. "Ideals, though. Keep those. Follow them. Never let anyone talk you down into being useful and quiet while the hill eats the people who drew you maps." He wipes his eye with a knuckle, still grinning. "You want the world reshaped. I can hear it on you. Every boy who sits at that counter long enough starts wanting it."

"I didn't say reshaped."

"You didn't have to." He leans in a fraction. "Reshaped by whom, boy?"

The question lands harder than the laugh. I open my mouth and find the same empty place I found on the ice truck when Riku asked what happens after I catch Kaito.

"I don't know yet," I say.

He laughs again, delighted, as if that were the prize answer. "Honest. I like honest. The world only ever listens to one voice at a time, Aoyama — remember that when somebody offers you a choir." He says my surname like he has been holding it ready, and I go cold because I never gave it to him.

"How do you know my name?"

"Your friend uses it when he tells you to sleep. People are loud about small things." He pushes off the wall. "Go back inside and eat. Don't follow quiet coats into alleys as a hobby — tonight was educational; tomorrow it might not be."

At the mouth of the alley two figures in dark coats are waiting where the streetlamp fails. They don't speak. They don't greet him. They just fold in behind him as he walks, and the three of them turn the corner toward the elevated line, and by the time I take two steps after them the street is empty in a way that feels practiced.

I stand under the overhang until the damp works through my sleeves. His face is already sliding out of my head. The question — reshaped by whom — stays.

---

When I push back through Minato's curtain, Riku is on his feet and angry in the quiet way.

"You followed him."

"He heard me talking. I wanted to know why he cared."

"And?"

"He asked me who reshapes the world. I said I didn't know. Then he left with two men who don't talk." I sit. My hands are steadier than they feel. "He knew my name because he heard you say it. That's all."

Riku stares at me a second longer, then curses under his breath and drops back onto his stool. "Next time a warm coat wants a walk, you take me. Or you don't go. Those are the options."

"Those are the options," I agree.

Machi sets a cup in front of me hard enough to click. "I didn't like him either. Tea drinkers who don't drink are worse than beer drinkers who don't drink." She jerks her chin at the door. "There's a dock runner in here with a mouth full of news. He's been waiting to tell anyone who'll listen. You two look like you need a different problem."

The dock runner is a man I half-know from Thursday bowls — thick wrists, a burn on one forearm from a line. He's already telling it to Gorō's empty stool, as if the stool might still argue.

"Depot warehouse on the east side," he says, when we lean in. "Not the big FDC stack — the civilian one that feeds the hill kitchens. Break-in before dawn, and there wasn't an alarm or a smashed latch. Guard on the door swears he opened it himself and waved the thieves through, then sat down on the step and cried because he can't remember why. He says he never saw their faces — says he never saw them at all, except he must have, because the door was open and the rice was gone." He shakes his head. "Captain's calling it heat and shame. The boys on the dock are calling it something else: a door that opens because a mouth asked."

Riku snorts. "Dock stories. Every week somebody's aunt saw a ghost."

"Ask the guard," the runner says. "He's still on the step. He'll tell you the same thing three times and none of it will have a smashed lock in it."

I hear the rest of the room move on — another beer, another complaint about registration, Machi telling someone the back room is full. I don't move on. A door that opened because a mouth asked is the same wrong shape as a stool man at a well who forgot a boy with blood on his lip, and the same wrong shape as a soldier's hands opening in a drying yard. I would know that shape in any city.

Riku is watching me. "You've gone the color of leftover rice. What?"

"I need to see that warehouse," I say. "Tonight, or at first light — before they move the guard or tidy the story."

"Because a dock runner told a ghost story?"

"Because I've seen that story before, and it wasn't a ghost." I stand up. "Are you coming, or am I walking alone?"

Riku looks at Machi. Machi looks at me, prices whatever she sees, and turns back to her pots.

"Take the back alley," she says. "Pairs like the main street after dark."

Riku clicks his tongue once — help, this time — and pulls his jacket on.

"You set the pace. If this turns out to be heat and shame, you owe me breakfast for a week."

"If it turns out to be what I think," I say, and I don't finish it, because finishing it would mean saying Kaito's name in a bar that still has ears.

We go out through the rope curtain into the damp street and turn east.
