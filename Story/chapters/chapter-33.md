# Chapter 33 — Progressive Clearing

The first pair hits the bay before Habu finishes repeating the order.

The smoker already has his rifle up. Two more gray-greens come hard around the empty dock with theirs leveled at the purge door — at me, at the blood on my mouth, at Mira and Mei behind my shoulder. Habu wanted us held. They came to do it.

"Drop it," I say to the smoker, and I put everything under it. "Kneel. Don't touch the radio."

He drops it. He kneels. His eyes go empty the way the quiet-floor soldier's did. Blood hits my lip hard enough to taste. Mira makes a sound behind me that is not permission.

The nearer of Habu's pair fires.

I do not have time for a person-sentence. The round is already in the air between his muzzle and my chest. What comes out of me is not aimed at him.

"Stop."

The bullet stops.

Not in a wall. Not in Mira. In the air, a handspan from my shirt, spinning wrong, as if the night itself caught it. For one stupid heartbeat nobody breathes — not me, not Mira, not Mei, not the man who fired. Then the round drops to the concrete and rolls into a drain like an ordinary piece of trash.

The gray-green stares at his rifle like it lied to him. His partner freezes mid-step, still waiting for a clean shot that is not coming.

My knees go. Mira catches me under the arm. Something in my skull has split clean open; blood is running from my nose freely now; my vision goes white at the edges and comes back wrong. I have never paid like this for a word. I did not even point the word at a person.

"What did you—" Mei starts.

"Later," Mira says. She is already hauling me upright. "Kaito. Look at me. Can you stand?"

I stand. The kneeling man is still kneeling. Habu's pair is still staring. On the speaker Habu is sharp now. He wants more men in the bay, the mezzanine locked, and someone who can tell him what just happened down here.

I wipe my face on my sleeve and walk at the shooter. The kids on the drains are still behind my eyes. Habu thanking them is still in my teeth. Sitting these men down is not enough.

"Shoot your partner," I tell him. "Then yourself."

He turns. He fires. The partner goes down hard against the stamp desk. Then he puts the muzzle under his own chin and finishes the sentence. The smoker on his knees does not look up. I put the same weight on him before Mira can reach my sleeve.

"You too. Same."

He does. The bay goes quiet except for the radio still crackling Habu's voice. Mei makes a sound like she swallowed glass. Mira's hand locks on my belt and does not let go.

"That was not a door," she says. "That was—"

"The mezzanine," I say. "Habu's glass. The man who thanked them."

Mei stares at the three bodies like her tablet might still make them into numbers. Then she nods once, sharp, and moves.

---

We take the kitchen junction first because it is on the way and because the painted lines still hold the ghosts of armband rows.

Two guards are arguing about whether A/B cleanup needs more bags. They turn when they hear us. One raises a rifle. I do not wait for Mira's hand.

"Put the rifle in your mouth," I tell the one with it up. "Pull the trigger."

He does. The second man is still blinking at the body when I put the weight on him.

"Your turn. Bash your head in."

He follows. Blood sheets over my mouth. The junction smells like copper and old curry. Mira swears and hauls me behind the serving counter. Mei grabs one of the dropped rifles like it might bite her. Mira takes the other without asking permission — checks the chamber the way she checked doors on the quiet floors, then keeps it pointed at the floor.

Boots answer the shots from two corridors at once.

"The left corridor goes back into A-block," Mei says. "You'll hit the cleanup crews and whoever is still pretending this is inventory work. The right stair goes up to the lobby glass — that is where Habu sits."

"We go right," I say.

Mira blocks me with her shoulder — hard enough that it hurts.

"You just stopped a bullet and then you made three men kill themselves," she says, low and furious. "I don't know what that costs and neither do you. If you burn yourself out in the first stairwell, Habu writes you down as a vent problem and the rest of this building keeps thanking kids into drains. Pace it."

"Then keep up," I say.

A third guard comes out of the dish room with a pistol already raised. He is young. His hands shake. He sees the bodies and still tries to be brave.

"On the ground," he says. "All of you. Now."

"Why don't you just slit your own throat," I tell him.

He does. Mira takes one step toward the sink like she might be sick, then locks it down, keeps the rifle, and follows me into the lobby stair.

---

The stairwell fills with gray-greens from above and below.

Geography is simple: concrete steps, metal rail, a landing with a fire door, another landing with a glass panel looking into the dark lobby. Three above. Two below. Rifles up. Someone shouts for us to get on the ground. Mira fires once into the ceiling — warning, not aim — and the shout turns into scramble.

I put weight into the men above first because they have the angle.

"Turn your rifles on each other. Fire until none of you are standing."

The landing becomes a short, ugly noise. Three bodies hit the steps. The ones below fire. Mira shoves me sideways; a round bites the rail where my head was. I put the sentence on them before they can climb.

"Same as the ones above. Now."

They do. Mei kicks the fire door open onto a side corridor and we spill through over spent brass and a man who is still twitching. My ears ring. Blood is in my mouth and in my eyes. The stopped bullet is still sitting in my head like a second skull. I do not try that again. People. Only people. That is enough.

The side corridor runs behind the lobby glass. Through it I see Habu on the mezzanine — soft shoulders, reading glasses in his hair, tea cup in hand — looking down at clerks who have started to understand the gunfire is not a drill. Beside him, for one blink, the dark coat from earlier stands with empty hands and easy weight. Then a clerk runs across the glass and the coat is gone again. Only Habu remains, setting his tea down carefully so it will not spill.

He picks up a handset. His voice fills the building, still soft.

"All units, we have an active disruption on the west stair and kitchen junction. Protect the remaining inventory. C and east stay sealed. Do not invent panic."

Remaining inventory. C. East. The ones he did not finish yet.

I put my palm on the lobby glass hard enough that it should hurt. It does not. Everything hurts somewhere else.

"Open the mezzanine stair," I tell Mei.

"Card only goes so far," she says. "Habu's door is keyed different. You need a person with the right belt or you need to break the glass and climb, and climbing with that much blood on your face is how you die mid-air."

A clerk runs the lobby floor below us with a radio. I smash the side door's window with the rifle butt Mei is not using, lean through, and put the sentence on him before he can finish calling for help.

"Bring Habu's key to this door. Now."

He runs for the mezzanine like the words are the only map he has. Mira watches him go, jaw locked.

"That is still a person," she says.

"So were they," I say.

---

The clerk comes back with a keycard and empty eyes. The mezzanine door opens. He stays in the stair like a tool we have not put down yet. Habu is already standing when we enter the glass office — not running, not armed. Soft. Attentive. The tea is still steaming.

"You must be the west fence," he says, as if greeting a late parent at a school play. "I had hoped we could finish the evening without frightening the remaining children. You've made a great deal of noise."

Mira's rifle tip comes up. Mei stays at the door watching the stair.

I walk to his desk. Blood drips on his blotter. He notices and does not flinch.

"You thanked them," I say.

"I did. Fear makes a mess. Calm keeps dignity." He folds his hands. "If you've come to discuss the volunteer track, I should tell you it was already authorized before you cut my wire. Contingency. You were the reason we moved the schedule up, not the reason the track exists."

"Shut your mouth," I say, and the weight under it is not a joke.

His mouth closes. His eyes stay awake — not emptied, not blank. He hears. He understands. He just cannot open his jaw around another soft sentence. For the first time since the lobby speakers started, Habu looks like a man in a glass box.

Boots hit the mezzanine stair — a lot of them. Habu's last call for units has arrived. Mira fires into the doorframe. Mei shouts a count. Through the glass I can see gray-greens flooding the lobby floor, rifles up, shouting for the mezzanine, shouting for the soft man who cannot answer them anymore.

I walk to the glass rail above them. Blood runs freely down my chin and I let it. Mira grabs for my sleeve and misses.

"Kaito—"

I put everything I have left into the lobby.

"Every man with a rifle — turn it on the nearest man in a gray-green coat. Fire until you are the last one standing. Then fire into your own head."

The lobby becomes one long roar. Men with rifles shoot the man beside them, then the man who turns, then themselves when there is nobody left to aim at. Clerks drop behind desks and stay there. A radio keeps shouting for status that will not come. Habu's eyes go wide above his sealed mouth. Mei covers her ears. Mira is screaming my name and I do not hear the shape of it until the last shot echoes and the floor below is still.

So still.

No more boots on the mezzanine stair. No more rifles hunting the glass. The building's west answer is dead on the lobby tile — Habu's armed shift, Habu's pairs, Habu's kindness with guns. The smoker in the bay. The cleanup argument. The dish-room boy. The stair. The clerk who fetched the key is still breathing in the stairwell behind us, empty-eyed, useless now that the door is open. I do not finish him. I do not remember to.

My legs go. The Word takes what it wants for a bill that size. I hit the glass rail on the way down and Mira catches me before the second fall.

"Stop!" she yells, right against my ear, loud enough that it cuts through the ringing. "Stop it — they're dead, Kaito, they're all dead, you have to stop!"

I try to find another sentence for the next corridor, for medical, for whoever is still breathing in a coat. Mira's arms lock around me hard enough to hurt, face pressed into my bloody shoulder, rifle trapped useless between us.

"Stop," she says again, not a command with weight under it — just Mira, furious and shaking. "I am not watching you empty yourself into a building until there is nothing left of you. You hear me. You stop."

I hear her. The next sentence dies in my throat. Habu sits at his desk with his mouth shut and his tea cooling, staring at the slaughter he ordered into the lobby. Mei is crying without sound at the service door. Below us, nobody moves.

I stop because Mira is holding me and will not let go.

Outside the glass, the corridors toward medical and east are still dark. C is still sealed. The review is over. The men who answered Habu's call are finished.

I do not look for the next rifle.

I let her hold me in the quiet she yelled into existence, blood on both of us, and for the first time since the gallery I do not have a sentence ready.
