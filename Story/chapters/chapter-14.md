# Chapter 14 — Minato

The landlord from last night is already done with us. He takes Mira's last coins for the room and tells her, without looking at me, that same-day work that doesn't ask where you're from lives toward North Market if you're stupid, arcade-side bars if you're hungry, and Station Plaza if you like answering questions until dark.

"We're not stupid," I say.

"Then don't prove me wrong," he says, and shuts the door on the kettle steam.

The narrower door over the takoyaki stand opens with chopsticks still in a woman's hand.

"If you're selling something, downstairs," she says.

"Looking for a place that feeds working boys without a speech about papers," Mira says.

"Minato." The chopsticks point past Mira's shoulder, toward the deeper neon, two turns off the bright strip. "Not the actual harbor. It's the bar's name. Mama there takes cash and bad stories if she decides you are worth it." She glances at me. "Your brother looks like he swallowed gravel."

"Not my brother," Mira says. "We're just traveling together. He needs food."

"Same trouble either way." The door shuts harder than the first one.

On the street I taste copper at the edge of my tongue and hate that I know the word for it now. Mira's hand finds my sleeve before my feet can invent a shortcut through a gray-green pocket.

"Keep your mouth shut," she says. "We walk around them."

"I know."

"Then do it."

---

Minato sits under a pine-named hill's shadow, noren dark with old smoke. The sign is small. Broth and charcoal and something pickled that makes my mouth hurt in a useful way. Inside is warmer than the street. Low counter. Corner stool occupied by an old man in a cardigan with one beer he is not finishing.

A woman behind the counter — fifty-something, bun speared with a chopstick, forearms like she has carried kegs and worse — looks up as we come in. Flat gaze. Prices us in one pass.

She sets down two bowls of oden before we have sat.

"Trouble eats standing up," she says. "That's how I know. Sit before you tip over on my floor."

Mira sits like she has done this in other rooms. I sit because my knees remember the road. The broth hits like a hand on the back of the neck — hot, honest, not kind exactly.

I start once. "We're looking for—"

"Eat first," she says. "Then you can tell me what you're running from and I'll tell you if I care."

I eat. Mira eats like someone who has decided food is fuel and conversation can wait. When the bowls are more empty than full, she sets her chopsticks down and looks at Mama straight.

"Same-day work," Mira says. "Something that doesn't care where we're from."

Mama snorts once. "People who ask before they finish chewing usually get the door. You finished. Fine." She points toward the noren. "North Market loading line takes hands until dark if you show up looking useful. Past the shotengai bend. Don't run there making a scene. Gray-green likes the plaza end."

"Is he hurt," the old man on the corner stool says, not looking up from his beer.

"I'm alright," I say.

"That's a report, not an answer." His laugh sounds like a gearbox. "Name's Gorō. Drove trams in this town longer than that throat has been giving you trouble. One new fact before you leave my morning unfinished: North Market's casual line dies at sundown. After that it's registered crews only, and registered crews want paper. Get there while the light still helps strangers."

Mira glances at him. "We weren't asking for a sermon."

"You got broth. Sermon's free." He tips the beer an inch.

Mama points a chopstick at him without looking. "Enough, Gorō."

"I'm helping."

"You're collecting." She looks at me once more. "You come back still upright, there's rice. You come back with gray-green on your heels, there's the alley and you're not my problem."

Mira pays what we have left that isn't pride. I thank Mama for the bowls.

Outside, the pine-shadow street is cooler. Neon leaks pink at the turn.

"North Market," Mira says. "You load crates and you keep quiet. If someone blocks you, we walk around them. If walking isn't enough—"

"Then what."

"Then I pull you. And you let me." She starts toward the shotengai bend. "Come on. Day's not done."

---

East Yards, later the same day.

I wait by a chain-link gate with a painted number I do not understand, trying not to look like I am apologizing to the fence. Riku has gone between containers with a package and a promise to come back. A yard worker with a clipboard stops anyway.

"You lost?"

"Waiting for my cousin."

"Name."

I point toward the containers and try to look stupid. It works well enough that he shrugs and moves on.

When Riku comes back he has less package and the same crooked-tooth almost-smile.

"Still upright," he says. "Progress." He tips his chin at a patch of cracked asphalt between idle trucks. "Before we ask about your loud friend — first you stop standing like a receipt someone left on a counter."

"I'm just standing."

"You're inviting the next pipe." He walks into the lot space and waits. "Come here. Wider stance. Bend your knees. Hands where you can use them. I'm not making you a soldier. I'm making you harder to steal."

I go, because arguing in an open yard feels louder than following. He shoves my shoulder once. I stumble, catch, don't fall.

"Good," Riku says. "You look like someone who might survive a Tuesday. When we ask about your friend, keep it ordinary — don't look like you're filing a report. If I tell you to shut up mid-sentence, you shut up mid-sentence."

I put my hands down when he clicks his tongue. We try a loader by the container stacks first. He keeps chaining a crate while I talk.

"Sorry — I'm looking for friends who came through yesterday. Did anyone ask you how to get into Central without going past the station?"

He doesn't look up. "A pair did. Late afternoon. I told them the fence route and they headed for the neon."

"Do you know if they found a room?"

"I load boxes. I don't take reservations." He yanks the chain tight. "If they stayed anywhere, it was arcade side. That's all I've got."

Riku buys two canned coffees from a cooler woman, puts one in my hand, and asks her the same thing in fewer words. She mentions the charm-shop stairs and gives us a look that says we are late.

"Central Arcade first," Riku says, once we are moving. "Two doors. If the trail dies, we eat. If it doesn't, you keep walking."

The city comes up around us — fry oil, wet neon, train brakes — and for a second the pink signs could be home.

"Come on, soft boy," Riku says. "The day isn't over."
