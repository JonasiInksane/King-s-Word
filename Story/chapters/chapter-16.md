# Chapter 16 — The City That Works

The man who hates new faces is called Tanabe, and he hates mine on sight.

He runs a wholesale cellar off North Market — dry goods, oil, salt in sacks the size of children — and a handcart route that feeds shops in six wards. When Riku brings me in, Tanabe is doing sums at a standing desk, and he looks at me over his glasses the way the ice driver looked at me, except the ice driver decided in one second and Tanabe enjoys taking ten.

"I don't take boys off the road," he says. "Boys off the road drop a cart once and vanish owing me a ward's groceries."

"He unloaded ice all yesterday morning and washed bowls at Minato last night," Riku says. "Machi let him sleep in the back. You know what that costs her to allow."

"Machi feeds strays. I run a business." Tanabe sets down his pencil. "Give me one reason that isn't this one vouching for you."

I could build something. Sato's careful voice is still in me, the boring-errand boy who got through two checkpoints. But I'm tired of that uniform, and something in me decides that this man with his sums deserves the plain thing.

"I can't drop the cart and vanish," I say. "I'm looking for somebody in this city, and the only way I can afford to keep looking is to be worth keeping. You'd be paying me to stay findable."

Tanabe blinks. Whatever he was lining up to say next doesn't get said. He looks at me a moment longer, and then the no I could see him building just isn't there anymore.

"Be here at six sharp," he says, and goes back to his sums. "Late once and you're done."

Outside, Riku gives me a sideways look, half a step longer than it needs to be. "Huh," he says. "You could sell a wet umbrella." And that's all he says about it.

---

The days find a shape.

Six sharp at the cellar, load by weight so the cart doesn't fight the camber. North Market first while the fish trucks still own the streets, then the long fan outward — the mender's ward, the bath-house street, a noodle row where three shops take the same oil and pretend not to know each other's order. Tanabe pays daily, in coins counted twice, like a man who has been shorted before.

The route teaches me the city faster than a year of wandering would have, and where the route stops, Gorō starts.

He's at Minato most nights, the one beer standing sentry in front of him, and he pays for company in geography. He draws me the city on the counter with a wet finger: the wards like wedges around the harbor, the elevated line and where it actually stops rather than where the map says, the three bridges and which one floods, which streets a handcart can take at noon and which ones will get you cursed at in two dialects.

"Forty years on the trams," he says. "I know this city the way you know a person. And like a person, it's been getting sicker. My line got shortened twice — materiel priority, they said, everything up the hill gets the steel first." He says up the hill the way other men say a name they're not speaking. "You'll see. Everything in this city that stops working, stops working from the hill down."

"What's on the hill?"

"Offices," Gorō says, and drinks, which is how I learn that even the free map has one district it charges for.

Work, food, sleep — I stand near all three, the way the tea seller said. On the route I listen at every shop door for a scraped voice. At the market I watch the day-labor lines forming at dawn, rows of backs and caps, and learn how many tall, thin, dark-haired boys one city can hold. There's a registry office two streets off Station Plaza where new arrivals are supposed to declare lodging, and a bus depot with a board listing every arrival from the east, and I don't go inside either one, because desks keep lists, and a boy asking after a boy makes two entries in one line. Kaito's trail being cold hurts less than it did in Ishibashi. Cold means no story has grown legs. Cold means no yard has broken sideways with him standing in the middle of it. I tell myself cold is what careful looks like from the outside, and most days I believe it.

Once, a fish man on the noodle row mentions a coast boy working evening loads at the docks — young, keeps to himself, voice like he gargled the harbor. I take the cart back early and stand where the shift lets out. The boy comes through with the crowd, twenty-five if he's a day, throat wrecked from tar fumes, and the girl who meets him has a baby on her hip.

I walk the long way home. Wrong boy. Next time I hear about a coast voice, I'll go look again.

---

Thursday, I learn, is a ceremony.

Thursday nights Machi feeds working boys whether they can pay or not — one bowl, one refill, no discussion. The counter fills with dock hands and market boys and a tram conductor who knew Gorō when they were both young enough to be handsome, according to Gorō. Machi runs it like a harbor master, and I watch her read every new face at the curtain the way she read mine, and I never once see her get it wrong.

The door to her back room drags. It has dragged since I first slept behind it — you have to lift the handle and shoulder it, and everyone does, and nobody fixes it, because a bar full of working men is somehow never working. The second Thursday I borrow a plane and a screwdriver from the tool sharpener up the street, take the door off its hinges before the evening rush, and rehang it square.

Machi watches me do it from the pots. She doesn't say thank you. When I sit down she puts an egg in my oden that I didn't ask for and charges me for a bowl without one. At the end of the counter Gorō taps his glass. "That door's been dragging since the flood of '02," he says. "I was there. Want to hear about it?" Somebody groans. He tells it anyway. It's a good story, and it's better the third time.

Riku, for his part, teaches me the city's other language.

He catches two dock boys sizing us up on the walk home one night — nothing in it yet, just two pairs of eyes weighing us from a doorway — and after they've moved on he stops me under the rail line.

"You walk wrong," he says. "Same as the day I met you. You walk like you're following something, head down, all your weight going forward. To a certain kind of eye that says nobody's expecting this one anywhere. That's what got you the ditch outside Kuroishi." He squares me up with two hands like I'm furniture. "Keep your chin level and put your shoulders back where they live. You walk like somebody's holding your seat at a counter, because somebody is. Doorway boys count that stuff better than clerks count stamps. Make them pick someone else."

"That's the whole trick? Pretend I'm expected?"

"You are expected, idiot. Machi holds the mats. Tanabe holds the route. It stopped being pretending two Thursdays ago." He clicks his tongue and walks on. "Keep up."

I stand under the rail line for a second with that landing on me, and then a tram goes over and shakes the whole street like a pan, and I catch him up.

He's right, is the thing. Some evening while I was watching for it the city stopped being a place I was passing through. The coins I keep in three knots of cloth prove it: one knot for food, one knot for Tanabe-day mornings when the cart needs a wheel or I need gloves — and one knot, the heaviest, that I don't spend. Two bus fares east, and a bit over. I count it some nights, back-room lamp low, and I can never decide if it's a plan or a prayer. Two fares means I'm still going to find him. Two fares means we're still going home.

---

Then, on a morning like the other mornings, I get to the cellar at six sharp and Tanabe isn't doing sums. He's standing in the cellar mouth looking up the roofed street, where three gray-greens with clipboards are moving stall to stall in the dawn rush — not searching, not stopping anyone. Counting. One writes while two look, and they look at hands and backs, at the boys hauling crates, at the casual line forming by the fish trucks, and the market noise around them is a note lower than any dawn I've heard here.

"They did the docks yesterday," Tanabe says, quiet, not to me exactly. "They counted the evening shift out the gate. A man I know says they were counting the difference — book crew against real hands."

At the top of the street, where the notices go — bus schedules, boil warnings, the mayor's blessings — a fourth soldier is pasting up a fresh sheet, white as a bone against the old weathered paper. People are already drifting toward it in the way people do, wanting to know and not wanting to be seen wanting to know.

Riku appears at my shoulder, drawn up the street by the same lowered noise as everyone else, and we read it together over a fishmonger's head.

LABOR REGISTRATION — ALL CASUAL AND DAY-HIRE WORKERS. By order of the Federation Defense Command, all unregistered laborers must present themselves at their ward office within seven days. Employers of unregistered labor after this period are subject to penalty. Registration requires proof of residence and origin documentation.

Origin documentation. Two words for everything I don't have, at the bottom of a page with a seal on it, seven days wide.

Riku reads it twice. He clicks his tongue, once, and it doesn't mean help this time.

"That's aimed at us," he says.
