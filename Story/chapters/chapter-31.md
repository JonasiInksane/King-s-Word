# Chapter 31 — The Stove

The upper slit above the loading bay is a maintenance walk with a grated floor and a smell of oil that gets into the teeth.

Mei leads. Mira hates every step and takes it anyway. The contact stays at the turn behind us with the cutters, watching the corridor we just left. Below us the service court is white under the floodlights — concrete, a canvas truck backed to the bay door, two gray-greens with rifles, a clerk with a stamp box, and a folding line of armband kids being walked out of the kitchen junction in groups of ten.

Not running. Not crying loud enough to carry. Walking the way you walk when someone soft has told you that cooperation is warmth.

Habu's voice comes through the bay speakers, the same lean-in as before.

"A and B evening review, first group. Thank you for your patience. Step onto the truck when your number is called. Leave cups and personal items with the clerk — you will not need them on the short ride, and we would rather they not get lost." A small laugh, almost shy. "I know the lights are unkind. Hold onto each other if you need to. Inventory that stays calm gets home sooner."

Home. He says it like the word still has a house attached to it — not a truck and a blank line.

The first ten climb onto the truck. A boy still has a cup in his hand; the clerk takes it from him and drops it into a bin without looking at his face. Beside him a girl fusses with her armband so it sits straight on her sleeve, then sits on the bench like she is trying not to take too much space. One of the gray-greens checks names against a list and waves the next kid forward. Nobody asks which town the west gate opens onto.

Mira's fingers dig into the grate rail.

"We've seen it," she says. "Blank destination. Soft voice. Now we go before the second group fills the court. Mei — move."

Mei does not move. She is counting under her breath, matching the truck bench to the night sheet still crumpled in her fist.

"That is thirty," she says. "A-block kitchen runners. Thursday curry kids. They waved at C on the way to dinner two days ago."

"Mei," Mira says. "I am not debating the menu. The west wire is still our exit. Suzumori is still in the ditch. If we stay for the whole show, we die in a building that already knows our faces."

"Then leave," Mei says, without looking at her. "Take him and your clean road and go. I'm staying until I know whether the second group comes back."

Below, the truck's canvas sides flap once. The clerk stamps something. The gray-greens close the tailgate. The engine turns over — the light close motor we heard from the wrong side of the night — and the truck rolls toward the west gate under the floodlights.

It does not go far.

It stops inside the inner fence line, at a low windowless annex I took for a shed from the ditch. The bay door on that annex rolls up. No town road. No open country. Just a concrete mouth and a strip of harsher light, and the armband kids being walked down from the truck into it in the same neat ten.

A sound starts inside that I do not have a clean name for — machinery, fans, something that wants air moved in a hurry. One of the kids stumbles on the ramp. A guard steadies him by the elbow, gentle as Habu's lobby hand on a crying boy's head, and walks him in.

The annex door rolls down.

The court is empty except for the clerk's bin of cups.

I hear Mira say my name. I do not answer. My hands are locked on the grate rail hard enough that the metal bites.

---

"Kaito." Mira gets in front of me on the grate, close enough that I have to see her face instead of the closed annex. "Listen to me. We came for one pull. The building is doing something worse than we booked for. That does not mean you stand here until they finish. It means we take Mei, we cut back to the wire, and we live long enough to burn this place with people who are not already inside it."

"They're kids," I say.

"I know they're kids." Her voice shakes once and she kills it. "I have been counting dead kids since before I met you. Tonight I can still get one person out of a letter-wing. That is the job you agreed to in the ditch. Say you remember."

"I remember." Blood is in my mouth again. "I also remember Habu thanking them for holding still. I remember the blank line. I remember the annex that isn't a road." I look past her at Mei. "How many groups?"

"A and B together run about two hundred," Mei says. Quiet. Precise. The tablet girl, not a speechmaker. "If they keep tens, you are watching the first of twenty. Contingency nights do not leave leftovers for breakfast."

Mira turns on her.

"You brought us here to watch them die so we would feel guilty enough to stay. That is not a map. That is a hostage."

"I brought you here because you asked me to walk while they were still inventory on a wall," Mei says. "Now they are inventory in a shed. If your extract cannot survive knowing that, it was never an extract. It was a theft."

The contact's voice cuts up the stairwell, low and ugly. "Second group is leaving the junction. Also boots on our corridor. We are out of quiet."

Below, another ten armbands step into the court. Habu's soft voice thanks them for their patience. A cup hits the bin. The annex door starts up again. One of the kids — tall for fourteen, cloth band crooked — looks up at the grated slit as if he felt eyes on him. For a stupid half-second I think he will shout. He only frowns, confused, and a guard turns his shoulder toward the ramp with the same fond two-finger push I saw in the junction.

"Don't," Mira says, because she felt me lean toward the rail. "You look, or you leave. You do not call down to them. You do not make them a reason for rifles to find this walk."

"I'm looking," I say.

"Then keep your feet."

The tall kid goes into the harsher light. The door starts down. The fans pull.

Mira grabs my jacket.

"We go," she says. "Now. You open one door if we hit a guard — one — and then you shut that mouth until the ditch. Mei, you are coming if I have to drag you by the shirt."

Mei looks at me, not at Mira.

"Is that still your plan?" she asks. "One door. One girl. Two hundred blanks."

I watch the second group climb the short ramp into the harsher light. A boy near the back turns his head as if he heard something through the fans, and a guard's hand on his shoulder turns it forward again, kind, firm, finished.

Something in my chest that has been holding since the quiet floors — since the pine road, since *go home*, since every clean sentence that bought us an exit — lets go in a way that is not relief.

"No," I say.

Mira's grip tightens. "Kaito—"

"No." I pull free. The scraped voice stays quiet; the decision does not need weight under it yet. "You can take her to the ditch. You can call it clean. I am not walking out of a building that just thanked children for lining up into a shed."

"You stay and you die," Mira says. "Or worse — you use that mouth until there is nothing left of you, and I am the one who brought you here to watch it happen."

"Then you shouldn't have pointed me at the door," I say. "You wanted a key. Keys cut both ways. Suzumori said it. I am done pretending I only open the doors you like."

Mei folds the night sheet smaller and shoves it into her shirt.

"If you stay, I stay," she says. "I can get you into the medical stair above A-block before the third group loads. I cannot stop the annex. I can show you where the speaker wire runs, and where Habu's glass office looks down, and which guards still think this is a school drill." She glances at Mira. "You can drag me. You will lose the boy either way."

Mira looks at me like she is watching a roof edge with a girl on it and no clean sentence left.

"Abort," she tells the contact, rough. "Ditch. Tell Suzumori the extract failed. Do not wait on the wire past first light."

"And you?" the contact asks.

"I stay long enough to keep him from eating the first rifle that points at him." Mira does not look soft when she says it. She looks like someone who has already lost the night she booked and is pricing the next mistake. "After that — I don't know. Move."

The contact goes. Boots from the far corridor get louder. Below us the second ten disappear into the annex. The door rolls down. The fans pull harder.

I wipe my mouth. No blood this time — I haven't spent a sentence — and somehow that is worse, because the thing waiting in my throat has nowhere to go yet.

"Medical stair," I tell Mei. "Before the third group."

She nods once and takes the grated walk toward a dark door marked with a red cross stencil, not a letter.

Mira falls in on my left, jaw set, already watching exits.

"You do not open a mouth on a child," she says under the fan noise. "Not to save them. Not to stop them. Not to make this easier to watch. If you break that, I will put you down myself and drag what is left to the ditch. Clear?"

"Clear," I say.

It is not a promise that the night ends clean. It is a promise that the first wrong door I open will not be a fourteen-year-old's eyes.

At the medical stair, Habu's voice comes back through a wall speaker, still soft, still thanking, still calling the next ten by number like dinner.

I go down into the building that is shaped on purpose, with Mira's shoulder against mine and Mei's tablet light cutting the dark ahead.

The third group is already forming in the court. The annex door is already rising.

I follow Mei into the stairwell with my mouth shut. Mira's shoulder stays against mine. Below us Habu's soft voice keeps thanking children into the shed.
