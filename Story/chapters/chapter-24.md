# Chapter 24 — East Against the Word

My feet are walking east and I did not ask them to.

That is the only honest way to say it. The pine road runs downhill out of the scrub toward the far smudge of Takamatsu, and my body takes it at a good traveling pace — arms swinging, breath even, a boy with somewhere to be. Inside the boy, I am telling my knees to stop. They don't answer. The two sentences sit behind my eyes where he left them, not loud, not cruel, just load-bearing. *Stop following me. Go home.*

Riku keeps pace beside me and says nothing. He has blood dried at his hairline and he carries his left forearm tucked against his ribs where the rifle butt caught it. Every few hundred meters he looks over at me — not scared. Pricing.

"So," he says at last. "Are we going to talk about the part where a soldier put his hands on his head and knelt in the road because your friend told him to?"

"You saw it."

"I saw a lot of things this morning. I'm choosing which one to start with." He steps over a fallen branch without breaking stride. "A grown man with a rifle knelt like it was gym class. Then your friend aimed two sentences at you, and your face went somewhere else, and now you're marching toward the city we just committed a crime outside of, at a pace I'd call cheerful. So talk to me, Aoyama, or I stop walking next to whatever this is."

"He's not my cousin," I say, because we should bury the oldest lie first.

"I worked that out around the second rifle." He doesn't slow. "Keep going."

"I don't have a name for what he does." That is true, and I hold onto it, because the true parts are the only footing I have. "When he puts weight under his voice, people do what the words say. I've seen it three times now — a drying yard in Nagihama, a shrine yard, this morning. I don't know the rules of it. I don't think he fully knows them either. And this morning he put that weight under *go home* and pointed it at me, and my legs have been someone else's ever since."

Riku is quiet for ten steps. When he speaks again his voice has lost the crooked-tooth angle it keeps for the world.

"Where do your legs think home is? Exactly."

I've been avoiding the question for an hour, because I already know, and knowing is worse. Kōgetsu is a name on an exclusion map. Sugi's house holds Kaito's grief, not mine. The road doesn't count. Which leaves one door with a rope curtain and one back room that smells of dry rice, where a woman ladles oden at boys who worked, and my body — my stupid, honest body — decided that is where a home goes.

"Minato," I say.

Riku stops walking. I don't. That's the point. He has to jog to get back to my shoulder.

"No," he says. "No, you are not taking a pine-road assault through Machi's curtain. Every pair in that city is going to be shaking bars and bath houses for two boys within a day, and you want to hand her a reason to lose more than a stool."

"I don't want any of this. My feet aren't asking me."

"Then we make them ask." He gets in front of me and plants his good hand flat on my chest. My legs keep going. He gives ground, boots skidding on the grit, swearing with great fluency, and then he stops trying to be a wall and becomes a hook instead — arm around my shoulder, hip turned in, and we both go down into the dry ditch in a tangle of elbows.

My body gets up. It isn't angry. That is the worst part — no fury in it, just an errand. Riku hangs off my arm with all his weight, heels dragging furrows.

"Fight it," he says through his teeth. "Whatever it is, it's your body and his sentence. Somebody's name is on the deed. Make it yours."

I fight it. There is nothing to grab. It is like arguing with the current when you are already swimming — the road pulls, the pull is patient, and every time I stop one leg the other has already voted. I get a fistful of dead grass at the ditch lip and hold on and shake, and my legs walk against the anchor of my own arm until something in my shoulder burns.

"Talk," Riku pants. "You're the talker. Talk."

So I talk — not to him. To the sentence.

"He said *home*." My voice comes out low and cracked and I let it. "Kōgetsu is gone. There's tape where my street was. Nagihama was never mine — I slept in a shed and owed a woman vegetables. The bar is Machi's, and the back room came with wash-up duty, and if I bring soldiers through her curtain it stops being anyone's home at all." The grass tears. I grab a root instead. "He said *go home* like it was an address. It isn't an address. It was him. It was a roof and a girl and him, and two of the three are gone, and the third one just—"

Something lets go.

Not like a rope cut. Like a rope that has been hauling on a ring in a wall, and the wall quietly admits there is no ring, there never was, and all that force sits down in the mud at once. My legs stop. All of me stops. I fold onto the ditch grass with my ears ringing and my shirt soaked through and my hands shaking like I have carried something heavy up a long flight of stairs, and Riku sits half on top of me, breathing hard, not letting go of my arm yet.

"You stopped," he says.

"I stopped."

He rolls off me and lies in the ditch looking at the sky, cradling the bad forearm. "Good. Because I have maybe one more of those in me, and then you were going to drag us both into Station Plaza by the ankles."

We lie there while my breath comes back. A truck goes by up on the road — civilian, fish crates — and neither of us moves until it's gone.

"The other sentence," Riku says, eyes still on the sky. "*Stop following me.* Is that one still in there?"

I try it the way you'd press a bruise — careful, just enough to feel if it still hurts. *Stop following me.* West is where he went. If I stand up right now and start walking after him, I don't know whether my legs will do it or lock the way they did for *home*.

"I don't know," I say. "It didn't fray with the first one. It's simpler. It has him in it, and he isn't gone the way home is."

"So if you turn west right now and try to walk after him, either you can or your legs go on strike again, and we find out which in front of whoever's watching the roads." He sits up, spits grit, and looks at me properly. "That's an experiment for somewhere with fewer rifles."

"Riku." My voice is steadier than I feel. "You could go alone. You're a scrap hauler who got robbed on the road. Next to me, with that rifle bruise, you're an accomplice."

He looks at me for a long second. Then he clicks his tongue, once, and it means what it usually means.

"You still owe me breakfast for a week," he says. "You think I'm letting you disappear owing me food?" He stands, hauls me up by the collar with his good arm, and steadies me with one finger on my chest until I stop swaying — the same as the first day, on a different road. "We go north of the road, cross-country, and we argue about west when your legs are yours for a full hour. Walk, Aoyama. Slowly, this time. Like a person."

---

We don't get the hour.

The pine road meets the inland road at a crossing with a dry shrine and a concrete water tank, and the crossing has grown a checkpoint since yesterday — one canvas truck, cones, four gray-greens, and a folding table that means somebody intends to stay all day. We see it from the treeline. There is no way around that doesn't mean open field in both directions, and we are two boys who look exactly like two boys avoiding a checkpoint.

"Through it," Riku says grimly. "Fast is guilty. Bored is invisible."

"Let me do the talking."

"That sentence has started to worry me."

The corporal at the table is maybe thirty, with a farmer's neck and a clipboard he writes on slowly, like the pen is expensive. He takes the seasonal-work story the way a man takes weak tea — no objection, no pleasure. I keep my shoulders down and my voice ordinary and answer only what is asked. Where from: the coast, burned out, like everyone. Where to: wherever there's work that feeds. Papers—

"Papers," he says.

"We were robbed outside Ishibashi in the spring." True, and I give it to him plainly, with the tiredness it deserves. "Three men and a pipe. They took the transit chit and the money. Since then we work for people who don't need paper, and we were hoping to register properly once we—"

And it is working. I can feel it working, the way I could feel it work on gates and clerks all the way down this country — his pen lifts, his shoulders come down half a degree, the day is long and we are boring and there is a version of the next minute where he waves us through to keep his line moving. The pen hovers over the release column.

Then he catches himself.

I watch it happen. He doesn't know what he is catching — there is nothing to catch, just a polite boy and a tired morning — but some part of him notices that he was about to do something easy, and the noticing offends him. His neck goes red. The pen comes back down like a boot.

"Everyone's boring today," he says, to himself more than to me, and his voice has gone flat and personal. "Whole road full of boring boys, the one morning command has us out here over an assault." He looks up, and now he is actually looking. "Two boys, they told us. One tall one who fights."

"Lot of tall boys on the coast road," Riku says. Easy. Too easy.

"Roll your sleeve up."

The bruise on Riku's forearm has come up purple and yellow through the morning, and it has a shape. Anyone who has done the drills knows the shape. The corporal looks at it the way a man looks at a winning ticket he almost threw away.

"Rifle stock," he says. "Fresh." And then, over his shoulder, not loud, which is somehow worse: "Sergeant. These two."

Riku's mouth opens with something that would have made things worse, and I step on his foot with everything I have.

They are not gentle and they are not cruel. That frightens me more than either would have. They take belts and laces, they pat us down with the boredom of men who do this in their sleep, and nobody asks a single question about this morning — not who knelt, not who shouted, nothing. The corporal writes. A private wires a paper tag to each of our jackets with a number on it and no line for a name.

"You'll be reviewed," the corporal says, already looking past us at the next body on his road. "Processing will sort what you are."

"Sort us where?" I ask, because a real question is the only tool they haven't taken.

He shrugs. "Inland. There's a facility. You'll eat better than you've been eating, by the look of you." He signs something and tears it and keeps both halves, which tells me more about where we are going than the word *facility* does.

They put us up into the canvas truck with a bench down each side. There is one other passenger — a gray-stubbled man in a seed-company cap who doesn't look at us, holding his own tag in both hands like it might be exchanged for something later.

Riku settles onto the bench across from me, careful of the arm. Through the open tailgate I watch the checkpoint shrink — cones, shrine, water tank — until a bend takes it. This morning my legs wanted east. The truck is going the other way.

"Hey." He says it quietly, under the engine, and waits until I look at him. "Back in the ditch. Your legs were yours at the end. Whatever pit they're driving us to — remember whose legs they are."

The soldier riding the tailgate leans in and flicks Riku's tag with one finger, friendly as a schoolteacher.

"Don't lose the number," he says. "Boys who lose their number get counted the hard way."
