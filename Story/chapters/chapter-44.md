# Chapter 44 — Without Them

I count the till twice because the first count doesn't feel true, and somewhere under the second count I realize I'm stalling — not because the coins are wrong, but because the second I finish, there's nothing left standing between me and the thing I told Riku I wouldn't do without him.

He made me say it out loud, our first morning in this city, before either of us had eaten — sat me down on the boarding house stairs and waited until I said *I don't go near Kaito without you standing next to me*, then nodded once like a man closing a deal. I said it because I owed him that. That morning, I believed it.

I'm going to break that promise in about four minutes, and the only honest thing I can do about it now is not pretend otherwise while I'm doing it.

The stall man left twenty minutes ago with his barrels and his instructions — lock the till, drop the shutter, leave the key with the noodle cart two stalls down. I've done all of it except the shutter. I leave a gap at the corner wide enough for a boy to duck through sideways, and I sit down on the upturned crate to wait, because standing felt like something I'd have to explain to my own legs.

Kaito ducks through the gap a little after the second string of lanterns catches down the lane. He doesn't say anything at first. He just stands there in the narrow space between the barrels with the lamp light cutting half his face into shadow, and for a second neither of us moves.

Then he crosses the stall toward me, and I feel it before I understand what I'm feeling — some part of my legs deciding, on their own, without asking me, that they are not allowed to close that distance from the other side. It isn't pain. It's more like trying to walk against water that only exists for me. I push through it anyway, standing up off the crate to meet him halfway, and it breaks the way ice breaks under a boot — sudden, then just gone, like it was never load-bearing in the first place.

"You felt something," Kaito says. He's close enough now that I can see the thin scar through his eyebrow catching the lamp. "Your face just did a thing."

"I'm fine." It's not a lie exactly. Whatever that was, it's already over. "I didn't know if I'd be able to walk toward you. After the pine road. I wasn't sure if it was still — holding."

Something goes still in him. "Stop following me. Go home," he says, quiet, like the words themselves might still be armed. "You remember it that clean."

"I remember every word of it that clean." My voice comes out flatter than I mean it to, all the careful going out of it at once. "I remember my feet turning east before I decided anything. I remember walking for an hour thinking about my mother's apartment like it was still standing there waiting, and then somewhere past the second checkpoint I couldn't remember which floor. That's when it broke. Not because I was strong. Because you told a version of me to go somewhere that doesn't exist anymore, and my body didn't have anywhere left to walk to."

Kaito doesn't flinch away from that the way I expect him to. He just stands with it, jaw tight, hands loose at his sides like he's decided not to hide behind moving them for once.

"I didn't have a better sentence," he says. "You were about to die next to me on that road, and the only thing my mouth could reach for fast enough was something that would put miles between you and whatever was coming. I'm not going to tell you I'm sorry I said it. I'm sorry I didn't know a way to save you that didn't take your legs away from you while I did it."

"That's not the same as sorry."

"No," he says. "It's not. It's the truth instead."

I want to be angrier than I am. I spent weeks building the shape of this exact conversation in my head — on the road east with Riku pulling me by the collar, in a bunk in Shizuhara, in a truck bed watching him go past in a ditch with a girl's hand fisted in his jacket — and every version had me saying something that actually cut. Standing here with the real him in front of me, all I've got is the plain fact of it, laid out instead of thrown.

"You don't get to decide what happens to my legs," I say. "Not again. If you ever put weight under a sentence pointed at me again, I want you to at least look at me while you do it. Not shout it over your shoulder and watch it work from a distance."

"I looked at you the whole time," Kaito says. "That's the part you don't know. I watched your face go empty and I kept watching it, because turning away would've been letting myself pretend I didn't do it."

That lands somewhere I didn't leave room for. I sit back down on the crate because my legs have earned a rest twice in five minutes, and Kaito lowers himself onto a barrel across from me, close enough that our knees almost touch in the narrow gap between the stacks.

"Shizuhara," I say, because if I don't say it now I'll find a reason not to. "I need to know what you did there. Not the version Mei gave us secondhand through a locked door. Yours."

His hands find each other, thumb working over a knuckle. "How much do you already have?"

"Vents. Gas, then a hose. A and B, all of them, in groups of ten. Habu's mouth shut and left in his own office, still breathing. Guards on the west fence, dead, however many of them there were." I make myself keep my voice level saying it, because if I let it shake now I won't get through the rest. "Riku said your name in the corridor before Mei even told us hers. He said, *that was Kaito. Same boy. Same mess.* I want you to tell me if he was right."

"He was right." No hedge in it. No softening. "I didn't kill the ones in the vents. I got there after that part was already done — I heard it happening below me and couldn't reach it in time. What I did was everyone standing between me and Habu once I knew. The guards who came at us. The two who tried to stop us at the medical stair. I stopped being able to count somewhere in the kitchen corridor."

"And Habu?"

"I left him alive." His jaw works once. "Ending him would have been for me, not for anyone he already hurt, and Mira wouldn't let me pretend otherwise. I shut his mouth instead. He's still up there behind that glass, breathing, unable to give an order to anyone, for as long as that holds."

"You could have died in there."

"I know."

"You could have made me watch you die in there, from a truck bed, going the wrong direction, unable to do a single thing about it." My voice does shake now, and I let it. "That's the part I can't put down. Not the guards. Not even Habu. The idea that the last time I ever saw you could have been through the slats of a truck, and I would have spent the rest of whatever life I've got not knowing if you walked out of that building or not."

Kaito's throat moves. "I saw you too," he says. "In the ditch. I've thought about that stretch of road more than almost anything else since. Half a second, and then you were gone around a bend, and I didn't even get to say your name loud enough for it to reach you."

"I heard you," I say. "I couldn't make out the words over the engine. I saw your mouth start something and then Mira pulled you down before it finished, and I spent the next three days furious that I'd never know what it was."

"I don't remember what I was going to say." He looks almost embarrassed admitting it, which is its own strange comfort — that some things got lost for him too. "Might not have been words at all. Just noise. Your name, maybe, without anything after it."

We sit with that for a second, the lamp hissing low between us, brine and cut daikon thick in the air.

"I'm not the same as I was on the roof," Kaito says eventually. "I've watched what happens in my own hands now, and I don't get to pretend it's still just bad luck and a temper. I need you to know that before we decide anything, because if you're hoping I'll turn back into the version of me from before the truck took Ryn, I'm not going to lie and say that's still in here waiting to come back out."

"I don't want him back," I say, and mean it more than I expected to when the sentence started. "I want to know who you are now. That's different. I've changed too — something happened at that fence I still haven't told Riku the whole story of, because I don't fully understand it myself yet."

Something sharpens in Kaito's face, quick and gone. "What happened?"

"Not tonight," I say, and it costs me to say it, because I can see he wants it and I'm choosing to keep it anyway. "I'll say it out loud eventually. Just not standing in a stranger's pickle stall with a shutter that isn't even locked."

He doesn't push. He just nods, slow, and for once that doesn't feel like distance between us — it feels like the first honest boundary either of us has drawn since the pine road, the kind that says *not never, just not yet* instead of *never ask again.*

"So what happens now?" I say. "You go back to Mira and Suzumori and don't tell them. I go back to Riku and Mei and Ōhashi and don't tell them. We meet in pickle stalls until the festival, and nobody who actually cares about either of us knows we've found each other."

"That's not a plan. That's just delaying the part where somebody gets hurt finding out sideways." Kaito rubs a hand over his jaw, and for a second he looks less like the boy who emptied a lobby and more like the one who used to argue with a teacher over a seating chart. "Riku already knows my name. Mei already knows it. If Mira sees you on a street corner before I've told her myself, that's worse for everybody than doing this the hard way once."

"I made Riku a promise," I say. "I talk to him before I go chasing you again. I already broke half of it just by being here tonight without telling him first."

"Then don't break the rest of it. Tell him tomorrow, first thing, exactly how this happened — you didn't go looking, I walked up in a line. That's true. It's also not going to make him less furious that you didn't run straight to him after."

"No," I say. "It won't."

"So we do it together," Kaito says. "Not just us circling each other in an empty stall like criminals. Everybody. Riku, Mei, Ōhashi. Mira, Suzumori. One room, one night, before the festival turns this whole city into a crowd we could lose each other in for good this time. Say what needs saying where all of it gets heard at once, instead of feeding it out one careful sentence at a time until somebody feels lied to."

It's not a soft idea. I can already see it going wrong — Riku and Mira in one room with weeks of separate roads behind them, Suzumori's opinions about kept things, Mei's flat refusal to be anyone's steady hand — but I can also see the alternative clearly enough to know it's worse: both of us running secret errands to the same pickle stall until someone's patience runs out first.

"They're not going to like each other," I say.

"They don't have to like each other," Kaito says. "They just have to sit in the same room long enough to say what actually happened instead of what sounds safe."

Outside the gap in the shutter, footsteps come down the lane at a pace that isn't a stroll — two sets, unhurried but deliberate. Kaito goes still the way he does now, listening with his whole body instead of just his ears, and I find myself doing the same.

They pass — just two men heading for the noodle cart, voices fading into the market noise beyond the barrels.

"We can't keep doing that every time somebody walks past," I say, quieter now. "Jumping at footsteps in a stall that isn't even ours."

"Then let's not have a reason to jump," Kaito says. "Tomorrow. Tell Riku everything tonight so it's not a fresh wound in the morning. I'll tell Mira the same. We find a place big enough for all of us and small enough that nobody official notices seven people arguing in it."

"This stall at dusk," I say. "Same place. You tell me where when you have a room. I am not handing you our boarding house so you can knock and get Riku's fist before you get a sentence out."

"Fair," he says. "Dusk. Here. If the stall man is back, we talk like customers and walk."

"Tomorrow," I say, because saying it once makes it real in a way that thinking it hasn't managed yet. "I'll bring them wherever you say. You bring yours."

"Tomorrow," Kaito says, and holds out his hand — not for a shake, just open, like he's not sure what to do with it now that the sentence is finished. I take it anyway, because some things don't need a better reason than that.

Down the lane, the noodle cart's steam whistle lets out a short, ordinary shriek, and both of us laugh before either one decides to, startled, the way you laugh at nothing when everything in you has been braced too long for something worse.

"Go home before Mira starts walking the canal looking for you," I say. "I still have to lock this shutter properly, or the stall man's going to notice whose fingerprints are all over his barrels."

"Tomorrow," Kaito says again, backing toward the gap, like repeating it enough times will keep either of us from finding a reason to back out.

"Tomorrow," I say back, and mean it exactly as much as he does.

I drop the shutter and turn the key, and stand a moment in the lane with the market noise washing past. Tomorrow I walk into our room and tell Riku I found Kaito, and met him twice, and never said a word. How do I make that sound like anything except what it is?
