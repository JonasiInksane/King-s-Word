# Chapter 19 — The Break-In

The room above the tool sharpener smells like oil and dry rice, and I have slept in it long enough that the ceiling crack looks like a river I already know.

Mira is at the low table with yesterday's pay counted into three piles — food, rent, the pile she doesn't explain. I wash my face in the basin by the window. Outside, the bath house is already breathing steam into the alley. Somewhere a tram takes the corner too hard. Takamatsu at first light sounds like work starting, not like a town waking up scared, and that is the whole point of being here.

"You're early," Mira says.

"Foreman wants the east run before the market opens — rice and oil for the hill kitchens." I dry my hands on my shirt. "He said if I'm late he takes the next boy."

"Then don't be late." She looks up. The fever lie has become ordinary in her mouth — she uses it on clerks without blinking, and I have said *spring fever, never came back right* so many times that my scraped voice almost believes it. "Remember — no talking under anything. Not for a shortcut, not for a crate, and not because you're angry."

"I know."

"Knowing and doing are different jobs." She says it the way she has said it every week since the yards, and I take it the way I take the yen she pushes across the table. "Get water after. If we're both free, Minato — different hour than last time. The counter was crowded."

"Trouble comes in shifts," I say, because she said it once and it stuck.

She almost smiles. It still doesn't reach her eyes. "Go."

---

The east warehouse that feeds the hill kitchens sits behind a wire fence with a civilian guard on a folding chair and a stamp box he never opens before noon. I have unloaded here six times. The foreman likes that I don't drop crates and don't ask questions. Today the load is rice — sacks that fight your spine — and cooking oil in tins that sweat when the sun finds them.

We are short two hands. The foreman swears at the registration sheets and at boys who vanished rather than put their names on a ward form. He points at me and a thin man from the docks and says, "You two stay after the unload. There's an early pull for the hill. Cash on top if it leaves before the market crowds the gate."

The thin man looks at the guard, then at the fence, then at his own boots. "What's early?"

"Before the second stamp," the foreman says. "Kitchen clerk on the hill wants the rice in before the officers eat. You load, you keep quiet, and you get paid twice."

I should say no. Twice pay is the kind of number that comes with a second set of eyes. Mira's rule sits in my throat like a stone I have learned to swallow. Then I think of the third pile on her table — the one she doesn't explain — and of how thin the food pile was last week, and I nod.

The thin man nods too, slower.

We unload the morning truck into the warehouse bay — dust, twine, the smell of dry grain that never quite leaves your nose. The guard on the chair watches us the way men watch work they aren't paid to do, bored until something goes wrong. His rifle leans against the fence post within reach: private contract for the hill, not gray-green.

When the bay is stacked, the foreman checks his watch and swears again. "Clerk's early. Gate stays shut until the second stamp unless someone opens it from inside. You—" he jabs a finger at me "—go tell the chair to open for a kitchen pull. He knows the stamp. Make him useful."

The thin man stays with the cart. I walk to the guard.

Up close he is older than he looked from the bay — gray at the temples, eyes already tired of the day. He takes in my dust and my voice before I finish the first word.

"Kitchen pull," I say, ordinary wage-boy length. "Foreman says open for the hill — rice and oil, early."

"Stamp isn't till later." He doesn't reach for the latch. "Nobody opens early without a chit from the hill desk."

"Foreman said you know the stamp."

"Foreman says a lot of things that aren't paper." He tips his chin at the closed gate. "Come back when the clerk does. Or bring a chit."

Behind me the thin man shifts his weight on the cart. The foreman is already walking toward us with the look of a man about to make this my problem. The rice is stacked, the oil is sweating, and the hill wants feeding. The command rises the way it always does when a door is the only thing between work and a fight — the same shape I used at the well, the same shape I swore I wouldn't use in this city. Mira's rule sits useless against it.

The foreman shouts my name like a curse.

I look at the guard's eyes. I keep my voice low, under the fence rattle, under the market starting two streets over.

"Open the gate. You never saw us. You never saw faces."

It lands.

His hand finds the latch without asking his face first. The gate swings. His eyes go empty the way the well man's did — a held breath of nothing — and then he is staring past us at the street as if the street asked him a question he forgot. Blood finds my upper lip. Copper fills my mouth for a breath and then eases. I wipe it on my wrist and don't look at the thin man.

"Move," I tell him, normal voice, nothing under it.

We push the cart through. The guard sits back down on his chair with the gate still open and puts his face in his hands like a man who has just remembered he was supposed to be ashamed of something and can't find the something.

The hill road climbs through pine with cook-smoke already on the air. We deliver to a side door where a kitchen clerk counts sacks without looking at our faces, pays the foreman's envelope into my hand, and shuts the door on us like we were never there.

On the walk back down the thin man says, quietly, "What did you say to him?"

"Nothing he won't forget."

He doesn't ask again. That is the other skill this city taught me: people who smell a wrong thing often decide they prefer not to own it.

---

By midday I am back in the yards with ordinary crates and an ordinary mouth. The foreman pays the double and doesn't ask how the gate opened. Gossip is already moving faster than carts — a dock boy tells a noodle stall that the east warehouse guard opened for thieves and then cried on the step. Someone else says heat. Someone else says shame. Nobody says the word that would make it true.

I keep stacking.

At the water tower after the whistle Mira is waiting with the sack and a look I have learned to read as *we need to talk somewhere without ears*.

"You used it," she says. Not a question.

"The gate wouldn't open, and the hill pull was short-handed after registration took the rest." I spit into the dust. "I kept it short."

"Short still leaves a mark." She starts walking before I can argue, cutting us through the arcade where neon is already on and the crowd is thick enough to be a wall. "We don't go back to that warehouse, and we don't talk about it at Minato. We eat, we pay rent, and we look like boys who haul rice."

"And the third pile?"

She glances at me. "There's someone I need you to meet. Not today — tonight, late. A contact. They have a room behind the rail sheds and they don't like new faces, so you keep your mouth the wage length and you let me talk."

"What kind of contact?"

"The kind that knew we were coming before I finished asking for a roof in this city." She says it flat, like logistics. "I didn't tell you because you would have asked questions I couldn't answer in a street. Tonight you get the short version. Tomorrow we decide if we use them."

"Use them for what?"

"For not dying when the gray-greens stop counting hands and start counting mouths." She tips her chin at a side street. "Minato first, different hour — bowls, then we go."

I follow her. My lip has stopped bleeding. The gate is still open in my head, and the guard's empty eyes, and the part of me that is glad it worked — the part I have been trying not to name since the buyer on the pier — sits down next to the rule I broke and does not apologize.

---

Minato at this hour is thinner than the Thursday crowds Mira described from other boys' talk. Machi sets two bowls down before we have claimed stools, prices me in one pass, and says nothing about where we sleep. An old man's stool at the end is empty. Someone has pushed it in. Machi's eyes flick there once and away, and I know better than to ask a bar mama about absences.

"Trouble eats standing up," she says, when I hesitate. "Sit."

We sit. The broth is hot. Mira eats like fuel. I listen to the room — dock talk, registration curses, a runner at the far end starting a story about a warehouse gate and a crying guard. Mira's knee presses mine once. I keep my eyes on my bowl.

Outside, after, the street smells like rain that hasn't decided to fall. Mira turns us toward the rail sheds.

"If this person asks what you can do," she says, "you say nothing. If they already know, you still say nothing. I do the talking. Clear?"

"Clear."

"And Kaito —" She stops under the elevated line while a tram shakes the street. "The gate was a mistake we survive by not repeating. The contact isn't a reason to get louder. They're a reason to get harder to find."

"Then why meet them?"

"Because harder to find costs favors, and favors need a door that opens both ways." She starts walking again. "Come on. They're waiting."

We take the alley behind the bath house toward the rail sheds. Behind us the east-side story is already growing legs. Ahead, Mira's contact is waiting in a room I have never entered, and I keep my mouth empty on the walk because the gate already proved what happens when I don't.
