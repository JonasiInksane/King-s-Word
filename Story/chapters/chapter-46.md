# Chapter 46 — Pond Night

The fifteenth shows up faster than it has any right to, the way every deadline in this city seems to once you've agreed to enjoy the one at the end of it.

We spend the morning acting like warehouse hands who have the day off — Riku arguing with the landlady about whether festival nights count against rent credit, Mei washing her hair in the shared bath like she is preparing for an inspection, Ōhashi deciding the notebook is coming whether anyone likes it or not. I buy a cheap plain yukata from a stall two lanes down, cotton the color of weak tea, because showing up in the same shirt I've worn all week would look like I hadn't decided to actually come. Riku finds a plain dark jacket instead of his work coat, which on him counts as festival dress. Mei borrows something from a neighbor girl on the second floor and pushes the sleeves up out of habit even though there's nothing left to carry.

Last night, on the stair outside Endo's, I asked where we should meet. Kaito rubbed the back of his neck like he still expected someone to cancel.

"The bridge by the reed pond," he said. "Dusk. If you're late I'm eating without you."

"Don't eat without us," I said. "Riku will start a fight with the corn cart."

"Then don't be late."

We aren't. For a few minutes it's just the four of us and the crowd thickening around the lanterns, and nobody quite knows how to stand after last night — not angry anymore, just careful, like the wrong joke might put the bruise back.

Then Kaito comes across the bridge with Mira and Suzumori.

He's in dark blue cotton that doesn't try to be anything. Mira is in pale green that isn't new but has clearly been kept somewhere careful for exactly this kind of night. Suzumori has traded her stick for a shorter cane and a clean gray wrap she wouldn't let anyone else pay for. For a second nobody quite knows how to stand near each other either. Then Riku opens his mouth.

"You clean up like somebody who's never held a crate," he tells Kaito, by way of a greeting that isn't quite a greeting.

"You clean up like somebody who lost a fight with an iron," Kaito says back, and something that's been tight in Riku's face since the storeroom finally lets go, half an inch.

The bridge crowd thickens as we cross into the ward proper — lanterns strung two and three deep over every lane, paper Kaito himself might have stacked at Endo's counter a week ago, red and white and pale green swinging in a wind that smells like fry oil and canal water and somebody's incense stick burning too close to a stall of dried squid. A man with a drum the size of a barrel is warming up two streets over, and every few seconds the crowd answers him with a laugh that has nothing to do with the joke, just the noise of people glad to be standing where they're standing.

"I forgot cities did this," Mei says, mostly to herself, watching a pair of kids race paper boats down the canal's edge with actual coins riding on the outcome. "Just — this. For no reason except the date on a calendar."

"There's a reason," Suzumori says. "It's the first one since the checkpoints went up on the coast road. Half this crowd lost somebody east. The rest came anyway." She doesn't say it like a lecture. She says it like a woman confirming a fact she already priced in before she agreed to come.

We eat standing at a cart selling grilled corn brushed with something sweet and a little burnt, because Kaito insists, because apparently there's a version of him that gets loud and cheerful about corn on a stick when nobody's life is currently on fire, and it's strange and good to watch. Ōhashi buys a paper cone of roasted chestnuts and gives half of it to Mei without being asked, the way he's been doing small unannounced things for her since Shizuhara, and she takes it without comment, which from Mei is the whole thank-you.

"You are going to get grease on that notebook," Riku tells him.

"I have survived worse threats to this notebook than a chestnut," Ōhashi says, and licks his thumb clean before turning the page anyway, just in case.

A ring-toss stall catches Riku's eye two stalls further down — bottles lined up on a warped board, a hand-painted sign promising a paper fan for three rings and a whole candied apple for five — and he plants himself in front of it before anyone can talk him out of it.

"I used to be good at this," he announces, to nobody who asked.

"You used to be good at everything, according to you," Mei says.

He isn't good at it. His first ring skips off the nearest bottle and bounces into the crowd, his second goes wide enough that the stall owner has to duck, and by the third Kaito's laughing hard enough that he has to lean on the counter, which draws a look from the owner that says he'd rather Kaito not lean on anything he hasn't paid for.

"Let the expert try," Kaito says, and pays for three rings of his own before Riku's finished protesting that there's no such thing as an expert at this game. He lands one clean, misses the second wide, and the third catches a bottle dead center and knocks it clean off the board, which gets him nothing but a shrug from the stall owner and a groan from the rest of us, because apparently knocking the bottle over doesn't count if the ring doesn't stay on.

"That's a rigged game," Kaito says, delighted anyway.

"Every game at every festival has been rigged since before either of us was born," Suzumori says, not even looking up from the folding stool she's found herself. "The rigging is the point. You are meant to lose cheerfully and buy a fan on your way out regardless."

Mei steps up next, pays for three rings without any speech about it, and lands two out of three clean around the bottle necks, calm as she counts a junction board, and holds her hand out to the stall owner for the promised fan like she's collecting a debt he tried to pretend he didn't owe.

"How," Riku says, betrayed.

"I watch things fall for a living," Mei says, already fanning herself with her prize. "Turns out that's useful for more than counting kids."

She gives the fan to Suzumori without being asked, the same unannounced way Ōhashi gave her the chestnuts, and Suzumori accepts it with a small nod that's as close to delighted as I've seen her get, snapping it open and fanning herself with the exaggerated dignity of a woman who has decided, tonight, to let herself be spoiled a little.

---

We find a spot on the grass bank above the reed pond an hour before the fireworks are due, because Suzumori refuses to stand for an hour on principle and everyone else is happy to sit rather than argue with her about it. The water's gone gold and pink with the last of the light, thick with paper lanterns already floating out from the near bank, little unsteady flames bobbing between the reeds like a low constellation that drifts instead of holding still.

Kaito lies back with his hands behind his head and looks, for one unguarded stretch, exactly like a boy who used to argue with a teacher over a seating chart and nothing worse than that.

"I keep waiting for someone to tell us to move," he says.

"Nobody's going to tell us to move," I say. "That's the whole point of tonight."

"I know that's the point. Knowing it and believing it in my legs are two different things." He turns his head toward me without lifting it off the grass. "You look like an actual person in that yukata. It's unsettling."

"You look like you're about to fall asleep in public."

"I might. Mira, wake me up if the sky does anything before I do."

"You'll wake up on your own," Mira says, not looking up from the lantern she's writing something small on with a borrowed brush, the ink still wet at the tip. "You always do the second something's about to matter."

Riku and Mei have gotten into an argument about whether the drummer two streets over is any good, which somehow turns into Riku doing an impression of the man's face mid-solo, which somehow gets Mei laughing hard enough that she has to put the chestnuts down before she drops them. It isn't a big laugh. It's short, and a little surprised at itself, and gone almost as soon as it lands — but I watch Ōhashi watching her do it, and I watch him decide not to say anything about it, which is its own kind of gift.

Suzumori sends her own lantern out onto the water without ceremony, just leans forward on her cane and lets the current take it, watching it join the little drifting field of light without a word about what she wrote on it, if she wrote anything at all. Mira sends hers after. Kaito doesn't buy one, and nobody makes him explain why, and I don't ask either, because some silences are a kindness and this is one of them.

For a while nobody says anything that needs saying. The drum two streets over settles into something slower. A vendor somewhere is arguing happily with a customer about the price of a candied plum. Somebody's little sister runs past us shrieking about a lost shoe, and her father catches her by the collar without breaking stride, laughing the whole time, and it's such an ordinary thing to see — a kid, a shoe, a laughing father — that my chest does something complicated it hasn't done in weeks.

"This is nice," Mei says, quiet, mostly to the water. "I am glad we decided to do this."

Down by the pond's edge, someone shouts that the fireworks crew is testing the first fuse, and half the crowd on the bank turns to look. For one more breath it's just a warm evening and seven people who finally got to sit still together without anybody hurting.

The first firework cracks open white above the reeds, and the whole bank tips its face up at once, waiting on the next one.
