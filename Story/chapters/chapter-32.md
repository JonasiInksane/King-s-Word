# Chapter 32 — Evening Review

The medical stair dumps us onto a corridor that smells like ozone and boiled disinfectant.

Mei takes the left turn without asking if we are ready. Her tablet is dark now — no light to give us away — and she counts doors under her breath the way she counted board numbers in the junction. Mira stays half a step behind my shoulder, listening for boots. Below us, through a slit window that looks down into the service court, the third group of armband kids is already on the canvas truck.

Habu's voice follows us through every speaker we pass.

"Thank you. Stay calm. Leave personal items with the clerk. You will be home sooner than you think."

Home. Still that word.

We hit a locked cross-door between medical and the annex approach. Mei swears once, ordinary, and digs a key card from inside her shirt that does not belong to her.

"War-sheet runners get these for recounts," she says. "Sometimes they forget to take them back." The lock clicks. "Stay low. The purge gallery has a glass strip over the chamber. If you came to look, that is where you look."

"Purge," Mira says.

Mei does not soften it. "That is what the maintenance board calls it. Habu calls it evening review."

---

The gallery is a narrow walk above a white room.

Through the glass: a concrete floor with drain channels cut in neat lines, still damp, as if it was just hosed. Vents along the far wall, metal mouths the size of dinner plates. A row of hooks for jackets that nobody is using. The floor is empty. Whatever happened to the first two truckloads, someone already took them out of sight — a low side hatch in the far wall stands open on darkness, and a hose is coiled neat beside it like a kitchen tool.

Ten kids from the court truck are walked in through the bay door — armbands still on, cups already gone. A clerk with a tablet stands at a side door. Two gray-greens frame the vents. Nobody is shouting. Nobody is aiming a rifle at a forehead. It looks like a waiting room until Habu's soft voice comes through a speaker inside the chamber itself.

"Inventory group three. Face the vents, please. This will only take a moment. Breathe normally. You have been very good tonight."

The kids face the vents. One girl reaches for the boy beside her and takes his hand. A guard nods at that like it is the right answer on a test.

Then the vents open.

Not smoke like a fire. A pale rush that makes the air in the chamber go thick and wrong. The girl with the handhold coughs first. The boy tries to pull his armband up over his mouth. Someone laughs — short, confused — and then the laugh turns into choking. They go down in a messy line, knees first, then hands, then faces toward the drains. The clerk marks something on the tablet without looking up. One gray-green watches the vents. The other watches the door.

It takes less time than a school drill.

When the vents cut off, two men in gray coveralls come through the side hatch with a flat cart. They work without hurry — ankles, wrists, stack, roll — and the bodies go into the dark hatch one after another. A third man follows with the hose and washes the floor until the concrete shines again. The hatch closes. The chamber looks like a waiting room once more.

The clerk speaks into a wall phone, still mild. "Group three clear. Send four."

I hit the glass with both palms before Mira can catch my wrists.

"Kaito—"

"Open it," I say to Mei. "Get me into that room."

"There is no door from the gallery into the chamber," Mei says. Her voice is flat because flat is what keeps her standing. "The access is through the bay. We have to go back down and around. If you break this glass you get a fall onto concrete and a rifle from below, and they still finish group four."

Below us the bay door rolls. Ten more armbands walk into the clean white room. Habu thanks them for their patience. None of them look at the floor like it means anything. A guard straightens a girl's armband so it sits neat.

"Face the vents, please," Habu says. "You're almost done."

The vents open again.

I run.

---

The way back down is wrong on purpose — medical cross-corridor, then a stair that should hit the bay and instead dumps into a hold lobby full of gray-greens checking rifles. Mira hauls me into a supply alcove before the first one turns. Mei's card opens a side hatch into a service crawl that smells like dust and mouse shit. We move on hands and knees. Through grated panels I see another ten walk into a clean white room. I see them fall. I see the cart men and the hose before the next line is called. Something in my chest is climbing so hard my teeth ache with it. Mira's hand finds the back of my neck and pins my head off the grate.

"Don't," she breathes.

"They're right there."

"And you're up here. Mouth shut. Crawl."

I crawl. My hands leave sweat on the concrete. Every time Habu thanks the next group, the heat under my ribs turns a notch sharper.

We drop out behind a linen cart near the annex outer door. Boots between us and the chamber. A clerk with a stamp. Habu still thanking. Mei points left — fence corridor, longer loop to purge access.

"Ditch," Mira says. "Now. We have seen enough."

"I'm not going to the ditch."

"Kaito—"

"Left."

Mei takes left without looking at either of us. Mira swears and follows.

Halfway down the fence corridor a pair comes around the corner. Mira pulls me into a doorway. Too late for clean. The nearer gray-green sees my face and lifts his rifle.

The sentence is already in my mouth — *drop it, face the wall* — copper taste, easy as the bay clerk. Mira's eyes cut to my lips. For one ugly second I want the weight more than the room. I want these two on the floor the way the kids are on the drains. That want is new, and it is loud, and it is not about doors anymore.

I do not speak. Not because she asked. Because speaking here still does not put me in that chamber.

Mei flashes the war-sheet card and a lie about east recount that is almost good enough. The older one buys it. The younger one does not like my face. He tips the rifle.

Mira hits him with the fire extinguisher from the wall bracket. Hard. He goes down. The older one starts to shout. Mei kicks the rifle aside and we run before the shout finishes becoming a building-wide problem.

Behind us Habu says, soft as ever, "Group seven, thank you. Face the vents."

"Seven," I say, and my voice comes out wrong — too thin, too hot. "They're on seven and we are still in a hallway."

"Keep running," Mira says.

We make the fence-side loop. Through a high window I get one more slice of the white room — another ten on their knees, a guard straightening a boy's armband so it sits neat while the vents breathe. The boy looks up as if he heard something. Then he doesn't.

I hit the glass with my shoulder. Mira hauls me off it before the pane decides which of us it likes less.

"Stop it."

"Get out of my way."

"You break that and every rifle on this floor finds you before you find a door."

"Then find me a door."

---

We stop in a dead stairwell because Mira puts her back to the exit and will not move.

Mei listens at the door. Through the concrete I can still hear the vents, or I am inventing them. My hands are fists. I do not remember making them fists.

"I've had enough," I say.

Not quiet this time. Not aimed at anyone with weight under it — not yet — but loud enough that Mira's face goes hard.

"You've had enough?" She steps in close, voice low and ugly. "I have been dragging you off glass and grates while they thank kids into a hose room. Do not stand there and tell me you are the only one who is done."

"Then stop blocking the door."

"So you can walk in and die with them? So I can watch you bleed out your mouth until there is nothing left to drag?" Her hand hits the wall beside my head hard enough that Mei flinches. "I brought you here for one girl. You turned it into a war with a soft speaker. I am angry too, Kaito. I am not stupid enough to call that a plan."

"I don't need a plan. I need into that room."

"You need to shut up and think for one second—"

"No." I push past her toward the door. Blood is on my lip from biting it. "Give me the card," I tell Mei. "Take me into that room the short way. I am done looking through glass."

Mei holds my eyes for a second. Whatever she sees there, she does not argue with it.

"We go through the loading bay and the clerk door," she says. "If Habu is still staging groups, we walk into rifles."

"Good," I say.

Mira laughs once, no humor in it. Then she swears and gets out of the doorway.

---

The loading bay smells like diesel and cold coffee. The canvas truck is gone. The dock is empty except for a tipped bin of cups and a gray-green leaning on the stamp desk with a cigarette, then looking at his radio like the hard part of the night is already over. Mei's card gets us past him before he decides we matter. The purge door stands open a hand's width for the cleanup crew. The cart men have not come yet.

I push through.

Bodies in armbands cover the concrete in rows that started neat and ended where people crawled. Some still have their bands straight. Some do not. The side hatch stands open on the dark bay beyond, and through it I can see the earlier stacks the hose never had to explain to the next group. Habu is not thanking anyone anymore. A wall speaker clicks once and says, mild as a dinner bell:

"A and B evening review complete. Inventory zero recorded. C block remains on hold. East remains sealed. Thank you for your cooperation."

I stand in the doorway with Mira's breath at my back and Mei's tablet hanging dead in her hand, and there is nobody left in the room to save. The kids who waved at C about curry are either on this floor or already through that hatch.

Mira says my name like a rope.

I do not take it.

Mei's voice breaks on the first try. She gets it out on the second.

"We were too late," she says. "I thought if we cut through medical—" She stops. Looks at the armbands. "Thursday curry. I knew their bunk numbers."

The smoking gray-green in the bay laughs at something on his radio. Somewhere upstairs, Habu asks a clerk for tea.

I walk into the white room far enough to see that none of them are breathing. A girl still has her fingers hooked in a boy's sleeve. The drains make a soft sound like a sink left running. Mira says my name again — not a rope this time, a warning — and then she stops, because whatever she sees on my face is not something she can pull me back from.

My jaw locks. My eyes burn, not from smoke — from holding still on the bodies and refusing to look away. Mei takes a step back without meaning to. Mira's mouth goes flat the way it does when a road turns into a fight. I do not wipe the blood off my lip. I do not make my face kinder for either of them.

"I watched you walk in," I tell them, quiet, useless, too late. "I watched him thank you. I sat on a grate and let distance win. I let Mira tell me not to open my mouth. I let the next door be soon enough." My throat burns. "It was not soon enough. You are on the floor and he is asking for tea like the night went well."

I turn toward the bay. Toward the wall speaker. Toward the soft voice upstairs that still thinks this building belongs to kindness.

Mira sees it coming. "Kaito—don't—"

I do not keep it in.

"HABU!" The shout hits the white room hard enough that the smoker in the bay drops his cigarette. "I heard you thank them! Come down here and look at what your evening review did!"

Silence for half a second. Then Habu's voice on the speaker — not soft anymore.

"There are intruders in the review chamber. Send two pairs to the bay and the purge door. Hold them there. Do not let them get out."

Boots answer from the dock side. The smoker's radio crackles alive. Mira swears and pulls Mei off the doorframe. We are not leaving through a clean road anymore.
