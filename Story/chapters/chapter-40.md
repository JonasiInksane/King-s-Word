# Chapter 40 — Cash Room

The lanterns over the canal are still burning when we go looking for a roof, but the streets have thinned out enough that our footsteps sound too loud on the stone.

"Haruki first," I say again, because saying it once did not make it feel finished. "So we start asking tomorrow. Tonight we just need a door that locks."

"Tonight we need a door that does not ask for papers," Mira says. "There is a difference and it matters more here than it did in Kuroishi."

Suzumori is limping. Not badly, but enough that she puts a hand on my shoulder every third step without asking, and I let her, because arguing about it would take longer than just being a post for a minute. We follow the canal until the lanterns start hanging over shopfronts instead of water, and Mira reads the hand-painted signs the way she read the checkpoint sergeant back on the coast — not for what they say, for what they don't.

"That one," she says, pointing at a narrow building with a faded blue door and a card in the window that just says ROOMS, no rates, no rules. "Places that post their rules want you filling out a form to go with them."

The woman who answers the door is younger than I expected, maybe thirty, with her hair tied back hard enough to pull at her eyebrows and a ledger already open on the counter like she was doing sums before we knocked. She looks at the three of us — me still wearing yesterday's blood on my collar in the shape of a stain I gave up trying to explain — and does not flinch, does not soften, just taps her pencil twice against the page.

"Two rooms or one," she says. "Third floor, water down the hall, no cooking in the rooms. Cash tonight, cash every morning after. I do not run a tab and I do not remember faces kindly if you skip out owing me."

"One room," Mira says, before I can open my mouth about money. "How much."

The landlady names a number. Mira doesn't argue it down — she just counts coins onto the counter from the fold she has been carrying since Takamatsu, slow enough that the landlady watches her hands instead of our faces, which I think is the point.

"Names," the landlady says, pencil ready.

"Aoki," Mira says. Not the name any of us were born with. "The old woman is my grandmother. The boy is my cousin."

The landlady looks at Suzumori, who does not correct the word *old*, and then at me, who apparently reads as cousin material with a split lip and dried blood on my jaw. She writes something in the ledger that is probably not even close to Aoki.

"Third floor," she says again. "If you fight, you're out before I ask why. If soldiers come asking, I never saw you. That part is not kindness. That part is bad for my rent roll." She slides a single brass key across the counter. "Bath's cold after nine. Deal with it."

We climb three flights of stairs that smell like somebody's dinner from two nights ago. The room is smaller than the one in Shizuhara's C-wing bunks but it has a door that shuts and a window that looks out over a rooftop instead of a fence, and for a second that is the whole difference in the world.

Suzumori sits on the nearest mattress and starts working her boot off with both hands.

"You are staring at the wallpaper like it insulted you," she tells me.

"I'm not staring at anything." I am. It's just a wall. It doesn't have a fence in it or a truck driving away from it, and I keep checking that fact like it might change if I look too long.

Mira sets the fold down on the windowsill, counts what's left with her thumb, and doesn't share the number out loud, which tells me it isn't a good one.

---

We go back out because Suzumori's stomach makes a sound loud enough that Mira laughs before she can stop herself — a short, surprised bark of a laugh, gone as fast as it came, like she forgot she still had that in her.

The night market is smaller than the one at the bridge but louder, packed into an alley between two canal walls with strings of paper lanterns overhead and a smell of frying batter that makes my mouth hurt from wanting it. We buy noodles from a cart because it's cheap and fast, and a bar of gray soap from a stall selling nothing but soap and string, and a jug we fill twice at a public tap before Mira decides two jugs is enough water to carry up three flights.

"We need more than soap," Suzumori says, counting the coins Mira has left in her palm without being asked to. "Coats that hide blood stains. Something for my ankle that is not spit and hope."

"We need rent for tomorrow more than we need a new coat," Mira says. "One thing at a time."

"You say that like it's wisdom and not just running out of money."

"It's both," Mira says. "Doesn't make it wrong."

Suzumori makes the dry sound in her throat that isn't quite a laugh. I watch the alley instead of the argument, because watching the alley is a thing my body still knows how to do even when the rest of me feels like a held breath. There's a man near the tea stand in a coat too heavy for the weather, and my shoulders go up before my brain catches why — dark wool, collar turned, the shape of a coat I have spent nights hating without ever seeing the whole face under it.

He turns to pay for his tea. He's sixty if he's a day, jowls, a merchant's soft hands. Not him. Not even close to him.

"Kaito." Mira's voice, low, at my elbow. She saw where I was looking. She doesn't say anything about it — no lecture, no *you are doing that thing* — she just puts herself half a step between me and the tea stand until my shoulders come back down on their own.

"It wasn't him," I say.

"I know."

"I know I know it wasn't him too. My legs didn't get the memo before my eyes did."

"That's what legs are like," she says. "Eat your noodles before they're cold."

We eat standing at the cart because there's nowhere to sit, and the broth is hot enough to hurt going down, which is the first thing in days that has hurt in a way I don't mind. Suzumori steals a piece of pork off my bowl without asking and eats it like she's daring me to say something.

"You two fight like you've been doing it for years," I say.

"We have only been in the same room since the quiet floors," Mira says. "That is not nothing. It is also not a lifetime."

"Feels like a lifetime," Suzumori says. "You pack like someone who has been running her whole life. I have been running most of mine. We recognize the walk."

---

Back in the room, Suzumori soaks her ankle in the second jug of water while Mira sits cross-legged on the mattress counting the fold again, like the number might improve if she asks it politely enough.

"How long does that last us," I ask.

"Four days if we're careful. Six if we're mean about food." She folds the cloth smaller. "After that we need work. I am not calling Okina for rent money, and I would rather cut off my own hand than beg a desk."

"So we find work."

"So *you* find work," Suzumori says, not looking up from her ankle. "I am not fast enough to carry crates, and Mira should not be the only one whose face this city learns first. You want to look for your friend without anyone remembering your voice — get a job that keeps your head down and your mouth shut for money instead of for free."

I want to argue that finding Haruki is the whole reason we're here, that every hour spent hauling something for a stranger is an hour the coat's truck gets further away with him in it. I open my mouth to say exactly that, and Mira watches me do it the way she watches everything now — patient, waiting to see if I'll actually say the loud thing or catch it.

I catch it. Mostly because Suzumori's ankle is the size of a fist and neither of them asked me to fix it with my voice, and some small stubborn part of me is glad they didn't ask, because I don't know yet if I'd have said no.

"If I go asking every driver and every boarding house today with my face and my voice, this city writes a report on me by Thursday," I say instead. "Suzumori already said it. Ask with coins and eyes, or get found."

"So?" Mira says.

"So I do it the slow way." Saying it out loud makes it feel more real than deciding it in my head did. "I get work somewhere that pays cash and doesn't ask questions. I ask around while I'm doing it — a name here, a face there, not a manhunt, just ears open. It takes longer. Nobody has a reason to remember my face by the end of the week. And it keeps money coming in so we're not living off your fold until it hits zero."

Suzumori looks at me for a long moment, the kind of look that used to make me want to fill the silence with something clever. I don't, this time.

"That is the first plan out of your mouth since Shizuhara that did not involve breaking something," she says. "Write the date down somewhere. It will not happen twice by accident."

"It's not a promise," I say. "It's just tomorrow's plan."

"Fair," Mira says, and for once she doesn't add anything to it — no steering, no *and after that*, just the one word standing on its own. She blows out the little oil lamp on the sill, and the room goes down to just the canal light coming grey through the window and the sound of the market below finally starting to quiet.

"Tomorrow," Suzumori says from her mattress, "you find work. I find whoever sells crutches in this city. And somebody in this room is going to ask about your friend before the week is out — quietly, like a person and not a fire alarm. Tall boy. Soft face."

"I said I would," I say.

"Say it again in the morning," she says. "A promise made in the dark doesn't count for much. Everyone knows that."

"Fine," I say. "In the morning I go looking for whoever's hiring, and I start there. Same plan, sunlight on it, if that's what it takes for you to believe I mean it."

"I don't trust anyone by moonlight," Suzumori says. "That's not personal. Go to sleep before you say something you'll have to keep."
