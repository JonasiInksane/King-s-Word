# Chapter 11 — Inland

The salt smell is gone by morning.

The ridge has given us dust instead — dry hills, scrub, a herring road that used to mean carts and now means two people walking west with one sack between them. Mira sets the pace — not hard, not soft, the pace of someone who has already decided how far today goes and is waiting for my feet to catch up.

I don't talk. She doesn't make me. Twice she hands me water without asking, and twice I take it, and both times my throat still feels like something heavy went down it the night before and never finished landing. Jun is not a thought I can hold all at once. He arrives in pieces — the radish fans, the thumb on the splice, the half-turn toward his mother — and I keep walking through them the way she told me to.

By noon the road tips into a long main street with a blackened shrine gate at one end and a bus depot at the other — hot asphalt, croquette oil, the copy-paper smell of an open office door. Kuroishi. The town has FDC the way a dog has fleas: pairs at the depot, a clipboard man under a shade cloth, nobody running, nobody smiling much.

"Not the depot," Mira says, quiet. "Not yet. We need a roof first."

"You know somebody?"

"I know a shop that used to take coast people overnight when the herring road still meant something." She glances at me once. "If she's still there, she won't ask questions. If she asks questions, we leave."

---

Orii's dried-goods sits halfway down the street between a shuttered tool shop and a place selling secondhand boots — a bell on the door, dust in the light, shelves of rice, tea, string, and soap, the boring things that keep a town standing.

The woman behind the counter does not look up for the bell — middle-aged, hair pinned flat, wearing an apron the color of old paper. She finishes counting a stack of tea boxes, sets the last one straight, and only then lifts her eyes.

"We're closed for lodging," she says.

"We're not lodging," Mira says. "We're cousins from the coast. Looking for evening work, and a floor if you still have the upstairs."

Orii's gaze goes over Mira, then over me — scraped voice, dust to the knees, a boy who looks like he walked out of something and hasn't decided what to call it. She does not ask which coast. She does not ask why.

She points upstairs without smiling.

"One night. Maybe two. You haul stock in the morning. You don't talk about the war in my shop. I don't stock it."

"Understood," Mira says.

"Payment's the hauling. There's a mat and a washbasin. Don't leave the water running." She looks at me again, longer. "And keep your mouth quiet if anyone with a clipboard comes in. This town has ears."

I almost laugh. It comes out wrong. She has already turned back to her tea boxes.

---

The room above the shop is one mat, one window onto the alley, a washbasin with a crack down the side. Mira unpacks like she has done this before — water, a cloth, a small tin of rice she bought at the counter without me noticing the price. I sit on the mat with my back to the wall and listen to the street — diesel, a radio somewhere playing a cheerful jingle over a war bulletin. Nobody screams. Nobody here knows Jun's name.

"Eat," Mira says.

"Not hungry."

"I didn't ask if you were hungry." She puts the rice in my hand. "You walk better with something in you. That's not kindness. That's logistics."

I eat. It tastes like nothing. It stays down, which is the whole job.

She watches me finish and doesn't pretend she isn't measuring whether I can walk tomorrow.

"You're going to keep seeing him," she says. Not gentle. Accurate. "In every quiet room. That's what it does. You don't have to talk about it with me. You just have to keep your feet moving when it hits."

"You packed the sack before the trucks came."

"Yes."

"How long before?"

A small pause. She folds the cloth once. "Long enough. The gray suit had been circling the harbor office for a week. My aunt's drivers talk and I listen." She meets my eyes. "I didn't know it would be Jun. If that matters."

It matters. It also doesn't change anything. I nod once, because that is what I have left that isn't a sentence with weight on it.

---

In the evening Mira goes down to ask Orii about the bus toward Ishibashi. I go out the back for air and find the alley opens onto a square with a stone well at the center — low wall, iron crank, a line of travelers filling bottles before the next walk. A man in a gray-green shirt with no rank tabs stands beside it with a folding stool and a notebook, checking bags as people come up. It isn't a full checkpoint, just the town's clipboard habit without the trucks.

I should go back upstairs, but I don't. My bottle is empty, and my throat is still wrong, and the well is right there.

I get in the short line behind an old woman with a market bag and a boy my age with a satchel. The gray-green man opens the satchel, thumbs through it, stamps a slip, and waves the boy on like he has done this a hundred times today and expects nothing from the hundred-and-first.

Then it's my turn.

"Bag," he says.

I hold it out. Empty. He looks at the empty and then at my face, and something in his eyes shifts — not recognition, interest. The interest of a man who has been told to watch for coast dust and scraped voices and boys who arrived without a bus.

"Where from?"

"Nagihama road."

"Papers."

I don't have papers. Mira is downstairs. Orii's ceiling is not a stamp.

"I lost them on the ridge," I say.

He doesn't buy it. His hand goes to the radio on his belt — not lifting it yet, just resting there — and he says, mild as a clerk, "You'll wait by the shade cloth. Secondary. Routine."

Same word they used on Jun at the pier. Same waiting to the side until somebody with a better clipboard decides.

Something rises in me before I have chosen anything. I am so tired of being parked.

Under the well's crank noise, aimed at the man with the notebook:

"Look the other way. You never saw me."

The copper comes up hard. My nose prickles. The man's eyes go loose for half a second — radio hunting stations — and then he turns his head toward the depot, exactly as told, and his hand leaves the radio, and I walk past the well with my empty bottle and my bleeding lip and nobody stops me.

Behind me I hear him say to the next person, a little confused, "Bag. Come on, I haven't got all evening."

I don't look back. I fill the bottle from the public tap on the alley wall instead, hands shaking, and go upstairs the long way so Mira won't see the blood until I've wiped it.

---

She sees it anyway. Cloth out, thumb under my chin, the same move as the pier.

"Who," she says. Not *what*. She already knows what.

"The well. He was checking bags, and he wanted me for secondary."

"And you told him to look away."

"I told him he never saw me."

She holds the cloth there a second longer than the blood needs. Her face stays level — reading weather that hasn't arrived yet.

"That leaves a mark," she says. "Not on paper. On people. Someone at that well is going to tell a story about a boy whose mouth made a clipboard man forget his job." She lowers the cloth. "We leave at first light for Ishibashi. Before the story grows legs."

"Orii said one night. Maybe two."

"One night. Then we go." Mira packs the sack while she talks — already deciding for both of us. "You used it in a town that still has buses. That's different from a shed. Different from a shrine yard with six hundred witnesses who will swear they saw smoke. Here it's quiet enough to remember."

I sit on the mat and try to feel sorry. The sorry is there. Under it, smaller, is the same lift I felt on the pier when the soldier's eyes went loose — a hand finding a grip — and I hate that it is still there after Jun, after the soldier under his own jaw, after Haru's open mouth in the gateway.

Mira ties the sack shut.

"Sleep," she says. "Tomorrow we walk again. And Kaito — next time a clipboard asks for paper, you let me talk first. Your mouth is what they're hunting. Mine is just a girl's."

She says it like logistics. I nod like logistics. Outside the window the well square is still busy — bottles, crank, somebody laughing at a joke I can't hear. I lie down and make myself stop listening to it.
