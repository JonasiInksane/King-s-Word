# Chapter 15 — East Yards

The line moves by shouting.

A man with a clipboard and no gray-green on him walks the fence, pointing at backs and shoulders. "You. You. Not you — too thin. You." When he gets to me he looks at my hands, then at my face, then at my hands again.

"Name."

"Kurosawa."

"From."

"West," I say, the same nothing I gave the truck man in Ishibashi.

He snorts. "Everybody's from west today. Can you lift?"

"Yes."

"Then stop talking and get on the truck. Afternoon load goes to the warehouse block before dark or I don't pay."

I climb onto the bed with six other boys. Nobody asks for papers. Nobody asks me to say something clever. The truck smells like diesel and wet cardboard, and when it lurches forward my shoulders remember Orii's rice sacks and the Ishibashi crates and decide this is the same work in a louder place.

The boy opposite me offers a cigarette. I shake my head. He shrugs and lights it anyway, cupping the flame against the wind off the road.

"First day?" he asks.

"In this yard."

"Don't argue with the gate man. Don't sit on the crates. Don't tell the foreman your story unless he asks, and he won't." He grins around the cigarette. "And if anyone in a clean shirt wants your name written down, you were never on this truck."

"Understood."

He laughs once. "Coast boy. You all say that." Then the warehouse block rises ahead and the talking stops.

---

The warehouse block is a canyon of corrugated walls and forklifts that don't care whose ribs they miss. We unload until the sun slides behind the pine hills and the floodlights come on yellow. A boy beside me drops a crate and the foreman is on him before the wood stops rocking.

"Pick it up. If it's broken, it comes out of your pay. If you drop another, you walk."

The boy picks it up. Nobody helps him. I keep my hands on my own load and my mouth shut, and when the foreman glances my way I am already moving toward the next stack. That is the whole skill today: be useful, be quiet, be gone when the money is counted.

He pays in cash at a folding table under a tarp, counting yen into each palm like he is measuring rope. When he reaches me he holds the notes a second longer than he held the others.

"Tomorrow same time if you want it," he says. "Don't be late. Don't argue with the gate. Don't bring friends who can't lift."

"Understood."

He pauses at the scrape in my voice. The old heat climbs my throat — the sentence that would make him forget he noticed, the way the well man forgot. I keep my mouth empty. He shrugs and moves to the next boy.

I walk back toward the water tower with the yen folded into my sock. The yards are emptying. Whistle already blown. Mira is not under the tower.

I wait.

Ten minutes. Twenty. A pair of gray-greens walks the chain-link on the far side of the road, not stopping anyone, just being seen. I keep my eyes on the rusted ladder and my hands where empty hands belong. If they come over and ask, I talk like a boy who wants a wage. Ordinary words. Nothing under them. Same rule as the crates. The sentence sits there anyway, unused, and I hate that I can feel the shape of it while I wait for a girl who said one hour.

---

She comes from the alley side, not the depot side, with the sack on her shoulder and a folded paper in her free hand. No grease on her wrists this time. She looks like she has been talking to people who keep rooms, not trays.

"You're late," I say.

"You're paid." She tips her chin at the way I'm standing — weight off one hip, the sock-money tell. "Good. Come on. I found a roof."

"What kind of roof?"

"Cash. No register. Landlady doesn't ask where west is if you pay tonight and don't fight in the hall." She starts walking before I finish reading her face. "It's two wards over. We go around Station Plaza. Cut through the arcade if the neon's on — crowds are cover."

I fall in beside her. "You were gone more than an hour."

"Roofs that don't write names take longer to find than roofs that do." She glances once at the gray-greens on the chain-link and turns us down a side street that smells like fry oil. "Did anyone ask you to talk?"

"The foreman asked if I could lift. I said yes."

"That's the right length." She almost smiles. It still doesn't reach her eyes. "Keep practicing that length."

---

The arcade is wet neon and takoyaki smoke and a hundred people who don't look at us. Mira walks like she has a destination measured in blocks, not guesses. Twice I almost ask how she knows which turn comes next. Twice I don't, because the answer would either be a lie or a truth I am not ready to make her say on a public street.

We cross a canal footbridge. On the far bank a truck has been stopped and a pair is checking the driver's board — not us, not our problem, close enough that my pulse climbs anyway. Mira doesn't change pace. She waits until we are past the next corner before she speaks.

"They do that on the big roads after dark," she says. "Yard boys on foot are boring. Stay boring."

"I'm trying."

"I know."

On the way she buys two bowls of oden from a cart that doesn't ask names, and we eat standing in a doorway while the arcade crowd thins. The broth is hot enough to hurt. I don't complain. My shoulders are already deciding they will hurt worse in the morning.

"How much left after the room?" I ask.

"Enough for breakfast if the yards pay you again. Not enough for a second night unless I earn too." She scrapes the bottom of her bowl. "There's a bar two ridges from the yards that feeds working boys if you look hungry enough. Not tonight. Tonight we sleep where we paid."

"What bar?"

"One that used to sit by a harbor and kept the name when the harbor died." She says it like a fact she overheard, not a place she has been. "Later. If we last."

The room is above a shuttered tool shop that smells like oil and dust, up a stair so narrow my shoulders brush both walls. The landlady is older than Orii and less interested in tea boxes. She takes the night's money, counts it twice, and points at a door with a number scratched into the paint.

"Mat. Basin. No cooking. No guests. Out by ten if you can't pay the next night." She looks at my mouth the way Orii did, then at Mira. "He always this quiet?"

"Fever," Mira says. "In the spring. It never came back right."

The landlady snorts. "Half the boys in the yards claim fever. As long as he doesn't shout in my hall, I don't care what season broke him." She goes back downstairs. We put the sack on the mat. The window looks onto an alley and a strip of sky that is already full of city light.

I sit with my back to the wall. My shoulders burn the useful way. My throat is still scraped. The yen left after the room and the oden is thin enough to count without taking it out of the sock.

"Where did you go?" I ask. "Really."

Mira hangs her jacket on the nail by the door. "Three places that wanted a family register. One that wanted a work stamp. One that took cash and asked how long we'd been walking. I told her two days. She believed two days."

"You've done this before."

"I've watched people do this before." She sits on the edge of the mat and unties her boots. "There's a difference."

There is. There also isn't. I let it sit between us with the sack.

"The foreman said tomorrow same time if I want it," I say.

"Take it. I'll find evening work — trays, laundry, whatever pays before dark." She looks at me properly for the first time since the tower. "And Kaito — if a clipboard leans in while I'm not there, you talk only if they make you, and you keep it short. You do not use your mouth the way you did at the well. Not here. A mark in this city travels farther than a mark in Kuroishi."

"I didn't use it today."

"I know. I'm saying it for tomorrow." She lies down with her back to the alley light. "Yards open early. I'll wake you."

---

I lie awake longer than she does. Below the window somebody laughs over a radio jingle, and a truck downshifts toward Station Plaza. The foreman's pause at my voice is still in my throat, unused. Mira came from the alley side with a roof already folded into paper, and she knew which turns to take without asking anyone on the street.

Before I sleep I put tomorrow's plan where I can reach it: yards at first light, her looking for evening work, cash for a second night if both of us earn. That is enough to hold until morning.
