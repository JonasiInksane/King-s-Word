# Chapter 15 — Hands, Not Papers

Riku shakes me off the cardboard while the arches are still black, and we walk the length of the market with our breath showing, past shuttered stalls and a cat that owns the whole street at this hour. Behind the depot there's a gravel lot with a corrugated shed, two trucks, and a man in a rubber apron sliding blocks of ice down a wooden ramp like he's angry at each one personally.

Riku lifts a hand. "Boss. You're short today."

The driver doesn't stop working. "Who told you that?"

"You did, last week, when you were complaining about it." Riku hooks a thumb at me. "There's two of us. We load here, we ride quiet and we unload in Takamatsu."

The driver straightens up and looks at me the way men look at secondhand tools. He takes in the bruise on my forearm, the road on my boots, and the fact that I'm standing still instead of explaining myself.

"Is he broken?"

"Bruised," Riku says. "He can still lift fine. You pay him the coins. Me you owe a beer, because I'm the one who found you two hands on a short day."

"Then start lifting."

That's the whole interview. No name asked, no paper wanted. I get my hands under a block and my spine understands immediately why the driver is built like a door. The blocks ride in sawdust, stacked in a wooden crib down the middle of the bed, and by the time the sky has gone from black to gray, my shirt is soaked through on a cold morning and I've stopped feeling my fingertips.

The driver checks the load, throws two folded tarps at us, and says, "You ride with the ice. Anybody looks in, you're crew. That means you don't wave, you don't smile, and you look bored. Do you think you can look bored?"

"He's a natural at it," Riku says.

We pull out of Ishibashi as the first light comes over the arches, and I watch the bridge and the dry river and the squid stall's cold grill slide away behind the tailgate. Kaito left this town at dawn yesterday. I'm leaving it at dawn today. A day behind then. Still a day behind now.

---

The bed of an ice truck is its own kind of country. Cold comes off the blocks in a flat, patient wall, the sawdust gets into everything, and the road plays the tailgate chain like an instrument with one note. Riku and I sit wedged between the crib and the side rail with the tarps over our knees, and for the first hour neither of us says anything, because the cold does the talking.

Then Riku digs a strip of dried squid out of his jacket — I don't ask where from — tears it in half, and hands me the bigger piece.

"So," he says. "Takamatsu. Have you been to a real city before?"

"Once, on a school trip. Two days — we saw a castle and a museum, and a boy in my class got lost buying melon bread."

"That's not a city. That's a school trip." He chews, watching the hills go by through the gap in the tarp. "In a city, nobody knows anybody. Nobody in Takamatsu is going to remember your face, your cousin's face, or the face of the man who robbed them last Tuesday. That's the bad news."

"What's the good news?"

"It's the same thing, said the other way." He grins, the crooked tooth showing. "Nobody's going to remember your face either. A boy with no papers can live a long time in a place like that if he's useful and quiet. You're quiet. We'll work on useful."

The truck drops into a lower gear for a climb. Somewhere under us the ice shifts and settles, and meltwater finds a seam in the boards and starts drawing a thin line toward the tailgate.

"Say you catch him," Riku says.

"Who?"

"Your cousin. Say tomorrow you turn a corner and there he is. What happens then? You grab his sleeve, and then what — you walk him home? He left on purpose, from the sound of it. People who leave on purpose don't come back because somebody chased them well."

The dried squid goes to sawdust in my mouth. It's the exact question I have been walking around since Nagihama, the way you walk around a hole in a dark room because you know the floor stops there. Bring him back. The plan has three words and I have never once finished it past the second.

"I have something to say to him," I say. "I started saying it in a gateway and didn't get to finish. That's all I know. When it's finished, I'll find out what happens then."

Riku looks at me for a while. Whatever he's weighing, he doesn't say it. He clicks his tongue once and pulls the tarp higher.

"Long way to ride for a sentence," he says. "Better be a good one."

---

The city announces itself before we can see it — the road widens, gets honest asphalt again, grows painted lines. Traffic thickens around us: carts, two buses, a convoy of gray-green trucks that Riku watches without moving his head. Then the ridge opens and Takamatsu is just there, the way the sea is just there, too big to have edges — harbor cranes, smokestacks, an elevated rail line stitching across the rooftops, streets running back into haze in every direction I look.

At the city edge there are cones, a swing barrier, and soldiers with a lamp even though it's daylight.

The truck slows. I hear the driver's window crank down, hear a bored voice ask for the load sheet. Boots crunch down the length of the bed. The tarp lifts at the tailgate and a young soldier looks in at two boys gray with sawdust sitting against a crib of ice.

"How many hands?" he says, to the driver, not to us.

"Two, plus me. It's on the sheet." The driver's voice comes back flat as his ramp. "And that ice was cut yesterday, so unless the army's buying it melted, we can count faster."

The soldier looks at me a beat longer than I'd like. I look at the ice the way crew looks at ice — bored, put-upon, a boy who has done this run all season and would rather be asleep. The tarp drops. The barrier swings open.

Riku exhales through his nose, not quite a laugh. "Congratulations," he says quietly. "You're an ice man. Nobody looks twice at ice men."

---

North Market takes the truck in whole.

I have never seen anything like it — a roofed street and then another roofed street crossing it, stalls three deep, fish on shaved ice that we are apparently here to replace, a noise that isn't one noise but a thousand negotiations happening at once. We unload into cellar hatches and cold boxes for two hours, and men take delivery of the blocks without ever really seeing us, and I begin to understand what Riku meant about the machine.

The driver pays me in coins, counting them twice into my palm so we both see the number, and tells Riku he runs again Friday. Then he's gone, and I'm standing in the middle of the largest crowd of my life with wet boots and money I earned with my back.

I try asking if someone has seen him once, because not trying isn't in me. There's a tea seller at the mouth of the roofed street, old enough to have seen everything twice, with a queue that never quite dies. I buy a cup with the driver's coins and drink it standing at the end of her boards, and when the queue thins I ask whether two people off the coast buses came through yesterday — a tall quiet one, and a girl who worked trays for food in Ishibashi.

"Came through doing what — buying tea?" She doesn't pause her pour. "Half the coast has walked through this market in the last month. Burned out, registered out, walked out. Nobody remembers faces here."

"They'd have been hungry. Somebody sold them a first meal."

"Somebody sells everyone their first meal. That's the whole city." She hands a customer his change and takes my empty cup back, which means we're done. Then, because I have paid and I am still standing there and the queue has died properly: "You want to find one person in Takamatsu, stop asking markets. Find what he has to keep going back to — work, food, sleep — and stand near it. Now move along, you're blocking a paying man's elbow."

It lands like the shop woman in Kuroishi and the squid woman in Ishibashi, except in those towns the trail was a day old and warm. Here it walked into eight hundred streets of strangers. I put the coins away and find Riku leaning on a pillar, watching me learn it.

"No luck?" he says.

"None."

"Then we do it the way the tea lady said, which is the way I was going to say. You get work, you get fed, you get a roof, and you let the city bring the faces past you instead of chasing them down eight hundred streets." He pushes off the pillar. "Which brings us to tonight. Come on. There's a place."

---

The place is two wards back from the market, down a street where the shops get narrower and more honest — a tool sharpener, a mender, a public bath exhaling steam. At the end of it hangs a rope curtain and a wooden sign with one word burned into it: MINATO. Riku pulls me inside with him.

Inside is one long counter, a shelf of bottles, steam, and a radio talking quietly to itself in the corner. An old man sits at the far end with a beer he doesn't seem to be drinking. Behind the counter stands a broad-shouldered woman, fifty-something, bun speared with a chopstick, forearms like she has carried heavier things than kegs. She looks up when the curtain moves, and her eyes go over Riku, then me.

She doesn't say welcome. She ladles two bowls of oden and sets them on the counter before either of us has touched a stool.

Riku raises an eyebrow at her. "We haven't ordered yet."

"Boys in trouble eat standing up," she says. "That's how I know. Sit down before you fall down."

We sit. The broth is hot all the way down, the first food since Ishibashi that didn't come off a skewer, and I have to concentrate on not making a sound about it. The woman goes back to her pots without another word, and it's the old man at the end of the counter who speaks next.

"The pair of them look road-bruised, and thin with it," he says to nobody, or to the beer. Then, to us: "Where are you guys from?"

"East," Riku says.

"That's a report, not an answer." The old man turns on his stool with a creak that might be the stool and might be him. Ginger-root hands, a cardigan despite the steam. "Forty years I drove trams in this city. I can tell a ward by how a man says its name. You two aren't from any ward. So: east." He lets the word sit there, unimpressed.

"Rules of the house," the woman says from the pots, without turning. "Nobody in here is from anywhere. Everybody in here is from before. Gorō knows the rule, he just likes hearing himself lay the trap."

"Gorō," the old man agrees, pointing at his own chest, as if the introduction had been on his schedule all along. "And since the mama has spoiled the game, I'll give you something instead of taking it. You unloaded ice at North Market — I can smell the sawdust. The casual line there dies at sundown. Anyone who tells you there's evening work at the market is selling you a walk. Evening work in this city is the docks and the rail sheds, and the rail sheds check papers." He taps the counter once. "One fact. That's the toll for sitting near me. Next one costs you a fact back."

"He also knows this city better than anyone I've met," Riku says, "as long as you give him something back when he talks. And he'll tell you about the flood of '02 if you don't watch your feet."

"The flood of '02 is worth telling," Gorō says with dignity, and turns back to his beer.

The woman — Machi, Riku calls her — refills my bowl without asking, which is its own kind of sentence. When the counter empties toward closing, she jerks her chin at a doorway behind the shelf.

"There's a back room with mats. Working boys sleep there sometimes, when they've worked. Did you two work today?"

"Since before dawn," Riku says.

"Then it's yours tonight. Wash the bowls first. Both of you — I've seen his hands, now I want to see yours."

We wash the bowls. The water is hot and my fingers come back to life in it, aching. Through the doorway the back room is narrow and smells of dry rice and clean straw — the first proper roof over me since Sugi's.

Riku claims the mat by the wall on the grounds that he found the place, then lies there with his hands behind his head, looking at the ceiling.

"Tomorrow," he says, "that favor you owe me. I'm calling it."

I stop with my jacket half folded. "Calling it how?"

"There's a man two wards over who needs hands and hates new faces. I vouch for you, you work like you worked today, and you don't make me regret the vouch. You do that, we're square." He yawns. "After that, anything I do for you, I do because I feel like it. Which is worse for you, because I'm moody."

"That's the whole favor? Just work?"

"That's the whole favor. You were expecting a kidney?" He rolls over, away from the lamp. "Sleep, Aoyama. The city starts loud."

"Then call it," I say. "Tomorrow. Vouch me."

He doesn't turn back. "Already did. Don't make me look stupid at dawn."
