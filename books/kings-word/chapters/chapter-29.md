# Chapter 29 — Breach Window

We kill the truck engine in the ditch short of the west fence and sit in the dark until the floodlights tell us whether anyone saw the last hundred meters.

They did not. Not yet. The lights are still dark on this stretch. Mira has her hand on my wrist the way she does when she wants the mouth shut before it starts. Suzumori is in the back seat with the contact, stick of wood crooked in her hair, eyes on the pale concrete block that does not look like a prison from the road.

"Shizuhara," Mira says. Low. "Civil Protection and Relocation. That is the cover on the gate sign. Inside it is where they put people who saw the wrong sweep, or carried the wrong flag, or knew a name somebody upstairs did not want walking around — volunteers, irregulars, and the ones they keep in the letter-wing." She does not look at me when she says the next part. "Including at least one person my road will not leave in there."

"You said extract," I say. "You did not say a building with a double fence and floodlights."

"I said a quiet pull. The fence is why it has to stay quiet." She tips her chin at the dark wire. "We use the west service road and come in at the loading bay behind the kitchen wing. East wing holds the specials — that is the target. We do not walk the lobby. We do not invent a story for a colonel. We cut the outer wire where the contact marked, we take the service door the night clerk leaves unlatched for crate runs, and we are gone before the second count notices a gap."

Suzumori makes a dry sound in the back.

"And if the latch isn't open?"

"Then we leave," Mira says. "That is the whole point of clean. No loud mouth on the first door. No rifles in a corridor full of kids. You open one door if paper fails. One. Then we move."

I watch the fence. My shoulder still pulls when I turn too far. Haruki is east of us somewhere with Riku, or he is not, and I do not get to know which from a ditch.

"Why this building?" I ask. "Why not wait for a transfer truck the way you did with Suzumori?"

"Because the transfer trucks from here do not go somewhere we can reach." Mira's voice stays level. "The contact has been counting night loads for three weeks. People leave. The review board stamps it tidy. Nobody comes back to argue the stamp. If we wait for a transfer truck, we're waiting for paperwork that already says where he went."

"So we steal their inventory before they finish the paperwork," the contact says from the back, not joking hard enough to make it funny.

Mira does not correct the word. That is worse than if she had.

"We take one person out of east," she says. "Maybe two if the second is already at the door. We do not clear a wing. We do not make a speech. Kaito — if I say abort, you abort. If a kid walks into the hall, you do not use your mouth to make the kid useful. Clear?"

"Clear," I say.

Suzumori leans forward until I have to look at her.

"Boy," she says. "You put weight on a friend the last time a road got loud. Do not put weight on a building full of children because a door is slow. That mouth cuts both ways. I am still saying it."

"I heard you on the pine road."

"Hearing me once is not the same as doing it when it gets loud." She sits back. "Go. Or do not. Do not half-go and then invent a reason to finish what Mira told you not to start."

Mira opens her door. The night air smells like cut grass. She looks at me once — not begging, not soft. Checking whether I am going to be a problem before we reach the wire.

"Service ditch," she says. "If I stop, you stop. If I run, you run. We are not here to prove anything about your throat."

"Then what am I here for?"

"The one door a fake pass will not open," she says. "Same as the last place where your voice had to open the door. After that you are a pair of hands and a scraped voice we keep quiet. Walk."

---

The outer wire is farther from the road than it looked from the ditch.

We go on foot through scrub that has been cut back in the last month — fresh ends, no cover worth naming. The contact leads. Mira counts under her breath, not numbers for show, just the paces between the marks the contact chalked two nights ago when the patrol was on the east side. Suzumori stays with the truck. Mira would not bring her to the wire, and Suzumori would not stay if she thought the boy needed watching more than the exit did. They settled it without me.

At the cut, the contact produces cutters from under her jacket and works without talking. The wire sings once. Mira flinches at the sound. I keep my hands free and my mouth shut.

"Through," the contact whispers. "Low. The inner fence is another twenty meters. Blind spot under the camera if you stay on the painted drain."

We go. Gravel bites through my boots. The building rises ahead — pale concrete, long windows, a flagpole that looks like a school until you see the inward curve of the wire on top of the second fence. Floodlights on this stretch are still dark. Somewhere east, through the body of the building, a different white glare is already up. Mira notices. Her jaw tightens.

"They lit the far side," she says.

"Not us," the contact says. "Not yet. Keep moving."

The inner fence has a service gate with a padlock that should be serious and is not. The contact has a key that does not belong to her. She does not explain it. Mira does not ask in front of me. The gate opens on a hinge that has been oiled recently. That is either luck or someone inside who still thinks crate runs matter more than rifles.

We cross into the shadow of the kitchen wing. The loading bay door is a black mouth ahead. No canvas truck. No clerk with a stamp. A radio plays something cheerful through a wall.

Mira stops us flat against the concrete.

"Listen," she says.

I listen. Boots on a mezzanine somewhere inside — not the bored walk of a night count. Faster. A voice, soft even through glass and distance, saying something about holding blocks. Another voice answers about the west fence.

About us.

"They saw the truck," I say.

"Or they saw the cut," Mira says. "Or they are jumpy because somebody else is jumpy. It does not change the job. Take the service latch to the east stair, then back out through the cut. If the bay is guarded, we abort at the door and we do not argue in the open."

The contact checks her watch by feel.

"Night clerk on the service latch has four minutes in his smoke habit," she says. "After that he comes back and the latch is his again. You want in without a fight, this is the window. After this, we force it or we leave."

Mira looks at me.

"Door," she says. "If he is there, you ask him to open and forget. If he is not, I open. Either way we are inside in under a minute or we are gone. Ready?"

"Ready."

We move along the wall to the green service door under a dead bulb. The contact was right about the smoke habit — the latch is off, the clerk is a shape at the far corner of the bay with his back to us and a cigarette making a small orange point. Mira's hand finds the handle.

Then the west floodlights slam on.

Hard white paints the bay, the wire, our boots, and the cut we left in the outer fence. The clerk drops his cigarette and turns. Somewhere above us a door bangs. The soft mezzanine voice is not soft anymore when it hits the stairwell speakers.

"West perimeter. Hold all blocks. Inventory stays put."

Mira's plan is still on her face for half a second — abort, ditch, truck. Then the clerk sees us, and his mouth opens around a shout, and the clean window is already dead.

"Open the door," I say, and I put the weight under it before Mira can pull me back. "You never saw us. Face the wall."

Copper hits the back of my nose. The clerk's eyes empty the way the quiet-floor clerk's did. He turns to the wall. His hands hang. The shout dies in his throat without becoming sound.

Mira stares at me hard enough to cut.

"I said if I say abort—"

"You didn't say abort," I say. My voice scrapes. Blood is already starting on my lip. "You told me to get the door open. The lights hit, he saw us, and he was about to bring the whole west side down on our heads. What did you want me to do — wait for him to finish the shout?"

The contact is already through, holding the service door with her shoulder. "Finish this inside. The ditch is lit."

Mira goes through. I follow. The door shuts on the white glare and the bay smells like ice melt and old fish. Behind us the clerk stands facing the wall as ordered. Ahead, a concrete stair goes up into the building that called us inventory over a speaker.

"East wing is two turns past the kitchen junction," the contact says, moving. "Specials door has a letter on the shift board, not a number. Do not stop for A or B. Do not look at the kids if they look at you."

"Kaito." Mira catches my sleeve at the first landing. She keeps her voice down and she does not waste it on the blood. "That was the one door. You do not open the next one because you are angry at lights. You open it because I point at it. Say you heard me."

"I heard you."

"Say you will do it."

I look at her. The scraped voice wants a shorter answer than she is asking for. The building's recycled air sits in my throat with the blood.

"I will open the door you point at," I say. "I will not open another door on my own while you're still running the plan you gave me."

She holds my eyes a second longer, then nods once, small, like she heard me and does not fully trust it.

"Then keep your mouth shut until I point," she says. "Move."

---

The kitchen junction is empty of cooks and full of the wrong kind of quiet.

A line of kids in armbands is frozen against the far wall where a guard told them to hold — thirteen, fourteen, clean clothes, cloth bands on their sleeves. They watch us pass the way people watch a fire in the next building: close enough to feel heat, not close enough to know if they should run. One of them almost lifts a hand. The guard's rifle tip comes up an inch. The hand drops.

Mira does not look at them. The contact does not look at them. I look once and then I do not, because looking turns them into a problem my mouth wants to solve, and Mira already told me what happens if I invent a second job.

We take the east turn. The shift board on the wall has columns in wet marker — A, B, C — and a blank strip with a letter painted where a number should be. East. No count. The contact was not guessing.

Boots hit the west corridor behind us. Not ours.

"Faster," Mira says.

The specials door is a plain door with a card reader and a guard who is not silent so much as bored past speech. He sees Mira first, then me, then the blood on my lip, and his hand goes to the radio on his shoulder.

Mira points at the door.

I do not wait for a cleaner sentence than the one that gets us through.

"Open it. Drop the radio. You forget that we were here."

He opens it. The radio hits the floor. His eyes go empty and stay that way. My nose prickles high up and the edge of the hall goes gray. Mira catches my elbow when my knees tip, and we are through the letter-door into a corridor that smells like disinfectant and old paper, and the contact pulls the door shut on the hold order still rolling through the speakers.

Behind the first glass panel on the right, a girl about seventeen looks up from a tablet as if we are late for an appointment she did not book. Short dark hair. War-issue shirt. She does not scream. She does not smile. She takes in the blood on my mouth and Mira's grip on my arm and the contact's cutters still in her fist, and her first words are not a greeting.

"You lit the west fence," she says. "Do you have any idea what that does to a night count?"

Mira steadies me against the wall.

"We're here to pull someone out of the east wing," Mira says. "War paperwork hold — not a volunteer. You can yell at us about the fence once we're moving."

The girl stands. The tablet stays in her hand like she does not trust the room without it.

"I am Serizawa Mei," she says. "And you just gave Habu a reason to blame every empty bunk on you before morning. If you came to pull someone out, you picked the worst night — or you're the reason it turned bad." She looks at me again, at the scraped voice, at the blood still wet on my lip. "Which door did you open to get in here?"

"Service door off the bay, then this one," I say. "Paper wasn't going to do either."

Mei glances at the specials door we came through, then at Mira.

"Then your quiet pull is already blown," she says. "Tell me who you came for before the guards in the west corridor reach this wing — and tell me if you're leaving the rest of us locked down while the speakers call us inventory."
