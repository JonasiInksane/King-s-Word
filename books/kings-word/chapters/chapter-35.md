# Chapter 35 — Unsealed

We never made it to the top of the kitchen stair.

A corporal caught us on the third step with a hold order already in his mouth — C stays in bays, west is not our business, get back before he writes us for inventing a kitchen check. Riku argued once. The corporal lifted his rifle an inch. We walked back to bay three with the floodlight glare still in our eyes and Katō's empty bunk waiting the way it had been waiting since morning.

That was hours ago.

C is locked to itself now. The junction door toward A and B has been carded shut since Habu told everyone to hold. For a while we heard the west side of the building doing something loud — motors, boots, a stretch of gunfire that made the high windows shake. Then the speakers went quiet. Habu did not come back. The corridor went so still I could hear Ōhashi's pencil when he turned a page he had already turned twice.

Bay three sits with its shirts on and its shoes ready. Nobody sleeps. Katō's mattress is still empty. His cap is still on the hook. Ōhashi has the notebook open on his knee.

"Board will call forty-one in the morning if they feel like lying," he says, not for the first time. "I have forty-two living names and one struck. Somebody has to be here when they invent the count."

"Somebody has to be here when they invent the next review," Riku says. His bad arm is tight against his ribs. He has been walking the aisle without stopping for more than a minute at a time. "We sat through the loud part. We are still sitting. If Katō walked into that noise, we are not finding him by polishing bunks."

"Mei told us to stay off the bay," I say.

"Mei is not here," Riku says. "East got sealed with the rest of us. Whatever she owed us for morning counts, she cannot pay it from behind a locked door."

"She said she would ask who left if the board went short again," I say.

"She said a lot of things before the locks closed." Riku stops at Katō's bunk, looks at the empty cap hook, and keeps walking. "Talk does not open doors."

I almost argue. Then the sealed C door at the end of the corridor clicks.

Not a guard's bored card. The hard sound of a lock that has been red all night going green. Footsteps come fast down the waxed floor — one person, not a squad — and Serizawa Mei pushes into bay three's doorway with a dark tablet in one hand and a cloth lanyard in the other. There is blood dried in a thin line near her hairline and gray ash on her sleeves that does not look like kitchen soot. She looks like she has been running.

"Get up," she says. "All of you who can walk. The door to C is open again, and it will not stay that way. Down the medical stair, then the service corridor toward the west fence — most of Habu's guards on that side are already dead. We are not walking forty people through the lobby. Two minutes. Then I leave with whoever is at the door."

Ōhashi stands so fast the notebook nearly falls. Men start pulling on shoes. Riku is already halfway to the aisle when Mei lifts the lanyard.

"How did you open that?" I ask, moving with him. "Habu sealed us in."

"I took his key out of his desk." She talks while she walks the bay toward the door, not while we stand and listen. "Somebody shut his mouth and left him in his glass office. A and B are not coming back to breakfast. Their evening review was a kill room — gas from the vents, then a hose to wash the floor. They thanked the kids into it in groups of ten. I watched. I was too late to stop it."

Riku's step hitches. His good hand finds a bunk rail hard enough that the metal complains, but he does not sit. He keeps going, jaw locked, eyes wet.

"That was the volunteer track," he says. Voice flat, for me more than for her. "Hot food. The review board sees you first."

"Yes," Mei says. "And that track ends in a white room with drains. The curry kids are dead. Habu left C locked and east sealed — that is why any of you are still breathing. If your Katō wore an A or B armband, he is probably dead with them. If he was C, he is still only a line in that book, and we are not hunting him from inside a bay."

Ōhashi's pencil drops. He does not pick it up. He shoves the notebook under his arm.

"Bay three," he says. "Anyone who can walk without a guard's permission, come with us. Anyone who cannot, hide in the wash-room and do not answer if someone starts calling names. I am not coming back to explain the board."

Six men fall in behind us. Two stay. I do not have time to hate them for it. We hit the bay door and turn into the C corridor toward the junction, Mei in front with Habu's card still in her fist.

"Who cut the west fence?" I ask, keeping pace. "Who started all of this?"

"People from outside," Mei says without slowing. "Not Habu's people, and not the kind who fill out FDC forms. They call themselves Remnant. They break people out of places like this when the board has already decided you belong on a list. Tonight they cut the west fence to take me out of the east wing. I was the person they came for."

"You're with them," Riku says. Not a question. An accusation. He is walking again, bad arm tight, not looking at her face.

"No. I was the one they meant to steal." She does not flinch. "A woman brought wire cutters and a boy with a scraped voice. When he tells someone to do something, they do it. They set off the fence lights. Habu used that as his excuse to wipe A and B. The Remnant people tried to pull me out and failed once the killing started. The boy did not leave with them at first — he went through the west side of the building and killed the guards who came for him. The woman dragged him out before he could keep going. I refused to walk out with them while C was still locked. Habu's key opens the east doors and the C locks. I used it. That is as much as you get."

"So the Remnant started the night that killed the volunteers," Ōhashi says from behind us, breathing hard around the notebook.

"Habu started the kill room," Mei says. "Remnant cut a fence and gave him an excuse he already had written down. If you want the man to blame, he is upstairs in the glass office. His mouth is shut. He is still breathing. I left him that way because I needed his keycard more than I needed a corpse."

My throat goes tight. A boy who tells armed men what to do, and they do it — that sounds like Kaito. I do not say his name yet.

At the junction I catch her sleeve for half a step.

"The boy with the scraped voice," I say, quiet. "Did you get his name?"

"Kurosawa," she says. "The woman called him that once when she was dragging him. I did not ask for more. Keep walking."

The name hits harder than I am ready for. I keep my face ordinary because Ōhashi's six are right behind us. Riku's eyes cut to me — he heard.

We keep moving.

A and B's painted lines are empty — no armbands, no kids arguing about Thursday curry. A tipped cup lies against the B stripe where someone left it when the hold order hit. Riku walks through without looking left or right. One of Ōhashi's six whispers a name toward the empty B line and then shuts up when Mei's head turns. Ōhashi counts under his breath anyway, notebook tight under his arm.

Mei leads toward the medical corridor. Behind us C's door stays open on the men who would not walk. Ahead, the service path is dark and quiet. Somewhere far above us a radio crackles once and dies. Nobody answers it.

At the medical door Mei badges Habu's card. The reader stays red for a second longer than I like, then goes green. Cold air and disinfectant come up the stair. She holds the door with her hip and waves us through — me, Riku, Ōhashi, the six — and does not look back at C.

"Keep your heads down," she says. "If a guard asks you anything, give him a boring answer and keep walking. Do not argue. Do not explain."

"What if they do not take a boring answer?" one of the six asks.

"Then you are already in trouble, and talking more will not help," Mei says. "Move."

We go down. Our shoes scrape the metal. Cold air pushes up from below. One of the six stumbles and catches the rail. Mei does not wait for him — she is already on the next landing, Habu's card out for the door at the bottom.

Riku catches my elbow on the turn, hard enough that I have to look at him.

"That was Kaito," he says under the fan noise. "Do not tell me it was some other Kurosawa. I stood on the pine road when he dropped that rifle with his mouth. Same boy. Same mess. What are you going to do when we hit the fence and he is already gone west?"

"I am going to get us out of this building first," I say. "Then I am going to find him. Same as before."

"Same as before almost put us on a volunteer list." His grip loosens. "Fine. Out first. But when we clear the wire, you do not get to go quiet on me again."

Mei badges the lower door. The lock clicks green. Service corridor air hits — oil, wet concrete, the faint stink of the court.

"Clear," she says. "Stay on my back. If I stop, you stop."

She steps through. We follow.
