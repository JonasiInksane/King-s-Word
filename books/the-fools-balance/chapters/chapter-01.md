# Chapter 1 — The Fool Arrives

Sir Renji Arata had three reports.

Three different crimes.

Three different names.

A thief on the east road who stole nothing worth listing—only a merchant’s certainty that the road was safe. A clown who walked into a wedding, clapped when the vows finished, and left before anyone decided whether to laugh or call the watch. A demon, according to a junior Hero’s seal, written in the careful panic of someone who did not want to be wrong in public.

_Demon activity. Unconfirmed. Requesting senior classification._

Same trail. Same direction. Same ridiculous little bell mentioned once in a margin, as if the writer had been embarrassed to ink it.

Renji hated impossible cases.

He folded the third report until the paper creased like a blade and tucked it into his belt. Classification was the Empire’s favorite kindness. Name a thing, and the thing became a category. Categories could be hunted. Categories could be filed. Categories did not sit in the mud and smile at you while a village burned behind them.

Commander Hoshikawa’s dispatch had been shorter than any of the reports.

_Identify. Contain. Do not escalate without cause._

Renji rode alone. Silver armor caught the late light in disciplined flashes. His katana rested easy at his hip. Rei—the living current every Hero learned to feel and spend—coiled under his skin the way lightning waited in stormcloud, not eager, not sleeping. He had been a Hero long enough to know eagerness got people killed, and long enough to know sleep got them killed slower.

The road into Ashvale should have smelled of river mud and cooking smoke.

It smelled of wet ash and something sweet gone wrong.

---

Ashvale’s outer wall had been a joke even before today—stacked stone meant more for pride than for war. Pride lay in pieces across the lane. The gate hung half off its hinges like a tooth about to fall. Beyond it, roofs had collapsed inward. A well-house leaned as if listening for water that would never answer. Black scars crawled the timber where fire had eaten and then, somehow, stopped—not smothered by rain, not fought out by a bucket line. Stopped as if the fire itself had lost interest.

Renji dismounted at the break in the wall. His boots found glass. A child’s wooden horse lay on its side in the grit, one wheel still turning in the wind that had no business being this gentle.

He listened.

No screaming.

No clash of steel.

Only a thin sound—metal, small, ridiculous.

A bell.

Renji’s hand found his sword. Not drawn. Ready. Ready was different from drawn; ready still left room for the world to be ordinary.

He stepped through the ruined gate.

In the square, amid broken carts and the black ribs of a market stall, a man sat on an overturned crate as if the crate had been placed there for him.

White hair, messy, as if the wind had opinions and he had none. A long black coat patched in places that suggested repair by someone who did not care about matching thread. A faded red scarf. A cracked white theatrical mask covered his face completely—the crack ran from the left eye toward the painted mouth like a fault line deciding whether to finish the job. No skin. No chin. Only porcelain and that fixed smile. Through the eye holes, golden eyes watched the square. At his waist, a small silver bell chimed whenever he shifted his weight.

He was laughing.

Not the sharp laugh of victory. Not the broken laugh of shock. Something looser—delighted, almost private—as if the ruins had told him a joke and he was the only one who understood the punchline.

Renji’s first thought was madness.

His second was that madness did not usually wait for an audience.

“You,” Renji said. The word came out clean. Hero-clean. “Stand. Identify yourself.”

The laughing stopped as if cut with a knife. The man’s head tipped. Behind the mask, golden eyes found Renji with an ease that made Renji’s spine tighten. The painted smile did not move. It looked like it had never needed to.

“Oh,” the man said. “You’re early.”

Renji waited.

The man looked around the destroyed village—slow, almost fond, as if admiring a stage after the actors had fled. Ash drifted past his shoulder. Somewhere a timber settled with a tired crack.

“Good,” he said. “I was starting to get bored.”

Renji did not answer that. Early implied a schedule. Bored implied he had been waiting among corpses and ruin for entertainment. Intention, in a destroyed village, was a word that wanted blood.

“Name,” Renji said.

The man stood. The bell chimed, cheerful as a festival. He brushed ash from his coat with the seriousness of someone cleaning a table before dinner.

“People call me lots of things,” he said. “Thief. Clown. Demon. Once someone called me ‘sir,’ but I think they were dying, so it doesn’t count.”

Renji’s jaw set. “Your name.”

“Harlequin,” the man offered, as if lending Renji a cup. “Or Akihiko. Or The Fool, if you like your titles dramatic. I answer to the bell, mostly. It’s louder than I am.”

He flicked the silver bell with a fingertip. It sang once, bright and stupid in the ruin.

Renji stepped closer. Ash gritted under his boots. Close enough to measure distance. Close enough that lightning Rei could close the rest if the man moved wrong.

“This village was under Imperial protection,” Renji said. “Who did this?”

Akihiko tilted his head the other way.

“I don’t know,” he said.

Renji’s patience, trained for courts and battlefields alike, held. Barely. “You don’t know.”

“No.” He looked faintly offended on behalf of honesty. “I wasn’t there.”

“Then why are you here?”

“Because someone left the door open.”

“What door?”

The mask’s smile seemed to widen a fraction—too pleased, too sharp for someone claiming ignorance. Renji told himself it was a trick of the crack and the light.

“The one everyone agreed not to look for.”

A sound cut across the square—wood screaming, a roof giving up. Renji’s head snapped toward the eastern lane. Smoke still threaded from a half-collapsed house. Under the groan of timber, a child’s voice rose thin as wire.

“Mama—!”

Renji moved.

Lightning Rei woke along his legs. Shiki—the body craft, Rei forced into muscle and tendon—sharpened him into something that made distance negotiable. He crossed the square in a blur of silver and blue spark, boots finding purchase on broken stone. The house’s front beam was failing. Inside, through the smoke-hole of a doorframe, he saw them: a woman pinned under a fallen joist, a boy no older than seven clawing at the wood with hands too small for the work, and behind them a secondary collapse starting—ceiling boards peeling like scabs.

Renji’s blade cleared its sheath. He could cut the joist. Cutting the joist might drop the rest. He could brace. Bracing meant time he might not have. He chose the third option Heroes were taught when the world refused clean math: get the living out and let the building finish dying alone.

He reached the threshold—

—and nearly collided with white hair and a patched coat.

Akihiko was inside the doorway.

Renji’s gut seized before his mind caught up—cold, animal, wrong. No human should have been able to move from the square to that doorway without making a sound. The bell had been silent. The ash had not whispered. Renji had been watching the beam. He told himself that was why. He had not been watching the man. That was the only explanation that did not require inventing a new kind of Rei—and Renji refused to invent things under fire.

Still, for one shameful heartbeat, his grip on the sword had tightened like a man facing something that should not exist.

“Borrowing,” Akihiko said cheerfully to no one, and kicked the support beam—not the joist pinning the woman, but a scorched upright two paces left that looked, to Renji’s eye, like the only thing still doing its job.

The beam cracked.

Renji’s shout died unfinished.

The roof did not fall on them.

It fell away—weight shifting, collapse rerouted, the house shedding itself into the alley like a beast rolling to expose a softer side. The joist lifted enough for Akihiko to slide an arm under the woman and haul. He moved wrong. He moved like a drunk stealing furniture. He moved like someone who had never studied structure.

The woman came free. The boy scrambled. Dust exploded. Renji caught the boy as the doorway coughed them into daylight, and for half a breath Renji’s world was only small ribs under his gauntlet and the smell of ash in a child’s hair.

Then the house finished dying behind them with a roar that would have been a funeral if anyone had been left to speak it.

Akihiko set the woman down against a trough that still held a finger of dirty water. He patted her shoulder once, gentle, absurd, as if she had coughed at a dinner table.

“There,” he said. “See? Kicking things works. People should kick more things. Politely.”

The woman stared at him. Tears cut clean lines through the soot on her face. The boy clung to Renji’s armor and would not look up.

Renji’s Rei still crackled under his skin, unused on the cut he had planned. His sword hung in his hand like an argument without a sentence.

“You nearly killed them,” Renji said.

Akihiko blinked. “Did I? I was aiming for the other one. The dead one. The one pretending.”

“That beam was holding—”

“No,” he said, suddenly flat, as if correcting a child who had miscounted apples. Then the brightness snapped back. “Sorry. I get cranky when wood lies.”

Renji looked back at the ruin. The alley collapse had missed a second house by a man’s width. Missed a water barrel that, if crushed, would have flooded the lane into a slurry and trapped the next survivors crawling from a cellar door Renji had not even seen until now—three faces, pale, coughing, blinking at daylight as if daylight were a rumor.

Lucky.

Or not lucky.

Renji’s training offered a colder word: _precise_.

He did not like that word in Akihiko’s mouth. He did not like it in Akihiko’s hands.

“Stay with them,” Renji told the woman, and meant the boy too. “Do not run the main road. Wait for Imperial retrieval.”

She nodded because Heroes said things that became instructions, and instructions were easier than hope.

Renji turned on him.

“You will explain what happened here.”

“Will I?” Akihiko asked, interested, as if Renji had proposed a game.

Renji leveled his blade. Lightning crawled the steel in thin blue threads—enough to warn, not enough to rewrite the fight. “By Imperial authority of Akatsuki, under Hero command, you are detained for questioning regarding the destruction of Ashvale and the endangerment of its people.”

He looked at the sword the way some men looked at weather.

“That’s a very long sentence,” he said. “Do you charge by the word?”

“Surrender your Rei and kneel.”

“Ah.” He brightened. “Rules.”

He stepped off the line of the blade—left, not back—sliding around Renji’s point as if the stance had a gap Renji had forgotten to close. Renji adjusted. Steel followed. Akihiko raised both empty hands.

“I don’t have a weapon,” he said. “Does that make this unfair, or more fair? I can never remember which one Heroes prefer.”

“Last warning.”

“You already used one,” he said. “That was it. Do I get a receipt?”

Renji moved.

He did not waste motion. Shiki drove his first cut along a line that would stop at the coat, not the throat—Hero doctrine: disable, then question. Lightning Rei sharpened the edge’s intent; the air cracked with the nearness of it.

Akihiko fell.

Not struck—or maybe struck by nothing but his own feet. Heels slipping on ash, body folding into a sprawl so complete it looked accidental. Renji’s blade passed through empty air. Follow-through carried him a half-step past. Bad footing. Bad timing. Renji’s, or his. Renji checked, pivoted, cut again for the wrist that would bind—

—and found his own sheath swinging up into his path. Akihiko had rolled through the fall into a sit with Renji’s empty sheath in both hands. When had he taken it? During the slip. During the laugh. During the half-second Renji’s eyes had tracked steel instead of fingers.

Wood met steel with a stupid, solid _clack_.

Renji’s eyes widened a fraction. He twisted, freed the blade, kicked for the sheath—

Akihiko let go.

The sheath clattered. Renji’s kick hit nothing—or hit the place he had been a blink ago, which was the same as nothing. For one exposed heartbeat Renji’s weight was committed wrong, and a finger—just a finger—tapped the flat of Renji’s katana as if testing a pot for heat.

“Hot,” he observed. “You care a lot.”

Renji’s next strike came harder. Pride was a sin. Leaving an opening was worse. Lightning flared. The cut would have split a training post and the post behind it.

Akihiko stepped into Renji’s guard.

Closer—not faster that Renji could swear to. Into the space where a long blade became an awkward stick, where Renji’s perfect distance collapsed into elbows and breath. His forehead nearly bumped Renji’s chin. Golden eyes filled Renji’s vision, amused.

“Ow,” he said, though nothing had touched him. “You’re very stabby for someone who wants answers.”

Renji drove a gauntleted fist toward his ribs—close-range, Shiki-backed, honest.

Akihiko laughed.

Renji’s fist met coat—or ash—or the idea of a man. No blur. No afterimage he could name. Only the sick certainty that either he had missed a target at arm’s length, or the target had never intended to be where Renji’s eyes said he was. Renji’s own momentum turned him toward the trough where the rescued woman still sat. He stopped the strike before it became a crime against the innocent. The stop cost him a grunt. Cost him balance. Cost him the clean story of the exchange.

Later, if anyone asked, he would not know which account to give.

When he turned again, Akihiko stood five paces off, holding Renji’s dropped sheath like a conductor’s baton. He offered it forward with both hands, ceremonial and absurd.

“You dropped this,” he said. “Heroes should keep their things. The world is full of thieves.”

Renji took the sheath. His fingers brushed Akihiko’s. Warm. Human. No demonic cold. No holy burn. Just skin and ash.

That, somehow, was worse.

“What are you?” Renji asked.

Akihiko tipped his head; the painted smile tipped with him. “Hungry, mostly. Also handsome. The second one’s disputed. Hard to verify.”

He dug in his coat and held up an empty paper wrapper between two fingers, solemn as a priest presenting evidence. There was nothing left inside but crumbs and a smear of something sticky.

“I only packed one snack,” he said. “You arrived before I could steal another. Rude.”

“Why save them?”

“The boy was loud,” he said, as if that settled law. “Loud children are hard to ignore. Like bells.” He flicked his own. “Also, roofs that want to quit are rude. I hate rude architecture.”

“And the village?” Renji’s voice tightened. “Who attacked Ashvale?”

Akihiko’s gaze drifted—not to Renji, not to the survivors, but to the leaning gate and the road beyond, where evening had begun to bruise the sky.

“Dunno,” he said. “Someone with bad manners. Or good manners and a worse hobby. People get weird about doors.”

“Speak plainly.”

“I am speaking plainly,” Akihiko said. “You’re just listening in Hero.”

Renji sheathed his sword. The click of steel home sounded like a decision he had not finished making. Containment required a living subject. Escalation without cause violated Hoshikawa’s line. Cause stood all around them in charcoal and silence, and still Renji could not point to this man’s hand and say _there_.

“You call yourself The Fool,” Renji said.

“Because everyone else already decided what everyone was supposed to be,” Akihiko replied, light as gossip. Then, brighter, as if remembering a joke: “Hero. Villain. Victim. Homework.”

“And you?”

“I hate assignments.” He wrinkled his nose. “Especially the ones written in blood. Terrible handwriting. Very dramatic. Nobody thinks about the cleanup.”

Renji stared at him. “You will come with me to the capital. Willingly, or bound.”

“Bound is a strong look on some people,” Akihiko said. “On me it just looks like fashion. Also itchy.”

He walked past Renji.

Not fleeing. Not charging. Walking toward the ruined gate as if the conversation had reached its natural end and politeness required him to leave the table. The bell chimed with each step. Renji’s hand returned to his hilt.

“Stop.”

Akihiko stopped under the leaning gate-arch. He put a palm to the scarred wood. For a moment he looked almost solemn—almost like a man touching a grave.

Then: “Ah.”

He looked at the gate the way a cook looks at a pot left on the fire too long.

“Still closed.”

Renji’s brow furrowed. “What?”

“That’s rude,” Akihiko said.

Rei moved.

Renji felt it—not lightning, not Shiki in the body, not Kotodama in a spoken seal, not Kage twisting what the eye trusted. None of the three disciplines Heroes were taught to name. Wrong. Familiar only in the way a bad dream is familiar.

The gate did not shatter.

Timber and hinges gave up their job without a blast. Dust lifted. The arch stopped working as a barrier—no crater, no clean break-lines—and wood and stone settled into the lane as if they had always been rubble there. For a breath it looked like Ashvale had never owned a gate at all.

Survivors cried out anyway—not from flying spars, but from a landmark vanishing while their eyes were open. Renji’s Rei flared on instinct and found nothing to catch. His blue veil closed on empty air.

When the dust thinned, the road lay open. Nothing stood between the ruin and the Empire’s horizon.

Akihiko stood in the middle of what had been an arch, coat gray with powder, smiling like a man who had fixed a stuck latch.

Renji’s voice came out low. “Why?”

He looked at the place where the gate had been—and could not, for a moment, remember how high the hinges had hung.

A criminal had done this.

A monster had saved the survivors.

A madman had noticed what every soldier would have missed—the beam that was already dead, the cellar mouths, the quiet that mattered more than the fire.

And the worst part was that Renji could not decide which description was wrong.

Akihiko turned.

For the first time since Renji had entered the square, the laughter was gone from everything except the eyes.

“You’re a hero, right?” The Fool asked.

Renji did not lower his guard. Ash still drifted between them like slow snow. Behind him, a village that had been a place was becoming a report. Ahead of him, Akihiko waited as if the answer mattered more than the sword—as if he had been waiting for a Hero specifically, and Renji’s arrival was not coincidence but confirmation.

“I am Sir Renji Arata,” Renji said. “Hero of Akatsuki.”

Behind the mask, something eased—the eyes, not the porcelain. Relieved, almost.

“Good,” he said.

He stepped through the open road, close enough that Renji could have ended him in one honest cut, and did not look at the blade at all.

“I was worried nobody would come.”
