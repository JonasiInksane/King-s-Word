# Chapter 1 — The Fool Arrives

Sir Renji Arata had three reports on the same trail, and none of them agreed.

A thief on the east road who stole nothing worth listing—only a merchant’s certainty that the road was safe. A clown who walked into a wedding, clapped when the vows finished, and left before anyone decided whether to laugh or call the watch. A demon, according to a junior Hero’s seal, written in the careful panic of someone who did not want to be wrong in public.

_Demon activity. Unconfirmed. Requesting senior classification._

Same direction. Same ridiculous little bell mentioned once in a margin, as if the writer had been embarrassed to ink it.

Renji hated impossible cases.

He folded the third report and tucked it into his belt. Classification was how the Empire made problems hunt-able. Name a thing, and it became a category. Categories could be chased. Categories did not sit in the mud and smile while a village burned behind them.

Commander Hoshikawa’s dispatch had been shorter than any of the reports.

_Identify. Contain. Do not escalate without cause._

Renji rode alone. Silver armor caught the late light. His katana rested easy at his hip. Rei—the living current every Hero learned to feel and spend—coiled under his skin the way lightning waited in stormcloud, not eager, not sleeping. He had been a Hero long enough to know eagerness got people killed, and long enough to know sleep got them killed slower.

The road into Ashvale should have smelled of river mud and cooking smoke. Instead it smelled of wet ash.

Renji slowed his horse.

---

Ashvale’s outer wall had never been much—stacked stone meant more for show than for war. Parts of it lay scattered across the lane. The gate leaned sideways, one hinge torn loose. Beyond it, roofs had collapsed into the street. A well-house tilted. Black scars crawled the timber where fire had eaten and then stopped—not smothered by rain, not fought out by a bucket line. Just stopped.

Renji dismounted at the break in the wall. His boots found glass. A child’s wooden horse lay in the grit with one wheel still turning.

He listened.

No voices.

Just a bell—metal, small, ridiculous.

Renji’s hand found his sword. Not drawn. Ready.

He stepped through the ruined gate.

In the square, amid broken carts and the black ribs of a market stall, a man sat on an overturned crate as if someone had put it there for him.

White hair, messy. A long black coat patched with thread that did not match. A faded red scarf. A cracked white theatrical mask covered his face completely—the crack ran from the left eye toward the painted mouth. No skin. No chin. Only porcelain and that fixed smile. Through the eye holes, golden eyes watched the square. At his waist, a small silver bell chimed whenever he shifted his weight.

He was laughing.

Not the sharp laugh of victory. Not the broken laugh of shock. Something looser—as if the ruins had told him a joke and he was the only one who got it.

Renji’s first thought was madness. His second was that madness did not usually wait for an audience.

“You,” Renji said. Clean. “Stand. Identify yourself.”

The laughing stopped. The man’s head tipped. Behind the mask, golden eyes found Renji. The painted smile did not move.

“Oh,” the man said. “You’re early.”

Renji waited.

The man looked around the destroyed village—slow, almost fond. Ash drifted past his shoulder. Somewhere a timber settled with a tired crack.

“Good,” he said. “I was starting to get bored.”

Renji did not answer that. Early implied a schedule. Bored implied he had been waiting here on purpose.

“Name,” Renji said.

The man stood. The bell chimed. He brushed ash from his coat with the seriousness of someone cleaning a table before dinner.

“People call me lots of things,” he said. “Thief. Clown. Demon. Once someone called me ‘sir,’ but I think they were dying, so it doesn’t count.”

Renji’s jaw set. “Your name.”

“Harlequin,” the man offered. “Or Akihiko. Or The Fool, if you like your titles dramatic. I answer to the bell, mostly. It’s louder than I am.”

He flicked the silver bell with a fingertip. It sang once, bright and stupid in the ruin.

Renji stepped closer. Ash gritted under his boots. Close enough to measure distance. Close enough that lightning Rei could close the rest if the man moved wrong.

“This village was under Imperial protection,” Renji said. “Who did this?”

Akihiko tilted his head the other way.

“I don’t know,” he said.

Renji held his patience. Barely. “You don’t know.”

“No.” He looked faintly offended. “I wasn’t there.”

“Then why are you here?”

“Because someone left the door open.”

“What door?”

The crack in the mask caught the light and made the painted smile look sharper than it was. Renji told himself that was all it was.

“The one everyone agreed not to look for.”

A sound cut across the square—wood screaming, a roof giving up. Renji’s head snapped toward the eastern lane. Smoke still threaded from a half-collapsed house. Under the groan of timber, a child’s voice rose thin as wire.

“Mama—!”

Renji moved.

Lightning Rei woke along his legs. Shiki—the body craft, Rei forced into muscle and tendon—sharpened him until distance became negotiable. He crossed the square in a blur of silver and blue spark. The house’s front beam was failing. Through the smoke-hole of a doorframe he saw them: a woman pinned under a fallen joist, a boy no older than seven clawing at the wood, and behind them a secondary collapse starting—ceiling boards peeling loose.

Renji’s blade cleared its sheath. He could cut the joist. Cutting might drop the rest. He could brace. Bracing meant time he might not have. He chose the option Heroes were taught when the world refused clean math: get the living out and let the building finish falling alone.

He reached the threshold—

—and nearly collided with white hair and a patched coat.

Akihiko was inside the doorway.

Renji’s gut seized. Akihiko had been in the square. Now he was in the doorway. No footsteps. No bell. No ash kicked up behind him.

Renji told himself he had missed it—he had been watching the failing beam, not the man. That was the only explanation that did not invent a new kind of Rei under fire.

Still, for one heartbeat, his grip on the sword tightened hard enough to hurt.

“Borrowing,” Akihiko said cheerfully to no one, and kicked the support beam—not the joist pinning the woman, but a scorched upright two paces left that looked, to Renji’s eye, like the only thing still doing its job.

The beam cracked.

Renji’s shout died unfinished.

The roof did not fall on them.

It fell away—weight shifting, collapse turning into the alley. The joist lifted enough for Akihiko to slide an arm under the woman and haul. He moved wrong. He moved like a drunk stealing furniture. He moved like someone who had never studied structure.

The woman came free. The boy scrambled. Dust exploded. Renji caught the boy as the doorway coughed them into daylight, and for half a breath his world was only small ribs under his gauntlet and ash in a child’s hair.

Then the house finished falling behind them with a roar.

Akihiko set the woman down against a trough that still held a finger of dirty water. He patted her shoulder once, gentle and absurd.

“There,” he said. “See? Kicking things works. People should kick more things. Politely.”

The woman stared at him. Tears cut clean lines through the soot on her face. The boy clung to Renji’s armor and would not look up.

Renji’s Rei still crackled under his skin, unused on the cut he had planned. His sword hung in his hand with nowhere useful to go.

“You nearly killed them,” Renji said.

Akihiko blinked. “Did I? I was aiming for the other one. The dead one. The one pretending.”

“That beam was holding—”

“No,” he said, suddenly flat. Then the brightness snapped back. “Sorry. I get cranky when wood lies.”

Renji looked back at the ruin. The alley collapse had missed a second house by a man’s width. Missed a water barrel that, if crushed, would have flooded the lane and trapped the next survivors crawling from a cellar door Renji had not even seen until now—three faces, pale, coughing, blinking at daylight.

Lucky.

Or not.

Renji’s training offered a colder word: _precise_.

He did not like that word near this man’s hands.

“Stay with them,” Renji told the woman, and meant the boy too. “Do not run the main road. Wait for Imperial retrieval.”

She nodded.

Renji turned on him.

“You will explain what happened here.”

“Will I?” Akihiko asked, interested.

Renji leveled his blade. Lightning crawled the steel in thin blue threads—enough to warn, not enough to end anything. “By Imperial authority of Akatsuki, under Hero command, you are detained for questioning regarding the destruction of Ashvale and the endangerment of its people.”

Akihiko looked at the sword the way some men looked at weather.

“That’s a very long sentence,” he said. “Do you charge by the word?”

“Surrender your Rei and kneel.”

“Ah.” He brightened. “Rules.”

He stepped off the line of the blade—left, not back—sliding around Renji’s point as if the stance had a gap Renji had forgotten to close. Renji adjusted. Steel followed. Akihiko raised both empty hands.

“I don’t have a weapon,” he said. “Does that make this unfair, or more fair? I can never remember which one Heroes prefer.”

“Last warning.”

“You already used one,” he said. “That was it. Do I get a receipt?”

Renji moved.

He did not waste motion. Shiki drove his first cut along a line that would stop at the coat, not the throat—Hero doctrine: disable, then question. Lightning Rei sharpened the edge’s intent. The air cracked with the nearness of it.

Akihiko fell.

Not struck—or maybe struck by nothing but his own feet. Heels slipping on ash, body folding into a sprawl so complete it looked accidental. Renji’s blade passed through empty air. Follow-through carried him a half-step past. Renji checked, pivoted, cut again for the wrist that would bind—

—and found his own sheath swinging up into his path. Akihiko had rolled through the fall into a sit with Renji’s empty sheath in both hands. When had he taken it? During the slip. During the laugh. During the half-second Renji’s eyes had tracked steel instead of fingers.

Wood met steel with a stupid, solid _clack_.

Renji twisted, freed the blade, kicked for the sheath—

Akihiko let go.

The sheath clattered. Renji’s kick hit nothing—or hit the place the man had been a blink ago. For one exposed heartbeat Renji’s weight was committed wrong, and a finger—just a finger—tapped the flat of Renji’s katana as if testing a pot for heat.

“Hot,” he observed. “You care a lot.”

Renji’s next strike came harder. Lightning flared. The cut would have split a training post and the post behind it.

Akihiko stepped into Renji’s guard.

Closer—not faster that Renji could swear to. Into the space where a long blade became an awkward stick. His forehead nearly bumped Renji’s chin. Golden eyes filled Renji’s vision, amused.

“Ow,” he said, though nothing had touched him. “You’re very stabby for someone who wants answers.”

Renji drove a gauntleted fist toward his ribs—close-range, Shiki-backed, honest.

Akihiko laughed.

Renji’s fist met coat—or ash—or empty air. No blur. No afterimage he could name. Only the sick certainty that either he had missed a target at arm’s length, or the target had never intended to be where Renji’s eyes said he was. Renji’s own momentum turned him toward the trough where the rescued woman still sat. He stopped the strike before it became a crime against the innocent. The stop cost him a grunt. Cost him balance.

Later, if anyone asked, he would not know which account to give.

When he turned again, Akihiko stood five paces off, holding Renji’s dropped sheath like a conductor’s baton. He offered it forward with both hands.

“You dropped this,” he said. “Heroes should keep their things. The world is full of thieves.”

Renji took the sheath. His fingers brushed Akihiko’s. Warm. Human. No demonic cold. No holy burn. Just skin and ash.

“What are you?” Renji asked.

Akihiko tipped his head; the painted smile tipped with him. “Hungry, mostly. Also handsome. The second one’s disputed. Hard to verify.”

He dug in his coat and held up an empty paper wrapper between two fingers. There was nothing left inside but crumbs and a smear of something sticky.

“I only packed one snack,” he said. “You arrived before I could steal another. Rude.”

“Why save them?”

“The boy was loud,” he said. “Loud children are hard to ignore. Like bells.” He flicked his own. “Also, roofs that want to quit are rude. I hate rude architecture.”

“And the village?” Renji’s voice tightened. “Who attacked Ashvale?”

Akihiko’s gaze drifted to the leaning gate and the road beyond, where the sky was going gray.

“Dunno,” he said. “Someone with bad manners. Or good manners and a worse hobby. People get weird about doors.”

“Speak plainly.”

“I am speaking plainly,” Akihiko said. “You’re just listening in Hero.”

Renji sheathed his sword. The click of steel home sounded like a decision he had not finished making. Containment required a living subject. Escalation without cause violated Hoshikawa’s line. Cause stood all around them in charcoal and silence, and still Renji could not point to this man’s hand and say _there_.

“You call yourself The Fool,” Renji said.

“Because everyone else already decided what everyone was supposed to be,” Akihiko replied, light as gossip. Then, brighter: “Hero. Villain. Victim. Homework.”

“And you?”

“I hate assignments.” He wrinkled his nose. “Especially the ones written in blood. Terrible handwriting. Very dramatic. Nobody thinks about the cleanup.”

Renji stared at him. “You will come with me to the capital. Willingly, or bound.”

“Bound is a strong look on some people,” Akihiko said. “On me it just looks like fashion. Also itchy.”

He walked past Renji.

Not fleeing. Not charging. Walking toward the ruined gate as if the conversation had reached its natural end. The bell chimed with each step. Renji’s hand returned to his hilt.

“Stop.”

Akihiko stopped under the leaning gate-arch. He put a palm to the scarred wood. For a moment he looked almost solemn.

Then: “Ah.”

He looked at the gate the way a cook looks at a pot left on the fire too long.

“Still closed.”

Renji’s brow furrowed. “What?”

“That’s rude,” Akihiko said.

Rei moved.

Renji felt it—not lightning, not Shiki in the body, not Kotodama in a spoken seal, not Kage twisting what the eye trusted. None of the three disciplines Heroes were taught to name. Wrong in a way he had no word for yet.

The gate did not shatter.

Timber and hinges simply stopped doing their job. Dust lifted. The arch settled into the lane as rubble—no crater, no clean break-lines. For a breath it looked like Ashvale had never owned a gate at all.

Survivors cried out—not from flying spars, but from a landmark vanishing while their eyes were open. Renji’s Rei flared on instinct and found nothing to catch. His blue veil closed on empty air.

When the dust thinned, the road lay open.

Akihiko stood in the middle of what had been an arch, coat gray with powder, smiling like a man who had fixed a stuck latch.

Renji’s voice came out low. “Why?”

He looked at the place where the gate had been—and could not, for a moment, remember how high the hinges had hung.

A criminal had done this.

A monster had saved the survivors.

A madman had noticed what every soldier would have missed—the beam that was already dead, the cellar mouths, the quiet that mattered more than the fire.

And Renji could not decide which description was wrong.

Akihiko turned.

For the first time since Renji had entered the square, the laughter was gone from everything except the eyes.

“You’re a hero, right?” The Fool asked.

Renji did not lower his guard. Ash still drifted between them. Behind him, survivors waited by the trough. Ahead of him, Akihiko waited as if the answer mattered more than the sword—as if he had been waiting for a Hero specifically, and Renji’s arrival was confirmation.

“I am Sir Renji Arata,” Renji said. “Hero of Akatsuki.”

Behind the mask, something eased—the eyes, not the porcelain.

“Good,” he said.

He stepped through the open road, close enough that Renji could have ended him in one honest cut, and did not look at the blade at all.

“I was worried nobody would come.”
