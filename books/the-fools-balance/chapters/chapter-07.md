# Chapter 7 — The Fool's Crime

The proclamation reached Hero Command before breakfast.

Renji heard boots in the corridor, then paper slapped onto the annex door by a clerk who would not meet his eyes. The seal was Imperial black. The ink was still wet.

Sir Renji Arata broke it with a thumb and read.

*By decree of the Imperial Court: the unclassified entity known as The Fool, also answering to Harlequin Akihiko, is named responsible for the destruction of the river city of Homura. Casualties: uncounted. Property: total. Motive: chaos. All citizens are ordered to treat sightings as hostile. Hero Command and Holy Dominion are authorized to escalate containment.*

Escalate.

After Kuro had postponed an execution.

Lightning stirred under Renji’s skin. He forced it down.

Behind him, in the holding room, a silver bell chimed once—soft, stupid, awake.

“Is that breakfast?” Akihiko asked through the door.

Renji folded the proclamation once, clean. “Stay seated.”

“I am seated.”

Renji opened the door.

Akihiko sat on the bench in the same cracked mask, same patched coat, soft Kotodama seals still sticky-light around his wrists. The painted smile faced Renji as if proclamations were someone else’s errand.

“Homura,” Renji said.

Akihiko’s fingers paused on the bell.

Not long. Long enough.

“River city,” Akihiko said.

“You destroyed it.”

“The paper says so.”

Renji stepped in and shut the door. The corridor outside was already loud with Command aides, a Church runner, and someone asking for Kuro.

“Did you,” Renji asked.

Akihiko considered the question the way he considered lunch: seriously, sideways, late.

“I stopped something,” he said. “Stopping is loud. Loud looks like destroying if you arrive after the song ends.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It’s the only one that doesn’t invent a cleaner story,” Akihiko said, and for once there was no bun joke attached.

Renji hated how much that sounded like last night’s interrogation.

He hated more that Kuro would use Homura to restart an execution.

---

Princess Ayame found him in the corridor before he reached the yard.

No kimono armor this morning—dark traveling cut, paper already awake at her sleeve like a second pulse. She held a second copy of the proclamation as if it might bite.

“You saw it,” she said.

“I saw it.”

“Homura fell three months ago,” Ayame said, quiet enough that the aides nearby would hear only tone. “The court called it a demon raid then. Incomplete reports. Sealed survivors. Now it is suddenly a Fool crime with your prisoner’s face on it.”

Renji’s jaw worked. “Who changed the story.”

“Someone who needs the postponement to look like negligence.” Ayame’s eyes flicked toward the east wing—Church lease, judgment chamber, Kuro’s clean robes. “I can’t prove that in a hallway. I can give you a door.”

“What door.”

“Refugee row. South canal. Homura survivors were parked there and told to be grateful and quiet. If your prisoner burned a city for fun, they will spit his name. If he didn’t—” She stopped. Paper shivered once against her wrist. “Then the court just lied in public, and I become the one holding the lie.”

Renji did not ask what she would do with it.

“Kuro is watching,” Renji said.

“So is Shigure,” Ayame said. “So take the Fool with you. Sealed. Visible. If you leave him here, the Church will invent an escape before noon.”

Akihiko called from the holding room:

“I heard you. I will come.”

Renji closed his eyes once. Opened them.

“Ten minutes,” he told Ayame. “If Mori’s watch tries to stop us—”

“I’ll invent a princess reason,” Ayame said. “I’m good at those.”

---

They took a covered carriage through streets where people had already heard the proclamation.

Vendors shouted less. Patrols shouted more. A boy on a corner held up a charcoal sketch of a cracked white mask with the word *MONSTER* under it. Renji did not look at Akihiko when they passed.

Akihiko looked anyway.

“The drawing is wrong,” he said.

“Quiet.”

Akihiko went quiet.

Renji kept the seals checked. Soft light. Holding. The Fool could break them. He had said so under Judgment Rei with no lie in the taste. He still didn’t. Renji held both truths at once: *not safe to trust* and *not safe to ignore*.

South canal smelled of wet rope and old fish. Refugee row was a line of low sheds. Cooking fires smoked between them. Laundry hung from ropes and stayed damp in the shade.

Captain Mori waited at the first shed with his jaw like a closed gate and two watchmen who looked unhappy to be near Church business.

“Arata,” Mori said. “You brought the festival act.”

“I brought a witness,” Renji said.

“You brought a spark into dry straw.” Mori’s gaze snagged on the seals, then on the mask. “Homura people don’t need theater. They need rice and a story that doesn’t change every week.”

“Then help me get the first story,” Renji said. “The one from before the ink got brave.”

Mori snorted. “Ayame already leaned on me. Fine. Old Den saw the river burn. He doesn’t like Heroes. He likes less being told what he saw.”

He jerked his chin at the third shed.

Renji went.

Akihiko followed, seals whispering and bell marking his steps. Children peeked from doorways and vanished. One girl froze. Akihiko lifted his sealed wrists in a small wave.

She did not wave back. She also did not run screaming. Renji put that in the same unstable column as the seals.

---

Old Den sat on a crate with a blanket over his knees and a stare that had outlived manners.

Burn scars climbed the left side of his neck into his hair. His hands shook only when he noticed Renji noticing.

“Hero,” Den said. Not a greeting. A category.

“Sir Renji Arata. I need your account of Homura.”

“Which one.” Den’s mouth twisted. “The raid story, or this morning’s clown story.”

Behind Renji, Akihiko sat on the shed threshold without being invited, mask tipped toward the canal light.

Den’s eyes found him and stopped.

Not spit. Not scream.

Recognition with nowhere polite to land.

“You,” Den said.

Akihiko lifted two sealed fingers. “Me.”

Den laughed once—ugly, wet—and coughed smoke that wasn’t there anymore.

“He came at the end,” Den told Renji, never looking away from the mask. “Not at the start. At the start Homura was… fine. Too fine. Markets open. Temples full. Everyone smiling like the smile had been nailed on.”

Renji’s lightning cooled into attention. “Possession.”

“Worse,” Den said. “Agreement. People started volunteering for the river rites. Kids first. Priests said it was blessing. The ones who refused woke up smiling anyway.” His hands clenched in the blanket. “Demons don’t always kick the door. Sometimes they rent the house and pay in calm.”

“And him.”

“He walked in laughing,” Den said. “We thought he was one more wrong smile. Then he told the mayor to stop conducting. Mayor’s mouth kept moving. No sound came out. The air went thin. The river… changed color. Not water color. Meaning color. You understand?”

“No,” Renji said.

“Good,” Den said. “Means you’re still sane.” He swallowed. “The city broke. Bridges. Halls. The smiling people fell down like puppets with cut strings. Half of Homura burned because the thing inside it didn’t want to leave polite. The other half lived because the thing left.”

Renji’s throat felt tight. “How many lived.”

“Thousands,” Den said. Immediate. Bitter. “Including me. Including the girl who won’t wave at him now because she doesn’t know if thank-you is allowed for a man the court just named monster.”

Nobody spoke for a moment.

Akihiko picked at a loose thread on his coat. “The eel stall survived. I checked.”

Den’s eyes shone wet and angry. “Don’t joke.”

“I’m not,” Akihiko said. “I am not asking for anything.”

Renji turned on him. “Why didn’t you say this.”

“I did,” Akihiko said. “Stopping is loud. You wanted a cleaner verb.”

Outside, boots.

Not Mori’s. Too synchronized. Church rhythm.

Shigure arrived first, quiet as usual. Kuro’s white robes followed.

Refugees pulled children indoors. Mori swore once, low.

Kuro stopped at the shed mouth. Cloth over eyes. Black gloves. Judgment Rei already tasting the row.

“Hero Arata,” Kuro said. “You took the anomaly into a crowd after a city-killing decree. That is either stupidity or collaboration.”

“It’s investigation,” Renji said.

“Investigation is what people call disobedience when they still want a salary.” Kuro turned toward Den. “Old man. Speak the Fool’s crime.”

Den’s jaw set. “He saved us.”

“He destroyed Homura.”

“Homura was already gone,” Den snapped. “We were furniture that hadn’t noticed yet.”

Judgment Rei pressed—Renji felt it as courtroom pressure in his teeth. Den’s scars flared with remembered heat. Akihiko’s seals brightened, warning-hot.

Akihiko did not stand. He did not run. He said, flat:

“Do not make a story here,” Akihiko said. “Leave the refugees out of it.”

Shigure stepped in.

Renji’s sword cleared the sheath fully this time—lightning threading blue along the steel, bright enough to white the shed walls.

“Not here,” Renji said. “Not them.”

Shigure drew his blade. Kage blurred its edge, so Renji tracked Shigure’s wrist instead.

Kuro lifted two fingers.

Shigure held. Barely.

“Your postponement,” Renji said to Kuro, voice low, “does not get to become a lie that eats survivors. Homura was demon-held. He broke the hold. The city burned because the hold fought back. That is not the same crime your paper printed.”

“It is a better sermon for saints,” Kuro said. “Saints die. Order lasts. The capital needs a monster it can point at while I decide what he is.”

Ayame’s voice cut from behind Kuro—she had come after all, paper crane perched on her shoulder like a spy:

“Then print *demon* on your decree and leave my refugees alone.”

Kuro did not turn. “Princess. Still collecting truths you cannot afford.”

“Still refusing executions you cannot categorize,” Ayame returned.

The air changed.

It was not Shigure. Something under the shed floor carried a sweet, spoiled smell mixed with canal damp. Renji’s lightning reacted a moment before the crate under Den cracked.

Den’s eyes rolled white.

His mouth smiled with too many corners.

“Homura,” said a voice that was not Den’s. “Still unfinished.”

The thing wearing Den lunged past Kuro toward the children two doors down.

Renji moved.

Blade first. Body second. Lightning along the cut—not to kill the man, to sever the string. The smile-thing met him with a forearm that bent wrong and a strength that belonged to river bottoms. Renji’s boots tore a groove in packed dirt. He dropped his weight, twisted, drove the flat into Den’s ribs hard enough to empty air, then spoke a short Kotodama edge he hated using on civilians:

“Sleep.”

The word caught—then slipped. Demon agreement laughed under it.

Akihiko was suddenly there without looking like he’d decided to be. Sealed wrists up. Bell chiming once against Den’s temple like an idiot accident.

The smile stuttered.

“Leave his face,” Akihiko told the thing inside.

Renji did not ask. He cut the opening Akihiko’s stupid bell made—lightning into the possession’s seam, Shiki bracing his arms against backlash. The sweet-rot tore. Den collapsed forward. Renji caught him before his scarred neck hit the crate edge.

Shigure had not moved to help the children.

He had moved to stand between the Fool and the exit.

Kuro’s Judgment Rei flooded the shed—confession pressure slamming every chest.

Den gasped awake, human, sobbing once.

The demon-smell died into canal fish and fear-sweat.

Renji kept his sword up anyway, tip angling not at Den—at the space where Church steel waited.

“You felt that,” Renji said to Kuro. Quiet. For the cloth. For the doctrine. “Homura isn’t finished because a clown likes fire. It’s unfinished because something still wants the smiles back. He is not your category error. Your paper is.”

Kuro stood very still.

Kuro stood very still. He had seen Akihiko feed children the day before; now he had seen the demon seam with his own Rei.

“Take the anomaly back to Command,” Kuro said at last. “Alive. For now. Captain Mori will quarantine this row. Princess—your copies will include my correction request to the court.”

“Correction,” Ayame said.

“Addendum,” Kuro amended, and the word sounded like a tooth cracking. “The decree stands until the Emperor unwrites it. I will not publicly call your Hero a liar. I will also not pretend I smelled nothing.”

He turned and left.

Shigure sheathed silence and followed.

Only when they were gone did Akihiko sit down hard on the threshold, seals flaring as if the almost-fight had billed him.

“The seals hurt,” he said.

Den gripped Renji’s sleeve with shaking fingers. “Don’t let them make him the fire,” he whispered. “We were the fire. He was the bucket. Buckets look empty. That’s how they work.”

Renji nodded once. He could not promise courts. He could promise work.

---

They rode back under a sky that had not changed and a city that had.

The charcoal *MONSTER* sketch had already been copied. Someone had added horns. Someone else had drawn a halo, then scratched it out.

In the carriage, Renji watched Akihiko watch the window.

“You could have told me Homura plainly,” Renji said.

“I did,” Akihiko said. “You were busy translating me into crimes.”

“I’m a Hero. Translation is the job.”

“Then translate this.” Akihiko’s golden eyes sat steady in the mask holes. “If you need me guilty to keep your order standing, say so early. I’ll practice looking guilty. I’m bad at homework, but—”

He stopped. Flicked the bell once, small.

“—but I show up,” he finished, flatter, as if retreating from a line that had gotten too close to true.

Renji’s hands tightened on his knees.

The Church had called a rescue a massacre, and something from Homura was still under refugee row.

He looked at the sealed wrists. At the man who could break them and didn’t. At the city outside inventing certainty in charcoal.

“I’m not going to make you look guilty,” Renji said. “I’m going to make the report ugly enough that lying takes work.”

Akihiko’s painted smile did not change.

“Good,” Akihiko said.

The carriage wheels hit a stone. The bell answered.

Renji did not smile.

He was already writing the first line in his head:

*Homura was not destroyed by The Fool. The Fool arrived when Homura had already been sold.*

If he filed it, Kuro would not be the only one who accused him of collaboration.

Outside, a herald began shouting the decree again for a fresh street.

Renji made a decision.

He would not let the cleaner story win today.

The Fool leaned his head against the carriage wall and, as if he hadn’t just helped cut a demon out of an old man’s face, asked:

“Will there be lunch?”
