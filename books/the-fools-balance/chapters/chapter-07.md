# Chapter 7 — Sera’s Rest

They stopped short of the first houses while there was still light enough to see faces.

The medic set a small fire off the road, behind a stand of scrub that cut the wind. Wataru unsaddled without being asked. Akihiko sat on a fallen rail with his sealed wrists in his lap and watched a sparrow fight another sparrow over a twig. The twig broke. Both birds left.

Renji checked the strip in his belt again. Sera’s Rest. No dispute filings. Headman smiling through a broken arm. He folded the paper and put it away.

“Stew,” the medic said. “What’s left of the travel pot. Salt’s in the pouch.”

“I’ll cook,” Wataru said.

“Fine,” Renji said.

Akihiko tipped his mask toward the pot. “I can watch it burn.”

“You will not,” Renji said.

Nobody argued about who cooked. Nobody argued about how much water. Nobody argued about whether the fire was too high. Wataru moved through the steps in a clean order. The medic passed him the salt pouch without looking up. Renji waited for someone to complain about the smoke, the place, the hour. Nobody did.

That was the first wrong note.

The medic sat with his kit open and rolled a clean wrap for no one in particular. “Yard yesterday,” he said to Wataru, quiet. “You kept the clerks moving. That’s the part I’m writing down, if anyone asks.”

Wataru’s ears went red under the hairline wrap. “I got thrown into a post.”

“You got up,” the medic said. “Twice.”

Akihiko pointed at Wataru with both sealed hands. “Points for twice. Posts are rude. I respect your disagreement with the post.”

When the stew was almost ready, Renji took the pouch from Wataru’s hand.

“Too much salt,” Renji said.

Wataru blinked. “I haven’t put any in yet.”

“Then too little,” Renji said. “Put more. No—less. Decide.”

Wataru looked at the pouch, then at Renji. “…Sir?”

“Pick,” Renji said. “Out loud. Argue with me if I’m wrong.”

Wataru’s mouth opened. Closed. The wrap at his hairline had dried dark. “More,” he said at last. “The dried meat is bland. It needs more.”

“Wrong,” Renji said. “Less. The dried meat is already salted.”

Wataru’s shoulders set. “Then why ask me to cook if you’ve already decided?”

“Because I needed you to push back,” Renji said. He handed the pouch over. “Do it your way. Keep doing that.”

Akihiko clapped once, soft, seals chiming. “Salt fight. Excellent. Points for both of you. Mostly for the scowling.”

The medic snorted into his sleeve and said nothing more helpful than that.

They ate. The stew was ordinary. Renji made himself taste the salt and dislike it a little on purpose. Wataru ate without looking at the village lights through the scrub. Akihiko refused a second portion and asked if anyone had saved bread crusts. Nobody had. He licked the spoon anyway.

When the fire was kicked out, Renji stood.

“We walk the last stretch,” he said. “Horses at the edge. Eyes open. If anyone finishes your sentence, you stop talking and you look at me.”

Wataru nodded too fast. The medic checked his kit straps twice. Akihiko stood, brushed dirt from his coat that did not come off, and flicked the silver bell once.

The village did not answer.

---

Sera’s Rest looked like a farming hamlet that had finished its week early.

The hedges ran even. Too even. The lane between the first houses had been swept. Tools leaned against walls in straight lines. A dog walked the same three posts, turned, and walked them again without sniffing. Chickens stayed in a neat cluster near a trough and did not fight over grain.

Wataru’s hand went to his pen, then away from it. “Sir. The tax clerk wasn’t lying.”

“Write later,” Renji said. “Watch now.”

Smoke rose from the bakehouse chimney in a thin, steady column. The door stood open. Heat came out onto the lane. Inside, three ovens glowed. Loaves sat on the cooling racks in perfect rows. The crusts were brown and even. Nobody stood at the peels. A woman sat on a stool by the wall with her hands empty in her lap and smiled—relief, not welcome. Renji had seen that smile on Sato in the records bay.

Akihiko stopped in the doorway.

He looked at the flour on the floor first. A single drag mark from a broom that had stopped halfway. Then he looked at the loaves.

“…They’re done,” he said. “Who watched them?”

The woman turned her head. “We don’t need to. They don’t burn here.”

“Since when?” Renji asked.

“We decided,” she said, easy. “A while ago. Or yesterday. It’s the same.”

Wataru stepped half a pace closer to Renji.

Akihiko walked to the nearest rack and tipped his mask toward a loaf. He did not touch it. “Salt?”

The woman blinked. “In the dough. Of course.”

“How much?” Akihiko asked.

She smiled wider. “Enough. We don’t argue about it anymore.”

Akihiko made a small sound. Not a laugh. He stepped back into the lane. Renji stayed long enough to open an oven door with the butt of a peel. Heat rolled out. The loaf on the stone was already brown and even. No one had turned it. He shut the door.

Further in, a man sat on a porch with his left arm bound wrong—splints too neat, cloth too clean for a farm break that should have made him swear. Village head, if the tax strip was right. He raised his good hand when he saw Renji’s crest.

“Heroes,” he said. Warm. Finished. “We hoped someone would come. Or we hoped no one would. We decided either was fine.”

“Your arm,” Renji said.

“It doesn’t trouble me.” The headman answered the next question before Renji asked it. “No bandits. No tax quarrel. Harvest is in. Fields are clean. Animals are calm. You can rest. There’s nothing you need to fix.”

“I didn’t ask about bandits yet,” Renji said.

“You were going to,” the headman said, still smiling. “Everyone does.”

Behind him, a bucket by the well stood full. Renji watched a boy tip it into a trough, set the empty bucket back on the stone, and walk away without cranking the rope. Water rose in the bucket again while Renji counted to five. No one at the crank. No splash from below that matched the rise. The boy did not look back.

Wataru saw it too. His voice went thin. “Sir—”

“I see it,” Renji said.

Akihiko had drifted to a fence post where a strap hung half-mended, needle still in the leather. He poked the needle with one sealed finger.

“Hello,” he told the strap. “You were in the middle of something.”

Nobody claimed the work. A woman passed with an empty basket and did not stop. In the field beyond the fence, weeds lay in small piles between the rows. There were no hands left to finish the job. The piles were already neat.

Near the porch, a notice board held last month’s market days and a blank space where dispute strips should have been pinned. The nails were clean. Renji touched one. No rust. No torn corner left behind.

“When did you stop filing?” he asked the headman.

“When filing became tiring,” the headman said. “We decided the harvest spoke for itself.”

Past the well, the harvest sat in stacked sheaves along a barn wall—straight rows, rope ties even, no loose straw underfoot. Renji counted twelve stacks. No one worked the yard. A sickle hung on a peg with the blade still clean.

“Who brought it in?” Renji asked.

“We did,” the headman said. “Or it was brought. The difference stopped mattering.”

Wataru wrote *difference stopped mattering* and underlined it once, hard enough to tear the strip a little.

The medic stayed by the horses at Renji’s order. Renji took Wataru and Akihiko farther in.

They found a family at a table with plates set and no steam. The food was cold and untouched. Four people sat with their hands folded. One child swung her feet, then stopped.

“Evening,” Renji said.

“Stay,” the father said. “We’ve made space.”

“We decided,” the mother added. Soft. Kind in the wrong way.

Wataru’s pen was in his fingers now. He did not write. “Where is the argument?” he asked, mostly to Renji. “Farms always have an argument. Whose turn at the well. Whose goat ate whose greens.”

The father nodded, helpful. “We don’t need those anymore.”

Akihiko crouched so his mask was level with the child. “Do you still like salty soup?”

The girl looked at her mother. Her mother looked at her father. The father’s smile did not change.

“…We like what everyone likes,” the girl said.

Akihiko’s bell gave a small chime. He stood. “Boring menu,” he said to Renji. “I want a complaint. Even a small one. About socks. Socks are honest.”

Nobody offered a sock complaint.

Renji moved them on before the family could start finishing each other’s kindness. In the lane a neighbor lifted a hand toward Wataru.

“You look tired. You don’t have to—”

“Stop,” Renji said.

The neighbor smiled, patient, and let the sentence die unfinished. Wataru’s mouth had already shaped the next word. He shut it and looked at Renji, pale under the wrap.

“I almost—” Wataru began.

“I saw,” Renji said. “That’s why you’re here. To notice when you almost do.”

At the grain shed, a man began, “You should—” and Renji cut in with a question about store counts. The man answered alone, slow, unused to carrying a whole sentence. Twice more Renji heard “We decided.” He let the words stand and did not let them become a chain.

Behind the shed, laundry hung dry and even on a line. No wind. No clothespins missing. A shirt had a tear at the cuff that nobody had bothered to mend. Akihiko stopped there longer than he stopped at the neat hedges.

“This one tried,” he said. He flicked the torn cuff. “I like this shirt.”

Wataru wrote that down, then crossed it out, then wrote it again smaller in the margin.

Renji sent Wataru to ask three separate people what day it was. He got three calm answers that did not match—market eve, rest day, harvest end—and none of the speakers cared about the mismatch. Renji put the mismatch on the strip himself.

At the far end of the lane, past the last house, a cart track ran west into darker trees. Wheel ruts fresh. One set of footprints in the soft mud beside them—leaving, not arriving. A woman’s shoe, by the size. No second pair.

Wataru crouched. “Someone walked out today.”

“Or last night,” Renji said. “After the clerk left.”

Akihiko stood in the ruts and tipped his head toward the trees, then back toward the bakehouse smoke.

“She took bread,” he said.

Renji looked at him.

Akihiko pointed at a crumb on a fence rail near the track, then at a second crumb farther on, small in the dust. “Or she meant to. Or she dropped it. Dropping means the loaf had weight.”

“Is that a report?” Renji asked.

“It’s crumbs,” Akihiko said. “I like crumbs.”

Renji followed the track twenty paces. The footprints stayed alone. No drag. No second walker. Same shape as Riku leaving Hero Command—one person, unhurried, carrying ease out on two feet.

He came back. The headman had followed them without hurry, broken arm held careful and painless.

“Don’t worry about the road,” the headman said. “Whoever left will be fine. We decided the harvest was enough. Outside is unnecessary.”

“Who left?” Renji asked.

The headman’s smile held. “A neighbor. Or a sister. Names are heavy. We put that down.”

Wataru’s jaw tightened. “Sir. We can’t leave them like this.”

“We also can’t cut a smile out of a village with a sword,” Renji said. He looked at Akihiko. “Soft seals stay on. You look. You do not escalate. If you need the cord loosened, you ask me first.”

Akihiko wiggled his fingers. “Decorative. I remember.”

He walked back toward the bakehouse without waiting for permission. Renji and Wataru followed. The woman on the stool had not moved. The loaves had not burned. The peels still leaned unused against the wall.

Akihiko stopped at the flour drag mark again. He crouched and drew a short crooked line in the flour with his knuckle, messy on purpose. Then he waited.

The woman watched. Her smile flickered.

“…That’s untidy,” she said.

“Yes,” Akihiko said. “Fix it. Or leave it. Pick. Out loud.”

She stared at the crooked line. Her hands stayed in her lap a moment too long. Then she stood, took the broom from the corner, and swept the mark away in three hard strokes. Dust lifted. She coughed. For one breath she looked annoyed at the broom, at him, at the work.

Then the smile came back.

“We decided it should be clean,” she said.

“You swept first,” Akihiko said. Bright. “I like brooms.”

Renji did not call it a cure. He called it a note.

Outside, evening had gone flat. The dog still walked its three posts. The bucket at the well was full again. On the west track, the crumbs were too small to follow in failing light.

“We hold at the edge tonight,” Renji said. “Watches. No one eats their bread. No one drinks from that well. Wataru—strip for Ayame’s bird: ovens, bucket, headman, footprints west. What you saw. Not what you hope it means.”

Wataru’s pen was already moving. “Yes, sir.”

Akihiko looked once more at the bakehouse door, then at the dark road.

“Do they still argue about salt in the next place?” he asked.

Nobody knew.

“Find out,” Renji said.

They walked back to the horses. Behind them, Sera’s Rest kept its neat lanes and its even hedges and its bread that would not burn. The headman waved with his good hand. The wave needed nothing back.

At the scrub where the medic had rebuilt a smaller fire, Renji sat with his sword across his knees and watched the village lights. Too steady. Too few. The medic handed Wataru water from their own skin and made him drink before he wrote another line. Wataru wrote until his wrap darkened again with sweat. Akihiko lay on his side facing the west track, sealed hands tucked under his cheek.

Ayame’s folded paper bird sat in Renji’s belt. He did not send it yet. The strip needed the west footprints and the day mismatch first. When Wataru finished, Renji read it once, cut two guesses, and kept every concrete line.

“Wake me if somebody complains,” Akihiko said. “About anything. Socks. Melons. Elbows.”

“Sleep,” Renji said.

“Cruel,” Akihiko said. “I accept.”

Second watch would be Wataru’s. Third the medic’s. Renji took first and did not pretend otherwise. He listened for disagreement from the houses and heard none. He listened for the well crank and heard none. Once, far west under the trees, something small moved—footsteps, or a branch.

He kept his eyes on that dark.
