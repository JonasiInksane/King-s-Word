# Chapter 10 — If the Grove Goes Silent

Morning left the cavern path behind them: ordinary birds, ordinary wind, the pale circle of bleach still riding the top of Niko’s scrap roll where his thumb could feel it. They climbed toward ridge nails and the promise of walls.

The ridge path gave them old survey nails and a view that made Lumi grab Niko’s sleeve.

Collapsed Fortress hunched on the height—walls bitten by time, courtyard open to sky. Below it, in a hollow of darker green, the trees stood too still.

“Silent Grove,” Marta said. Not a tour voice. A weather voice. “We don’t go in. We skirt. If it goes quiet where we are, we leave.”

“It’s already—” Lumi began.

“Listen,” Marta said.

Niko listened.

Birds on the ridge. Wind in dry grass. Far water. Ordinary.

In the hollow: nothing. Not hush. Not peace. A removed sound, as if someone had lifted the world’s throat and set it aside.

Bram’s hand found the pot strap and stayed there.

“I don’t like rooms without noise,” he said.

“It isn’t a room,” Lumi whispered.

“Worse.”

Marta checked the pilgrim ribbons on the skirt path—pale, honest, still pointing around rather than through. Niko checked his chalk scraps without unrolling them. Soft roots. Bleach bowl. Shore that walked. He did not stack them into a speech. He stacked them into boots.

“Fortress before dark,” Marta said. “We sleep behind walls. No clever detours.”

“I wasn’t going to detour into silent,” Lumi said.

“Good. Keep that opinion.”

Bram offered everyone a strip of dried fish like a blessing before weather. Marta took hers. Lumi took hers and almost dropped it. Niko chewed and tasted lake and salt and the memory of a shore that wouldn’t sit still.

They rested once on a flat rock where an old nail still held a scrap of faded cloth. Lumi read the cloth’s remaining letters and got as far as *lea—* before giving up. Marta drank. Bram checked the pot strap as if it might resign.

“If it goes quiet,” Niko said, “we leave. Not measure.”

“You say that now,” Marta said.

“I’m practicing.”

“Good. Practice out loud. Quiet people get clever.”

---

They took the skirt path—narrow, marked, pressed between ridge stone and the hollow’s shoulder. Halfway along, the ordinary noise thinned.

Not all at once. First the birds stopped. Then the insects. Then the wind seemed to move leaves without rubbing them together. Niko heard his own swallow. Heard Lumi’s crate creak. Heard Bram’s kettle try one soft complaint and fail.

Marta raised a fist.

Stop.

The parting began ahead of them on the skirt path itself.

Young trees leaned aside in a slow wave, trunks unbroken, making a corridor wide enough for something that was not them. Deer broke from the undergrowth in a panic glitter of antlers and vanished uphill. Behind the deer, shapes of glass-wolf gray flickered and held back—hungry, hesitant, waiting on a larger permission.

Niko’s mouth went dry.

“Marta.”

“I see it.”

Through the parting, something walked without hurry.

Niko saw antlers first—not bone alone, but branches living on bone, leaves still on them, as if the forest had loaned the animal a crown and forgotten to ask for it back. The body under it was deer and not. Huge. Quiet. Each step, more young wood bowed and left a path, then eased upright behind like a held breath released.

Hollow Stag.

The name arrived in Niko’s head without anyone saying it. Lumi’s textbook had a silhouette. Reality had weight.

Bram made a sound that was not a joke.

Marta’s voice stayed low.

“Do not run toward it. Do not follow the corridor. Back along our own prints. Slow.”

A wolf edged closer to the skirt path, ribs sharp, eyes on the fleeing deer line—and on them, soft humans with food smells. The Stag’s passage pressed the wolf’s choices into a narrower chute. Pressure without malice. Ecology with teeth.

“If we bolt, we look like deer,” Marta said. “If we freeze in its road, we get stepped through.”

Niko watched the leans. The Stag was not hunting them. It was leaving. The forest was helping it leave. Wolves were being squeezed out of an old territory and into new mistakes.

“Side,” Niko whispered. “The ribbon path. Not the parting. The parting is its door.”

Marta’s eyes flicked to him, then to the pale pilgrim ribbon on a trunk to their left—still their path, half swallowed by leans but not the Stag’s corridor.

“Yes. That.”

They moved.

Lumi’s boot slipped. Bram caught her crate, not her arm, and hauled both. Marta put herself on the wolf side. Niko counted leans the way he counted chalk marks.

The Stag’s head turned once.

For a heartbeat Niko saw an eye that was animal and old and uninterested in Guild years. Then the crown of branches passed, and more trees bowed, and the pressure of wolves shifted like water around a hull.

One wolf came too close.

Marta did not fight it for pride. She cracked a flare stick from her pack—Root Camp issue, mean red light and worse smell—and threw it not at the wolf but between wolf and party, onto stone. The wolf flinched from the chemical insult. Bram banged the pot once, loud and stupid and perfect. The wolf chose deer memory over pot-and-flare confusion and peeled away.

They reached a rib of open rock above the hollow. Sound rushed back—wind, a jay, Lumi’s ragged breath—as if the grove had spit them into the world again.

Below, the Silent Grove stayed silent.

The parting corridor closed behind the Stag’s passage, trees easing upright, the forest pretending it had always stood that way.

---

Niko’s hands shook when he chalked. He made himself write small: *Stag — forest parts — wolves pressed — skirt path / don’t follow corridor — flare + pot — alive.*

Lumi laughed once, ugly with relief, and wiped her face.

“Academy silhouette,” she said. “Didn’t say it would rewrite the trees.”

“Books miss things,” Marta said. She sat down hard on the rock, then stood again because sitting felt like inviting fate. “We don’t chase it. We don’t claim it. We go around to the fortress court before dark and we sleep behind walls.”

Bram stared at the pot as if it had graduated.

“I miss quiet forests that are quiet for normal reasons,” he said.

“Was that a normal Stag?” Lumi asked.

“No,” Bram said. “That’s why I miss the other kind.”

Niko looked at the hollow.

The Stag was already gone from view. The wolves were a problem moved, not solved. The bleached moss in the caverns made more sense. So did the ditch, in a sideways way he couldn’t prove—something in the basin’s habits had shifted, and the big animals were answering first.

He did not say they weren’t extinct. He didn’t know what *they* were yet.

He knew the forest had moved out of something’s way, and that his boots had survived by not pretending to be a shortcut.

“Flare’s gone,” Marta said, practical as breath. “One less loud friend. Pot still works.”

“Pot always works,” Bram said.

“Don’t test that sentence.”

Lumi hugged her crate once, briefly, as if apologizing to the books inside for the shaking.

“I want to write the eye,” she said. “I don’t have words that aren’t stupid.”

“Write stupid,” Niko said. “Stupid is honest.”

“That’s your survey method.”

“Sometimes.”

They shared the last of the dried fish on the open rock while their breathing slowed. Below, the hollow stayed emptied of sound. Above, the fortress teeth caught late light. Nobody made a toast. Nobody needed one.

Marta pointed up the rib toward the broken walls.

“Come on. Walls. Dark. Stew if Bram can forgive the day.”

“I can forgive stew,” Bram said. “The day can wait.”

They climbed toward the broken fortress, four people and one pot and a margin full of chalk that finally felt heavy enough to hang on a Wall someday—not as proof. As witness.

The path bent around a fallen wall-tooth and gave them a first look into the courtyard—open sky, weeds in old stone seams, a place that had stopped being a fort and started being shelter. Late light made the broken edges look softer than they were.

“Before dark,” Marta said again, as if repetition could stretch the sun.

“I’m going as fast as books allow,” Lumi said.

“Books can hurry.”

“They hate that.”

Bram shifted the pot higher.

“If we get walls, I make stew with actual weight again,” he said. “If we don’t, I make complaints with actual weight.”

“Both are edible,” Marta said.

Behind them, the grove kept its silence like a closed door.

Ahead, stone waited—ordinary for reasons walls could explain, or at least quieter than a forest that had learned how to move.
