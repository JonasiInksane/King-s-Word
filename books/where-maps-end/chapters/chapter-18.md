# Chapter 18 — Five Bellows, One Sky

The shaft went down further than the rope, which Marta had planned for, and then further than the second rope, which she had not.

They ended up doing it in stages off root brackets set into the wall at intervals — brackets, in a well, at a spacing that suited a person’s reach. Lumi noted this out loud twice. The second time nobody answered her, which she took as agreement.

Niko came off the last bracket into a room he could hear before he could see.

It breathed.

Not wind. Breath: an intake with a catch in it, a hold, a long soft push out that stirred the dust on his boots and moved on. Then quiet. Then again, slower than a sleeping person, faster than tide.

Bram got the lantern up.

The hall ran off into the dark on both sides, and along the far wall stood the bellows.

Door-sized. Bigger — barn-door-sized, ribbed with pale root and glass and old leather gone the colour of tea, hinged into the stone in a row like the pipes of something too big to be an instrument. Dozens of them. Most sagged open and still, leather split, ribs bare.

Five moved.

Five, spaced along the row without any pattern Niko could see, rising and falling on their own rhythm. Not together. Each on its own count, so that the hall never fully inhaled and never fully emptied — one of them was always giving.

“Lungs,” Lumi said.

“Lungs,” Bram agreed. He was already walking the row with the lantern up. “This one’s the loudest. He gets no salt.”

“Don’t name them.”

“Too late. That’s Grandmother.” He pointed the lantern at a big one near the middle, wheezing on a long slow count. “That’s Grandmother, that’s the one with the whistle, that’s the liar—”

“Why is that one the liar?”

“It sounds like it’s working harder than it is.”

Marta walked the row the other way with her hand on the wall and said nothing at all, and Niko realised she was counting exits and finding one, and that she would tell them about it later if it mattered.

He stood in the middle of the hall with the dust moving past his ankles and felt the thing arrive in his chest before it got to his mouth.

“The furniture,” he said.

The Jester was crouched at a dead bellows, poking the split leather with one finger.

“Mm?”

“You said the ground used to rearrange the furniture so the house could breathe.” Niko turned around, slowly, taking in the row. “There’s a house. There’s an actual house and it actually breathes and this is the part that does it.”

“That’s a very literal way to hear a joke,” the Jester said.

“Is it wrong?”

“It’s *literal.*” He stood up and brushed his hands. “Somebody made these, presumably. People make things. They can’t help it. Then they go off and die of something boring and the thing keeps going out of habit.” He looked down the row with his head tilted. “Five. Out of all that. Stubborn.”

Niko waited.

The Jester’s bells said something short, and he wandered off toward the whistling one.

Lumi came and stood at Niko’s shoulder.

“He didn’t say who,” she said.

“No.”

“I noticed him not saying it.”

“Me too.” Niko got his chalk out. “Don’t write it as anything. Write it as a room.”

---

The work took most of a day, or what they agreed to call a day.

The bellows fed a bank of stone valves at the hall’s end — wheels and gates, all of them seized, two of them cracked through. Air went in. Air went somewhere. Lumi got her hands on the one gate that still turned and could not make it turn far.

“It’s pressure,” she said, on her back under the housing. “It isn’t rust, it’s— every time Grandmother pushes, the gate wants to shut. I’m fighting a lung.”

“So stop fighting it,” Marta said.

“That is the *stupidest*—”

She fought the lung for another half hour.

Niko brought her water and stayed to hold the lantern, because holding the lantern was a job and he wanted to see. The Jester drifted over the way he drifted everywhere, and sat down on an upturned block, and watched Grandmother go up and down.

“Everything that breathes cheats on the out-breath, you know,” he said.

Lumi did not look out from under the housing.

“What does that mean.”

“Nothing. It’s not even mine. I heard it from a fish.” He was already looking at the ceiling. “Ignore me.”

Lumi swore at a bolt.

Then she went quiet under there for a long moment, and Niko watched her count — he could see her boot tapping — and on the fifth count, with the leather sighing out and the pressure dropping away for that one soft second, the gate went round a full turn as if it had never been stuck at all.

Lumi came out from under the housing with grease to the elbow and an expression Niko couldn’t read.

“I turned it on the out-breath,” she said.

“Good,” Marta said.

“It’s not— that’s not clever, that’s just *timing*—” She stopped. Looked at her own hand. Then she made a spark between two fingers, the way she did, gather and compress and release, except she waited a beat before the release, and the spark came out small and hard and blue and used almost nothing.

She stared at it until it died.

“I’m not writing that down,” she said.

“Write it down,” Niko said.

“I’ll write down the *gate.*”

The Jester was on the far side of the hall by then, holding his stick up next to a dead bellows to compare heights, and appeared to have no interest in valves whatsoever.

They set the working gates as far open as they would go, and the air changed. Niko felt it on his face — a draw, faint, going somewhere. Bram noticed it first as a smell.

“Wet,” he said. “Not garden wet. Big wet. Cold.”

He was standing behind the last bellows in the row.

There was a gap there where the stone had been cut away, and it was not a maintenance gap. It was wide enough for two of them abreast. It went down, and it had steps, and the steps were worn in the middle the way steps get worn by feet and nothing else.

Marta looked at it for a long time.

“We told Pell two days,” Bram said.

“I know what we told Pell.”

The air came up the stair against Niko’s face, cold and wet and smelling of stone and, faintly, absurdly, of something green.

Marta rolled the rope onto her shoulder.

“Lanterns lit. Nobody touches water. If I say up, we go up and we don’t discuss it on the stairs.”

---

They came out of the stair into the dark, and the dark had a sky in it.

Niko’s first thought was that they had come outside. It was so complete that his body believed it — the way the air opened, the way sound stopped coming back off anything. Night. Clear night, high summer, the good kind, with the stars out so thick they made grey between them.

Then Bram said, “Put your lantern down,” in a voice nobody had heard him use, and they put the lanterns down, and it got better.

Stars. Thousands. Fixed in drifts and rivers and clusters, some blue-white and small, some fat and yellow, banded across the dark in a long crowded arch that any child in Mossgate could have pointed at and named wrong.

And underneath them, another sky.

The floor was water. Black, unmoving, from their boots out to nowhere — and every star was in it, twice as many, upside down and perfectly still, so the cavern fell away above and below at once and the five of them stood on the seam of it like people who had been put somewhere by mistake.

Lumi sat down. Then she lay down on the cold stone with her head hanging over the water and looked at both skies at the same time.

“Nobody talk,” she said. “Nobody explain anything. I mean it. I’ll bite.”

Nobody talked.

Niko had his chalk in his hand and could not remember taking it out. He put it away.

After a while a star came loose.

It detached from the arch and fell — not fast, not straight, a slow yellow tumble across the whole dark, taking so long that Niko had time to lose it and find it again twice. It hit the water without a sound. The reflection came up to meet it and they touched and went out together.

A moment later, something under the surface lit up going after it. Long, thin, quick — a slip of green light unspooling through the black and then gone.

“Moths,” Bram said quietly. “They’re moths. And that’s something eating one.”

“It’s a *sky*,” Lumi said, from the floor.

“It can be both. Most things are food.”

Marta stayed standing, one hand on her knife out of habit and her face turned up like everyone else’s, and after a while she took her hand off the knife.

Niko looked for the Jester, because he wanted to see him enjoy this.

The Jester had stopped four paces back, mid-step, one foot not yet down.

He was looking up. Not the way he looked at trees — that hungry, pleased, checking-on-them way. He was just looking, with his mouth a little open and his bells not making any sound at all, and he stayed that way long enough that Niko’s pleasure curdled into something else.

“That’s new,” the Jester said.

Niko opened his mouth.

“Or I’m still asleep,” the Jester said, walking again, brisk, past him, bells suddenly loud. “That would also explain the coat.”

He went down to the water’s edge and crouched there with his back to all of them, and made a joke about his own reflection that Niko didn’t hear properly.

Above them the false stars hung exactly where they had been when the party came down the stair, and had not moved a hair, and gave no sign of intending to.
