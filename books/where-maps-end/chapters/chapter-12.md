# Chapter 12 — The Glass Garden

The glass showed first as a glint that wasn’t water.

They had come down off the fortress spur onto a shoulder of older path—cutter marks, pilgrim ribbon scraps, a nail that still held a tag reading *irrig.* in a handwriting that had given up halfway through the word. Wolves had left prints in soft dust and then thought better of the direction. Deer prints glittered faintly and vanished into fern.

Bram sniffed.

“Wet leaves. Old metal. Something sweet that’s pretending to be flowers.”

“That’s not a map,” Lumi said.

“It’s lunch’s cousin.”

Marta ignored both of them with professional warmth and pointed where the glint thickened into shapes: domes half-swallowed by living wood, panes spidered and still holding, green pressing up from inside as if the plants had filed a complaint and won.

“Glass Garden,” she said. “Name on the scrap. We treat it like a ruin that forgot to die.”

Niko’s fingers found chalk before his feet found the threshold.

Trees grew through glass—not shattered out, grown through, trunks sealing old breaks with patient bark. Inside the nearest dome, moss ran in clean veins along channels that still held a skin of water. Tiny bright insects moved like punctuation. The air smelled of damp soil and something mineral, almost like the Singing River’s breath without the music.

Wonder landed without asking for a plot job.

Lumi made a noise that was almost a hymn and then apologized to nobody.

“It’s still running,” she said. “Irrigation. Loops. That’s— no, wait. Not academy loops. Older. Ruder. Beautiful.”

“Don’t fall in love with plumbing,” Marta said. “Fall in love with exits.”

They moved careful through a broken arch where the door had been suggestion. Soft soil under boot. Glass grit. A beetle the size of a thumb clicked once and escorted them three paces out of courtesy or territorial paperwork.

Niko chalked: *Glass Garden — domes — trees through glass — channels wet — not on Guild three — still alive.*

In a side bay, crystal moss grew in tidy shelves—healthier than the cavern bowl, not theatrical. Bram pinched a leaf, sniffed, did not eat.

“Deer food,” he said. “Good manners version.”

“Can we—” Lumi began.

“No samples from the pretty shelves until we know if pretty is bait,” Marta said.

Lumi closed her kit with tragic dignity.

They found the sealed chamber by following a channel that refused to silt.

The door was not Guild work. Not Conqueror burn. Smooth join, glass and pale stone, a seam like a held breath. No lock anyone honest would carve. A viewing slit at face height showed dim green light and a shape on a slab that might have been a person if people came with ridiculous color even in sleep.

Niko stopped so hard Lumi walked into his pack.

“Sorry,” she said. “You’re a sudden wall.”

“Look.”

Marta looked. Bram looked. Lumi looked and then looked away and then looked again because looking away didn’t help.

A man slept on the slab as if sleep were a job he took seriously. Clothes that had never met Marta’s idea of travel. Soft bells at wrist and ankle that did not quite ring, muffled by dust and time. A stick leaned in the corner like a joke waiting for an audience. His face was peaceful in a way that made Niko’s neck itch. Peaceful wasn’t the basin’s current fashion.

“Alive?” Bram whispered.

Lumi held her meter near the slit, not touching.

“Something’s low. Not dead. Not academy dead. I don’t— wait. I don’t have a word that isn’t stupid.”

“Write stupid later,” Marta said. Her voice had gone flat the way it did near silent groves. “We do not wake strangers in sealed rooms because they look interesting.”

“He’s not a wolf,” Lumi said.

“Wolves are clearer.”

Niko watched the man’s chest. It rose. Fell. The bells stayed almost quiet. Beyond the chamber, somewhere in the domes, water ticked through a channel with the patience of a clock that had outlived its makers.

“If the garden’s still running,” Niko said slowly, “someone kept it running. Or something. Or he—”

“Don’t finish that,” Marta said. “You don’t know.”

“I’m noticing.”

“Notice quieter.”

Bram sat on his heels and peered as if the sleeper might smell like onions.

“He looks like he lost a bet with a costume chest,” Bram said. “If we wake him, does he owe us stew or do we owe him?”

“Neither,” Marta said. “We chalk. We leave. We come back with a better reason than curiosity.”

Niko’s better reason arrived as sound from the outer dome: glass-wolf nails on grit, then the soft panic glitter of a deer that had chosen the wrong door.

“Company,” Marta said.

They met the wolf in the channel bay—one animal, lean, foam-dry mouth, the same pressed hunger as the ridge. It went for Lumi’s crate first, as if books smelled like soft meat. Marta cut the line of its rush with boot and blade. Bram’s pot swung low and ugly. Niko caught a channel lip with his free hand and kicked a loose pane fragment into the wolf’s path; glass sang; the animal slipped. Lumi’s spark cracked close enough to smell ozone and old irrigation.

The wolf left bleeding into fern, not dead, not brave enough for a second opinion.

Bram exhaled through his teeth.

“I miss when lunch was the loudest thing.”

Niko’s hands shook as he chalked the scrape under the garden note. The sleeper’s chamber slit watched them without eyes.

Lumi spoke first, soft, stubborn.

“If cascade weather’s chewing the basin, and this place is still watering itself, he might know why. Or the room might. Or I’m inventing. I invent when I’m scared.”

“You’re always inventing,” Marta said. “Sometimes you’re right. That’s the problem.”

Niko looked at the sealed seam. At the ridiculous clothes. At the stick. At the wet channels that still obeyed a plan nobody present had drawn.

“What if he’s not a threat,” he said. “What if he’s stuck. Like a map that never got to hang.”

Marta’s jaw worked.

“Stuck people become problems when you pull them into moving days.”

“Stuck gardens become problems when you ignore them,” Lumi said, then blinked. “That sounded like a quote. I retract the music. I keep the plumbing.”

Bram stood, pot in hand, deciding something with his shoulders.

“I vote we ask him if he wants stew,” he said. “If he says no, we leave. If he says yes, we have manners. If he tries to eat us, we already practiced today.”

“That’s not a vote system,” Marta said.

“It’s my system.”

Silence held—the good kind, full of people thinking with their whole bodies.

Niko did not say *please*. He put his chalk away and waited, which was worse.

Marta put her knife away slowly.

“We try the seam,” she said. “If it doesn’t want to open, we don’t break the garden to satisfy a story. If it opens, nobody stands in front of his hands. Bram, pot ready. Lumi, spark only if I say. Niko— you talk first. You’re the one who collects strays made of questions.”

“I don’t—” Niko began.

“You do,” Lumi said, almost fond. “It’s your survey method.”

Outside the dome, wind moved ordinary leaves. Inside, water ticked. The sleeper breathed.

Niko set his palm near the seam—not on it—and felt cool air that smelled like soil older than Mossgate’s streets.

“If you’re listening,” he said to glass and dust and a man who might not be listening, “we’re not Conquerors. We’re survey. The basin’s getting strange in ways that bite roads. We saw an upside-down waterfall and left the lights. We saw a Stag leave and didn’t follow. We’re tired. We have stew opinions. If you want to keep sleeping, keep sleeping. If you want to wake—”

The seam warmed under his palm like a blush.

Marta swore once, quietly, the way she swore at weather.

“Of course,” she said. “Of course it listens to speeches.”

“That wasn’t a speech,” Niko said, mortified.

“It was enough of one.”

The seal did not explode. It sighed. Pale light thinned along the join. Air exchanged—dome air for chamber air, green for greener. Bells at the sleeper’s wrist stirred with the draft and made a sound so soft Niko thought he’d imagined it.

The man’s fingers twitched.

Lumi grabbed Niko’s sleeve hard enough to teach cloth a lesson.

“We did that,” she whispered. “We are doing that.”

Bram lifted the pot lid a finger-width, ready for comedy or disaster.

On the slab, the sleeper’s eyes opened—not slow like storybooks, not sudden like ambushes. Like someone checking whether the world had finished being inconvenient.

He sat up. Dust left his hair in a little disappointed cloud. One bell found its voice and rang, bright and stupid and alive.

He looked at them—the knife, the pot, the crate, the chalk-stained survey boy—and smiled as if wardrobe were the emergency.

Niko’s mouth went dry around a hundred better first questions.

The man on the slab stretched, winced at a joint, and spoke first anyway, cheerful as a wrong mushroom tossed without shame:

“You're all terribly dressed.”
