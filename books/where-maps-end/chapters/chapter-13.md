# Chapter 13 — Terribly Dressed

For a moment nobody answered, because answering felt like agreeing to a game with no posted rules.

Dust still hung in the chamber light. One of the man’s wrist-bells rang again, pleased with itself. He swung his legs off the slab like a guest who’d been given a bad inn bed and decided to be charming about it.

Marta’s knife stayed down. Her shoulders didn’t.

“Identify,” she said.

“I would,” he said, “if the clothes weren’t shouting so loud. Is brown the fashion now? All of you? At once? How democratic.”

Bram lifted the pot a fraction, then lowered it, offended on behalf of fabric.

“Our clothes work,” Bram said.

“So does a bucket,” the man said brightly. “That doesn’t make it a hat.”

Lumi’s mouth opened, closed, opened.

“I’m Lumi. That’s Marta. Bram. Niko. You’re— you’re in a sealed— we didn’t mean to— actually we did mean to a little— the seam listened—”

“Bells,” Marta cut in. “Name.”

The man considered the stick in the corner, took it, spun it once, and nearly dropped it. He laughed at himself without embarrassment.

“People used to call me all sorts of things when I was late,” he said. “Late seems accurate. You may call me whatever fits in a shout. Jester works. It’s wrong in a friendly way.”

“That’s not a name,” Marta said.

“It’s a habit,” he said. “Names are heavy before breakfast.”

Niko found his voice in the chalk dust on his fingers.

“How long were you asleep?”

The Jester—habit, wrong, friendly—tipped his head as if listening for a clock in the walls.

“A few years?” he offered. “Enough for fashion to die horribly. Not enough for trees to grow through the roof unless someone has been very ambitious with fertilizer.”

He looked up through the cracked dome green, eyes widening with real delight.

“Oh,” he said. “They did. Good for them.”

Lumi made a small wounded academic sound.

“A few years doesn’t— the growth rings— no, wait. I shouldn’t lecture a man who just sat up. Lecturing can wait. Measuring can’t. May I—”

“No touching,” Marta said.

“I was going to ask permission,” Lumi muttered.

“Ask with your hands in your pockets.”

Bram, who had been staring at the bells the way he stared at suspicious mushrooms, finally spoke the important question.

“Are you hungry?”

The Jester’s smile went soft and immediate.

“Starving,” he said. “I dreamt of soup that had opinions. Does yours?”

“Always,” Bram said, already turning toward the outer dome as if stew could solve seals. “Come where the light is less dramatic.”

Marta blocked the chamber mouth with her body—not a threat. A gate.

“Rules,” she said. “You walk where we walk. You don’t touch the channels. You don’t wander toward lights, silent groves, or anything that parts trees. You tell us if you feel sick, cursed, or inclined to bite. If you’re a problem, we leave you with water and a clear path. Clear?”

“Crystal,” the Jester said, then glanced at a moss shelf and giggled once. “Poor choice of word. Clear. Yes. I am an excellent temporary person.”

Niko watched him step into the dome light. The bells made a thin bright sentence around his ankles. Up close he looked less like a ghost and more like a traveler who’d lost a bet with time and refused to pay in dignity. His stick tapped glass grit. He paused at a channel, crouched, and flicked the water with one finger.

“Still cold,” he said. “Rude of it, after all this time. I like rude.”

“Do you know this place?” Niko asked.

The Jester’s face did something quick—blank, then sunny again.

“I know gardens,” he said. “I know sleep. I know that if you leave water alone long enough it starts inventing hobbies. Beyond that—” He shrugged, and a bell apologized for him. “I appear to have misplaced a few shelves in my head. Embarrassing. Don’t tell the trees.”

Marta’s eyes narrowed. Not belief. Not dismissal. Filing.

“Cover story,” she said to Niko, quiet. “Washed-out hire. Bad timing. We don’t advertise sealed slabs in Mossgate gossip.”

“I can be a washed-out hire,” the Jester said cheerfully, hearing anyway. “I wash out beautifully. Ask any river.”

They made a small camp in a dome bay where the glass still made a kind of roof and the channels kept their tick. Bram built a careful fire in a stone dish that looked designed for it, which made Lumi stare and then pretend she hadn’t.

Stew happened. Onions happened. The Jester accepted a bowl with both hands like communion and burned his tongue immediately.

“Ah,” he said, eyes watering. “Temperature. I remember temperature. Rude little miracle.”

“Blow on it,” Bram said.

“I am. Internally.”

Niko sat with his scrap roll and tried not to stare. Failed. The man’s presence made the garden feel less like a ruin and more like a room waiting for its occupant to notice the furniture had had children.

“The basin’s maps disagree,” Niko said, because silence made his curiosity itch. “Same place. Different years. River, forest, grass. We think the ground changed. Something used to move it. People say that something’s gone.”

The Jester ate, listening with his whole face the way Bram listened to pots.

“Gone is a big word,” he said lightly. “Misplaced is kinder. Things that dig rarely send letters.”

Niko’s chalk hand tightened.

“You’ve seen—”

“I’ve seen stew,” the Jester said, pointing with his spoon. “I’ve seen brown clothes. I’ve seen a boy who wants the world to confess in complete sentences. The world prefers footnotes.”

Lumi leaned forward despite Marta’s glance.

“There are bleach patches. Moss stress. A Hollow Stag leaving. Wolves pressed onto roads. If there’s a gardener’s logic to this place—”

“Gardener,” the Jester repeated, tasting the word. He looked oddly pleased, then oddly sad, then pleased again as if he’d dropped both feelings and chosen the lighter one. “I like gardeners. They argue with dirt and sometimes win. I am at best a person who woke up under glass. Don’t promote me.”

Marta set her empty bowl down.

“You’re coming with us until we know you’re not a hazard,” she said. “Or until we reach a road that can lose you politely. We move at our pace. You keep up.”

“I adore paces,” he said. “Especially ones with snacks.”

Bram nodded, treaty sealed in onion steam.

“Five bowls tomorrow,” Bram said. “I don’t cook for ghosts.”

“I’m only lightly ghost,” the Jester said. “Mostly bells.”

Lumi tried to explain sealed-chamber airflow to nobody in particular, got tangled in her own sentence, and accepted a second heel of bread as apology from the universe. The Jester watched her the way people watched street performers—delighted, slightly worried for the props.

“You’re very loud with books,” he told her.

“They’re loud first,” Lumi said.

“I respect that.”

Niko found himself smiling before he decided to. Marta saw it and did not comment, which was its own comment.

They divided small jobs because small jobs kept sealed chambers from becoming religion. Bram rinsed bowls in channel water and muttered about mineral taste. Marta checked the outer arch twice. Lumi labeled a scrap *Glass Garden — still running — do not trust my handwriting.* Niko copied her scrap under his own so the Wall, someday, would have two nervous witnesses.

“Why help us?” Niko asked the Jester, when the others were briefly busy being practical.

“You woke me,” he said. “Also the stew. Also you look like you collect questions the way I collect bad timing. I get lonely around unfinished lists.”

“That’s not a reason.”

“It’s a mood,” the Jester said. “Moods move people. Ask any wolf.”

Night came softer inside the dome than on the ridge. Outside, somewhere far, a wolf called and another answered—too many answers for comfort. Niko chalked by lantern: *woke sealed sleeper — calls himself Jester — claims few years — trees through glass older — jokes / won’t explain — garden still runs.*

He hesitated, then added: *said things that dig get misplaced — not proof.*

Lumi wrote her own ugly notes and then fell asleep sitting up with a book as a face-roof. Marta took watch at the arch. Bram snored once and denied it in advance.

The Jester did not sleep at once. He sat by a channel with his stick across his knees and watched water invent its hobby.

Niko, not sleeping either, heard him murmur—not to Niko, not for show—

“You’re taller,” he told a trunk that had punched the dome. “I don’t mind.”

Niko closed his eyes and pretended that was ordinary.

In the morning Bram said “Morning” to everyone, including the Jester, who replied “Is it?” with such sincere confusion that Lumi laughed until she hiccupped.

They packed. The garden ticked. The sealed chamber stayed open behind them like a mouth that had decided not to bite.

At the outer arch, the Jester paused and looked back once—not longing. Checking. As if counting whether the green still agreed to live without him standing over it.

“Coming?” Marta asked.

“Always,” he said, and the bells lied cheerfully beside his steps. “I hate being late twice.”

Niko stepped out into basin air that smelled of fern and distant bleach and roads that were starting to remember teeth.

Five people now. One pot. One stick. Too many questions that refused to dress properly.
