# Chapter 20 — The Throat Between

It was Lumi who worked out that they had done something dangerous.

She was sitting by the race with her feet up on a rock, happy, watching the water go, and then she wasn’t.

“The arms are capped,” she said.

Marta turned around.

“Upstairs. The cistern head. Three arms, three stone plugs, we stood there and looked at them.” Lumi got up. “We’ve just pointed a river at a corked bottle.”

Niko’s stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

“Where does it fill first?”

“The lowest thing between here and the cork,” Lumi said. “Which is the rock under the shelf above the salt post. Which is already failing. From underneath.”

Bram said something short. Marta was already coiling the rope.

“Up,” she said. “All of it, all four floors, we don’t stop, we don’t eat standing at the top of a ladder like last time. Bram, everything heavy goes on me.”

“That’s not—”

“*On me.*”

---

They went up through the Bellows Hall at a jog, and Marta cracked the two working gates wide as they passed, and Grandmother took a breath so big the dust came off the floor in a sheet.

They went up the well shaft on the brackets, and Niko’s arms were shaking before halfway and he did it anyway.

The Undercroft was full of a new sound: a deep, slow knock, coming up through the stone of the cistern head, like someone testing a door at the far end of a house.

Marta put her hand on the south-east cap.

“Warm,” she said.

They got it out with the bar, the hammer, Bram’s shoulder, and a great deal of swearing. It came free all at once and went over sideways, and nothing happened, and they stood there in the ringing quiet feeling foolish for four whole seconds before the knock came up the arm again — closer.

“Second one,” Lumi said. “The west arm too. Give it two mouths, not one.”

They gave it two mouths.

Niko was chalking the open arms when the first water came — not a flood, a wet dark tongue creeping out of a stone throat that had been dry longer than his grandmother’s grandmother — and he stopped writing and watched it come and forgot to breathe.

“It works,” he said.

“It leaves,” Marta said. “That’s different from arriving. Rafters. Go.”

---

The Rafters were exactly as they had left them, which after everything felt like an insult and a kindness at once.

Green light under, day light over. Hundreds of small yellow lanterns nobody had lit.

“Quick and careful,” Marta said. “Lumi, tell us wrong and I’ll leave you up here.”

Lumi had her nose an inch from a case.

“It isn’t a shell,” she said. “It’s a *shutter.* Look — the seam’s not fused, it’s held. It’s been held closed for— I don’t know. They’re waiting. They’re waiting for wet air and they haven’t had wet air since the cistern stopped sharing, so they’ve just—” She stopped and breathed out. “They’ve just been being patient. Cut the seam. Don’t touch the animal. Cut the seam and put it back in the fork and leave it alone.”

Bram did the first one, because Bram had the best hands and everybody knew it.

The case came open along its seam with a sound like a knife through a pea pod, and the yellow light spilled out over his fingers, and something inside stirred and did not come out.

“It’s alive,” he said.

He said it quietly, and he stood there for a second with the light in his hands, and Niko decided he would keep that — not the chalk note, the picture. Bram in a glass roof holding somebody’s two-thousand-year-old patience like a hot dish.

Then he put it back in the fork and said, “Next,” and they worked the catwalks until Niko’s knife hand cramped, and the light came loose all around them, and by the end the Rafters were louder than they had been — a dry rustle running along the branches, hundreds of small waking arguments.

None of them flew. Not yet.

“They’ll want the wet,” Lumi said, looking down through the glass at the atrium. “When the humidity comes up through the floors. Then they’ll go.”

“Go where?”

“Out.” She waved a hand at the whole basin. “Everywhere. That’s the job. That’s what they’re for.”

Marta was already at the ladder.

On the way down, in the Bellows Hall, Lumi lay on her back under the housing and set the last gate the whole way over — waiting for the out-breath, not fighting it, and doing it so quickly that Niko only knew she had waited because he was counting too.

She came out from under and didn’t look at anyone.

“It’s just timing,” she said.

“Nobody said anything,” Bram said.

“You were *about* to.”

The Jester had found a dead bellows to sit on and was retying one of his bells, which had come loose somewhere between floors, and did not appear to be listening at all.

---

The Throat began where the water went.

They found it below the wheel, following the race — a corridor cut square and true through the rock, going away from the Understars in a long straight line with the water running in a channel down its middle and a walkway of dry stone beside it. The walls were lined with pale root, ribbed at intervals, the fibres running lengthways like something drawn out.

And it breathed.

A draught came up the corridor into their faces. Held. Turned. Went back the other way past their ears. In and out, slow, timed to nothing they could see.

Bram put his hand out into it.

“Same rhythm as Grandmother,” he said.

“It can’t be,” Lumi said. “That’s two floors and half a mile of—”

“Same rhythm.”

Niko crouched and looked down the corridor as far as the lantern reached, and it kept going, dead straight, sloping very gently down.

“Marta,” he said. “Which way is this pointing?”

She had her compass out already. She looked at it a long time.

“South and east,” she said. “It’s pointing at Mossgate.”

Nobody moved.

“It’s a road,” Niko said. “It’s an actual road, under everything, from that wheel to our town. A day and a half over the ridge and it’s— what, three hours?”

“Four,” Marta said. “With the slope.”

“Why does it exist?”

“Ask it while we run.”

---

They ran.

Not the whole way. Marta set a pace they could hold and broke it twice for water, and both times she was up and moving before anyone had finished drinking.

The channel beside them was ankle-deep when they started.

At the first rest it was knee-deep.

At the second rest it was over the lip of the channel and running flat across the walkway in a sheet, cold, and moving faster than they were.

“That’s our river,” Lumi panted. “That is our river that we made.”

“Save it,” Marta said.

The beetles came past them in the third hour.

Not at them — *past* them, going the same way, a low clattering carpet of stone beetles pouring down the walkway between their boots, thousands of them, hurrying with the mindless certainty of things that had felt the water come up through the floor of their nest.

Niko flattened against the wall and let them go by and did not enjoy any part of it.

“They know something,” Bram said.

“They know less than we do,” Marta said. “That’s why they’re faster.”

---

The end of the Throat was a grate.

It sat in a low arched chamber where the corridor ran out — an iron and root lattice a little wider than a wagon, set into the wall at a slant, with daylight coming through it in grey bars. Beyond it Niko could see fern, and a slope of scrub, and the pale unmistakable smear of a bleach shelf going down toward a ditch.

The grate was packed solid.

Silt, root, two centuries of leaf litter, a fallen sapling gone black — all of it jammed into the lattice and set hard as brick.

The water was at their knees.

Nobody discussed it. Bram went at the mass with the bar. Marta went at the frame. Lumi got her hands in and pulled fistfuls of rotten wood out and threw them behind her and got more. Niko found the hinge-side and shouted that it was a gate, that the whole grate was a gate, that it wanted to swing—

It shifted.

Then the water behind them stopped being a floor and became a shove, and the grate went from *stuck* to *nearly open* in one great slam of pressure that would have opened it entirely if the top corner hadn’t bitten on its own frame.

A gap. Two hands wide. Water screaming through it.

“Wedge!” Marta shouted.

Bram put his pot in.

It went in on its side under the grate’s lip and it held, and it held, and Niko heard the metal complain and then stop complaining, and Bram’s face did something that Niko didn’t look at.

Marta got her shoulder and her bar into the gap and heaved and the grate went another handspan and the water took it, all at once, all the way.

She was still in the gap when it went.

Niko had her arm. He remembered having her arm. He did not remember the rest of it clearly, only that Bram had the other one and the Jester had a fistful of her collar and that the three of them came out of the arch on the flood in a tangle and rolled twenty feet down a scrub slope in the open grey daylight of a basin afternoon, and that Marta made a sound he had never heard her make.

The grate hung open behind them. The Throat emptied itself into the ditch above the salt post in a brown roaring rope of water and silt and two thousand years of leaves, and the ditch — dry since before the 1987 survey drew it as a riverbank — took it, and filled, and carried it away downslope.

Marta’s leg was open from knee to hip. Not spurting. Bad.

Lumi got her belt around it before Niko had finished being useless. Bram tore his own shirt without appearing to notice he was doing it. Marta swore in a low steady stream at the sky and let them work.

Niko went back to the arch.

The water was coming out of it in a solid column now, and behind it, up the dark of the Throat, he could see nothing at all — no walkway, no channel, no lantern-light corridor going back to a wheel and a shore and a hundred thousand false stars.

Just water, from wall to wall, for as far as there was.

His scrap roll was in it somewhere. He had felt it go in the tangle. He found it forty feet downslope in the ditch's new current, snagged on a root, and he pulled it out and it came apart in his hands, chalk running off the paper in grey ropes, three weeks of noticing turning into nothing while he held it.

He got maybe a third of it back. The Guild sheets, waxed, mostly survived. Everything of his own after the fortress was gone.

He stood in the ditch with the water round his shins and the ruined paper in his fist and laughed, once, badly.

Behind him Marta said, “Niko,” in the voice she used for exits.

He turned around.

Downslope, past the ditch, past the pale shelf, the trade shoulder ran away north toward Mossgate — and it was black with carts. Wagons nose to tail, people on foot beside them, a cart tipped in the verge with its wheel off, and the sound of it reaching them thin and wrong across half a mile of fern.

Above the shoulder, in the scrub on both sides of the road, the beetle noise had become a single flat hiss, like rain on a tent.

“We’re a day early or a day late,” Marta said, going grey around the mouth. “Somebody get me up.”
