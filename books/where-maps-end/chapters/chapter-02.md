# Chapter 2 — The Trade Road

The trees closed over the road sooner than Niko expected.

Mossgate’s noise fell away behind them—cart wheels, skewer smoke, the Guild clock—until what remained was boots on packed dirt and the creak of Bram’s cookware.

Nobody looked back except Niko, and only once.

The blank sheet was already out of sight.

---

They walked.

Marta ahead by half a length, Lumi drifting sideways whenever a leaf looked interesting, Bram somewhere in the middle with the kettle bumping his hip.

Niko kept the three Guild maps rolled under one arm and the chalk stub in his pocket.

The Trade Road was wide enough for two carts. Wheel ruts held yesterday’s rain. Someone had chalked a crooked arrow on a tree trunk and written *water* underneath, then crossed it out and written *was water*.

Lumi stopped.

“Is that an Expedition Rule or a complaint?”

“Both,” Marta said, without slowing.

Lumi hurried to catch up, books knocking inside the crate. Flour still dusted one cover from the baker’s stall. She hadn’t brushed it off.

Bram broke a bun in half and held a piece out without looking. Niko took it. Warm. Soft in the middle.

They ate while walking. Crumbs fell. A small bird hopped after them for ten paces and lost interest.

“Morning,” Bram said to a merchant coming the other way.

The merchant stared. It was past midday.

“Morning,” the merchant said anyway, confused, and kept going.

Niko almost asked. He didn’t.

---

At a bend, the trees thinned and let light through in strips. A two-wheeled cart had pulled off into the grass. A woman in a Harvester sash was counting wrapped bundles while a boy scraped mud from an axle.

Marta lifted two fingers as they passed. The woman lifted two fingers back. That was all.

“Friends?” Lumi asked.

“People who come back,” Marta said.

“That’s different?”

“Sometimes.”

Niko glanced at the bundles. Dried something. Salted something. The smell reminded him of Bram’s complaints about Guild food.

“Harvesters,” Lumi said, mostly to herself. “In and out. Document later. Or never.”

“We document,” Niko said.

“We try,” Lumi said. “The academy documents. I mostly spill.”

Bram made a small sound that might have been agreement and might have been the bun.

They left the cart behind.

---

Further on, a Guild rest circle sat under three leaning poles. No one was using it. A stone ring for fire. Pegs for packs. A painted board nailed crooked:

*Don’t camp on blue-glow moss.*  
*If the grove goes silent, leave.*  
*Hang your map.*

Marta didn’t stop at the circle. She did slow enough that Niko could read the board.

“You’ve seen those?” he asked.

“I’ve hung maps,” Marta said. “I’ve left. I’ve come back. The board stays longer than most people.”

“The silent grove one—” Lumi began.

“Later,” Marta said.

“I was going to say I’ve only read it.”

“Good. Keep it that way as long as you can.”

Niko looked at the third line again.

*Hang your map.*

They already had. A blank one. Waiting.

He started walking before he could think about fading paper.

---

They didn’t talk for a while.

A lizard on a warm stone. A cart’s distant rattle. The smell of crushed green where wheels had cut the verge.

Bram adjusted a strap once and kept walking.

Lumi hummed two notes, forgot the rest, and checked a page as if the missing notes might be written there.

Niko found himself counting steps between chalk marks on the trees. Someone had numbered them in an old hand. Twelve. Thirteen. A gap. Fifteen.

“Fourteen’s gone,” he said.

Marta glanced at the trunks.

“Storm. Or a knife. Or someone needed firewood and didn’t care about counting.”

“Or the road moved,” Lumi said, then winced. “I mean—no. That’s academy joke talk. Forget I—”

“Say it again when we have a reason,” Marta said.

Niko kept the missing fourteen in his head anyway.

---

They stopped when Marta said stop, which was when a fallen log offered seats that weren’t mud.

Bram sat, opened the kettle, sniffed, and decided it was still water. He passed it. Lumi drank too fast and coughed. Marta drank like someone who had learned not to waste stillness.

Niko unrolled the 1987 map across his knees.

Grassland. A road through the middle. Mile marks like beads.

He put his finger on Mossgate’s edge, then traced south.

“We’re about here.”

Lumi leaned in until her hair threatened the ink. Marta pulled her back by the crate strap without looking.

“Matches,” Lumi said. “So far.”

“So far,” Niko agreed.

He set the 1839 beside it. Forest where the grassland was now. Same corner stamp. Same locus mark.

Then 1712. River.

On paper, the three places occupied the same inked oval and refused to be the same place.

“How do you survey a river that isn’t there?” Niko asked.

“You don’t,” Marta said. “You survey what’s in front of you.”

“Then someone surveyed a river.”

“Someone surveyed a river in 1712,” Lumi said. “Someone surveyed trees in 1839. Someone surveyed grass in 1987. The Guild put them in one drawer and called it a problem.”

“Or a payroll,” Marta said.

Bram was chewing the last of his bun and not looking at the maps.

“You still think they’re wrong?” Niko asked him.

Bram swallowed.

“I think I’m going to cook when we stop for real. Wrong is your job.”

“Helpful,” Lumi said.

“I brought salt,” Bram said.

That, somehow, ended the argument.

Niko rolled the sheets again. The fold lines still didn’t match each other. He still didn’t mention it.

Marta stood first.

“Up.”

They got up. Lumi dropped a pencil, found it, dropped a different pencil, and decided that was enough stationery for one stop.

---

For a stretch the road was almost pretty.

Not beautiful in the way people meant when they talked about the Singing River—Niko had only heard of that, never seen it—but easy. Leaf-shadow. Warm dirt. A cart with bells somewhere ahead, faint enough to be company without being company.

“Do you always take this road?” Niko asked Marta.

“When south is the job.”

“And when it isn’t?”

“Different road.”

Lumi made a face at Marta’s back.

“She’s like this on purpose,” Lumi whispered.

“I can hear you,” Marta said.

“I know,” Lumi said, louder. “I’m practicing honesty.”

Bram shifted the pot to his other hand.

“Honesty doesn’t weigh as much as iron.”

“That’s not—” Lumi stopped. “Actually, that’s fair.”

They passed a shrine no taller than Niko’s knee: three stacked stones, a faded ribbon, a bowl with two copper bits and a berry gone to mush. Marta didn’t leave anything. Neither did Bram. Lumi hovered, then put the berry’s replacement—a dry biscuit crumb—into the bowl and looked guilty about the quality of the offering.

Niko didn’t ask which philosophy that was. He wrote nothing. Some things weren’t maps.

A little later Bram said, to no one in particular, “We should have bought onions.”

Nobody answered. He accepted that with dignity.

---

The road dipped.

Not much. Enough that the wheel ruts held a darker line of damp, as if water remembered the way even when it was gone.

A Guild mile-marker stood in the middle of that dip.

Stone post. Painted number flaking. The iron Guild stamp near the top was still clear enough to read if you knew the shape.

It stood in a dry ditch.

Not beside the ditch. In it. The post’s base sat where mud should have been soft, except the mud had cracked into pale plates that curled at the edges. Grass tried and failed along the sides. A child’s shoe print crossed the crack-bed and vanished where the ditch climbed back to the road.

Niko stopped.

Marta took two more steps before she noticed and turned.

“What.”

Niko pointed with the rolled maps.

“That’s a mile-marker.”

“Yes.”

“It’s in a ditch.”

“Yes.”

Lumi came up on his other side, already opening a book, already losing the page she wanted.

“Flood,” she said. “Markers get moved. Or the bank collapses and the post slides. Or someone replants it wrong after a repair.”

“Or,” Niko said.

He crouched at the ditch edge. The air smelled like old wet and dry dust at the same time. He touched a cracked plate with two fingers. It powdered.

On the 1987 map, this stretch was riverbank. A thin blue line hugging the road. Mile beads along the road. He checked the number on the post against the beads.

It matched the map’s place.

It did not match the map’s water.

“The 1987 sheet calls this a riverbank,” Niko said.

“Called,” Marta said. “Years ago.”

“It’s a Guild map. They don’t redraw for fun.”

“They don’t redraw for free,” Marta said. “There’s a difference.”

Bram had walked into the ditch without asking permission from anyone, including the ditch. He knocked the mile-marker with a knuckle like it might be hollow.

“Solid.”

“Don’t,” Marta said.

“I’m checking.”

“I didn’t say explain,” Marta said. “I said don’t.”

Niko almost smiled. He didn’t. He was still looking at the cracked bed.

“If there was a river here,” he said, “where did it go?”

“Underground,” Lumi said. “Or sideways. Or it was seasonal and the season quit. Or the surveyor inked the wrong bend. That happens. I’ve done it. Once. Twice. Don’t write that down.”

“You write everything down,” Niko said.

“Yes. That’s the problem.”

Marta scanned the trees, the road ahead, the empty ditch behind them. Not worried. Counting exits.

“We can stand here inventing rivers,” she said, “or we can walk to a place that still has one.”

“I want to mark it,” Niko said.

“Then mark it while walking.”

He did.

Chalk on the 1987 margin: *mile post in dry ditch — bed cracked — no water*. Date. His initials, small.

Lumi watched the chalk like it was a small animal.

“You’re going to fill that whole margin before Hunter Forest.”

“Probably.”

“Good,” she said, then looked startled that she’d said it.

Bram climbed out of the ditch, wiped his hands on his trousers, and started walking as if the conversation had been about salt all along.

“Morning,” he told the mile-marker.

The stone did not answer.

Niko waited one breath longer than Marta liked, then followed.

---

They talked less after that.

Cart traffic returned. A pair of Scholars in clean boots argued about sample jars without noticing the ditch. A dog slept in a wheel rut and did not move when they stepped around it.

Niko’s chalk stub left gray on his fingertips.

He wanted to ask whether a river could leave a Guild stamp behind like a dropped shoe. He wanted to ask whether three maps could all be right if the ground was the thing that changed. He kept both questions between his teeth.

Ahead, the trees thickened again. The Trade Road narrowed by a shoulder’s width. Moss showed on the north side of trunks in a way that made Lumi tilt her head and forget to explain why.

“Root Camp’s spur is east of Hunter Forest,” she said eventually. “Not soon. Don’t get excited. I’m not excited. I’m informing.”

“East,” Niko said.

Marta’s pace settled into the kind of walk that meant they would make the Mile-Three lean-tos before dark if nobody fell into another ditch for fun.

Bram shifted the pot to his other hip and didn’t say anything for a while.

Niko looked once over his shoulder.

The mile-marker was a small pale stick in the road’s dip, standing where water wasn’t.

On the map in his hand, the river still ran.

He rolled the sheet tighter and caught up.

Ahead, Marta raised a hand without turning.

“Mile-Three before dark. No inventing lakes on the way.”

“I wasn’t—” Niko began.

“You were,” Lumi said kindly.

Bram nodded.

They walked on under the thickening trees, four people and one dry river that refused to admit it was gone.
