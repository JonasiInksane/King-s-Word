# Chapter 3 — Mile-Three

They reached the lean-tos while the light was still deciding whether to leave.

Mile-Three was three roofs and a cleared ring of dirt. One lean-to sagged. One had a new beam. One smelled like someone else’s onions. A Guild post listed the same rules as the board on the road, sun-faded to almost polite.

Two other parties were already there.

A Scholar trio had claimed the good fire ring and were arguing about jar lids. A pair of Survivors sat with their backs to a pole, eating without talking, packs still on.

Marta took the sagging lean-to without asking anyone’s permission.

“We’re here,” she said.

Bram set the pot down like it had been personally wronged by the walk. Lumi sat on her crate and immediately opened a book to the wrong page. Niko stood a moment longer, listening to the road behind them thin into evening insects.

No river sound.

He hadn’t expected one. He noticed the absence anyway.

---

Bram cooked.

Water, salt, the mushrooms he still trusted, a heel of something dense from Marta’s pack that might have been bread yesterday. The smell pulled the Scholar argument quieter for half a minute, then the lids won again.

Then the butterflies came.

A dozen of them, no bigger than thumbnails, wings like thin crystal. They settled on the rim of Bram’s pot and the ladle handle and one brave one on his knuckle. Light broke through them onto the broth in little chips of color.

Nobody spoke.

Bram waited, spoon lifted, steam drifting around their wings.

They left as if they’d remembered an appointment. No trail. No explanation.

He kept cooking.

Lumi held out her bowl too early. Bram ignored her until the pot was ready, then filled all four. Marta’s hand was already in Niko’s bowl for a mushroom before he’d tasted anything. He made a noise. She chewed.

“Hey—”

“You hesitate,” Marta said. “Food doesn’t.”

Lumi blew on her bowl, forgot, burned her tongue, and stole a piece from Bram’s bowl while he was looking at the moth that had just bumped the lean-to beam. Bram moved his bowl farther away without comment and put one piece back in hers.

They sat with knees almost touching because the good space was taken.

“This is better than rations,” Niko said.

“Everything is better than rations,” Bram said.

Marta pointed her spoon at him.

“You said that about the buns.”

Bram shrugged and kept eating.

One of the Survivors glanced over, nodded once at Marta, and went back to chewing. Marta nodded back.

“How far to Hunter Forest from here?” Niko asked.

“Morning,” Marta said. “If we don’t invent geography.”

“I only invent rivers,” Niko said.

Nobody answered that. Lumi snorted broth onto a page anyway. Bram handed her a cloth that had already been a cloth for other emergencies.

Somewhere past the Scholar fire, someone sang two lines of a road song and forgot the third. The Survivors did not supply it.

For a while they just ate.

---

After bowls, Marta checked the packs once. Bram rinsed the pot at the trough—real water, cold, from a barrel marked *paid*—then spent a long time trying to get the kettle to stop rattling against the ladle when he packed them.

Clink.

He shifted the strap.

Clink.

He wedged a sock between them.

Clink, softer.

He took the sock out, glared at both pieces of metal, and packed them anyway. The rattle followed them into the lean-to’s dark like a small stubborn animal.

Lumi got her sleeve wet helping and muttered at it. Niko sat with the maps open on his thighs.

“If the river moved,” he said, “does the mile-marker move with it, or does the Guild pretend the road is the true thing and the water is—”

“Decoration,” Lumi said. “Sorry. Finishing again. But yes. Textbook assumes the ground stays. It never says that. It just never mentions the other option.”

“Which is?” Bram asked from flat on his back, hat already over his face.

“That the ground doesn’t,” Niko said.

Bram made a sound that might have been interest. His boot was still half on. He didn’t follow up.

“Sleep,” Marta said. “Argue with paper in the morning.”

Niko rolled the maps. Above the lean-tos, the first stars showed between branches. He slept badly, the kettle giving one last soft complaint when someone turned over.

---

Morning came with mist and somebody else’s porridge smell.

Bram sat up, looked at the sky, and said, “Morning.”

It was morning. Niko still found it funny.

They left before the Scholars finished deciding which lid was correct. The Survivors were already gone. The onion lean-to kept its secrets.

At the barrel, Bram filled the kettle and tasted the water with two fingers.

“Good.”

“For what?” Lumi asked.

Bram tied the kettle on and started walking.

Niko never found out what that meant either. He was beginning to suspect he never would.

On the way out, Niko paused at the Guild post and reread *If the grove goes silent, leave.*

“Have you been in a silent grove?” he asked Marta.

“Yes.”

“What was it like?”

“Silent.”

“That’s not—”

“It was enough,” Marta said, and took the south fork where the Trade Road narrowed.

---

The trees changed their minds about spacing.

Not all at once. First the shoulder of the road grew roots that refused to stay buried. Then the light came down in thinner coins. Then the chalk marks on trunks stopped being numbers and started being trail signs: a hooked line for Salt Cut, a coil for Pilgrim Coil, a burned slash someone had tried to scrub off and failed.

Lumi pointed at the slash.

“Red Scar. Conqueror path. Unstable. We’re not—”

“We’re not,” Marta said.

“I was informing again.”

“Inform quieter.”

Bram crouched without warning at the verge. Pale shapes dotted the crystal moss—small, pale, upright.

“Those look poisonous.”

Lumi leaned in, already shaking her head.

“They’re flowers.”

“Hm.”

Bram stood and kept walking. No apology. No joke.

Lumi scribbled *early flowering — academy chart late?* anyway. Niko chalked where the 1987 grassland ink began to lie about how many trees there were.

“Forest already,” he said.

“We’re camping before we lose the light to it,” Marta said, still walking.

---

They met the road’s idea of itself ending at a fork post.

Salt Cut: shorter, harvester-rutted, smelled faintly of old smoke and dried meat.  
Pilgrim Coil: longer, swept needles, a ribbon faded almost white tied at head height.

Marta took Pilgrim Coil without a vote.

“Why not the short one?” Niko asked, because he couldn’t not.

“Because short paths are where people hurry,” Marta said. “Hurrying people miss the ground. You’re here to look at ground.”

“That’s almost a compliment,” Lumi said.

“Don’t get used to it.”

Pilgrim Coil was narrow enough that they walked mostly single file. Needles underfoot. Bird noise. Once, farther off, a crack of something heavy moving—then nothing. Marta’s head turned toward the sound and away again.

A pilgrim coming the other way—gray sash, empty hands—stepped off the path to let them pass and touched two fingers to his brow. Marta did the same. Lumi tried, bumped her crate, and turned it into an awkward wave. The pilgrim smiled like he’d seen worse manners and went on.

“Do we owe him something?” Niko asked.

“Space,” Marta said. “That’s the Coil.”

Deer tracks crossed the path, neat and unhurried, then stopped where a young tree leaned oddly over the trail. The trunk wasn’t broken. The lean was gentle.

“The tracks quit,” Niko said.

“The moss under it is flowering early too,” Lumi said.

Marta glanced at the sky through the canopy.

“We’re still making the next shelf before dark. Move.”

Bram looked at the lean and kept walking.

A jay called once from somewhere left of the path.

Niko chalked *young tree leans — no break — tracks stop*. Lumi bumped his elbow, stole a dried fruit piece from the bag at Bram’s hip without asking, and kept walking. Bram noticed three steps later.

---

By midday the Coil widened into a needle-strewn shelf with room to sit. Marta allowed water. Bram set the fruit bag down and immediately lost two pieces to Lumi and one to Marta. He looked at Niko.

“You want one before they invent ownership?”

Niko took a piece. Sweet. A little dusty.

He checked the maps against what he could see.

1839 wanted forest here. It was getting forest.  
1987 wanted grass and a clean road. The clean road was becoming a suggestion.  
1712 wanted river. There was a damp smell downhill to the west, or maybe wishful thinking.

“Which map are we on?” he asked.

Marta capped her waterskin.

“The one with boots on it.”

“Maybe all three,” Lumi said, around fruit. “If the ground changed under the surveys. Then none of them are wrong. They’re just dated.”

Niko tapped the 1712 river with a fingernail.

“If we’re on all three, where’s the water?”

“Underground,” Lumi said. “Or sideways. Or I’m wrong and we should stop sounding clever before Marta—”

Marta stood.

“Hunter Forest proper after the next bend. Stay on the Coil. Don’t follow pretty moss off the path. Don’t follow parting trees if you see any.”

“Parting trees?” Niko asked.

“Later,” Marta said.

They walked.

Around the bend, the Trade Road’s last wide thought ended. Trees closed ranks. The air cooled. Light caught in hanging resin threads between two trunks—thin, gold—and then the path turned and it was gone.

Behind them, Mile-Three was already just a place they had eaten.

Ahead, the forest held whatever year it felt like holding.
