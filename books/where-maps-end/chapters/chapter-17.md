# Chapter 17 — The Well Hums Wrong

They camped in the Undercroft because Marta wanted stone over her head and Bram wanted somewhere the wind couldn’t insult his fire.

Niko slept badly and blamed the shaft. It breathed all night, in and out, slow enough that you only caught it if you stopped listening on purpose.

Pell arrived at mid-morning, coming down the atrium stair sideways like someone who had run out of the good kind of tired two days ago.

The Survivor with the torn sleeve. From the salt post.

“You chalk like a lunatic,” she said. “Arrows under rocks. Who does that.”

“It worked,” Niko said.

“It worked,” she agreed, and then had to sit down.

Bram put a bowl in her hands before she got as far as her news. She started to say something about time and he pointed at the bowl until she ate. Her eyes went red over the steam and stayed that way for a while, and Marta looked at the ceiling and let her have it.

Then Pell told them.

“Your trench went in the night. Not overrun — *under.* Pale came up on the far side like it walked beneath. Sheet’s in the ditch above the trade shoulder now. Beetles are loud enough to hear from the road.” She scraped the bowl. “And the roads are full. Everybody who was going to leave has decided today’s the day, all at once, with carts. Wolves like that. Wolves like a crowd that keeps dropping things.”

“How long,” Marta said.

“Before what?”

“Before Mossgate’s south approaches are a place people die on.”

Pell thought about lying. Niko watched her decide not to.

“Two days,” she said. “If the ditch holds. It won’t hold.”

Lumi had gone very still with her book half-open.

“The ditch is dry,” she said. “That’s the whole— it’s a dry ditch. It’s been dry since the road was surveyed. We put a trench next to a dry ditch and called it water management.” She shut the book. “We were bandaging a mouth. The problem is the throat.”

“Say that in a way that gives me a job,” Marta said.

“I don’t have one yet.”

“Then get one.”

---

Pell knew wells.

She said everybody knew wells where she came from, which turned out not to be true of anyone else present.

“You listen to them,” she said, kneeling at the lip of the shaft. “Don’t shout down. Shouting’s for people who want to hear themselves. You drop a stone and you take the note it gives back.”

“And the note means?”

“Water’s got a level. Rock’s got a shape. Change the level, you change the note.” She shrugged. “My gran could tell you dry from wet and wet from *going*. I can tell you dry from wet on a good day.”

She dropped a stone.

The shaft hummed.

Not an echo — a hum, low and thick, the whole column of the thing sounding at once, like someone had bowed a rope. It sat in Niko’s teeth. Lumi made a noise that was more or less a swear.

“That’s low,” Pell said. Her face didn’t change but her hands did. “That’s a stressed note. Water’s sunk and the rock’s dry a long way down and it’s pulling from wherever it can reach.”

“Do it again,” Niko said.

She did it again.

The note came back the same, and Lumi timed it with her fingers on her wrist, and the third time she timed it with her fingers on her wrist and her lips moving, and the fourth time she said, “Stop. Stop, everyone be quiet, stop.”

They stopped.

She dropped the stone herself, badly, and it clipped the wall going down and hummed anyway.

“It’s too long,” she said.

“Too long how,” Marta said.

“The note runs on. Past when it should be done. A shaft this wide, that stone, that fall time — I can guess the depth within a bit, and the hum should die about—” she snapped her fingers “—there. It doesn’t. It goes on for three, four times that. Like the note gets down to the bottom and finds out the bottom isn’t the bottom.”

Nobody said anything.

Niko went down on his stomach at the lip with his ear over the hole, which Marta permitted with visible cost.

He heard the tail of it. Long and soft and getting softer, the way a shout goes out over a lake at night and never quite ends, it just gets too quiet to argue with.

“There’s a room down there,” he said.

“There’s a *drop* down there,” Marta said.

“Both.” He sat up. “Marta, listen to the whole thing. The cistern used to send water three ways. Somebody capped it. The Trade Road ditch is dry and the shelf above the salt post is dying of thirst and this garden is drinking its own cup and the well says the water table under all of it has gone somewhere.” He heard himself getting fast and didn’t stop. “It’s one system. The ditch and the shelf and the garden. It’s one thing, and it’s failing from the bottom, and the bottom is further down than this floor.”

Lumi was nodding hard enough to hurt herself.

“He’s right, that’s— the shelf failing from underneath, that’s not a stain, that’s a water table, that’s—”

“I heard him,” Marta said.

The Jester had said nothing for a remarkable length of time. He was sitting on the cistern rim with his stick across his knees and his head cocked at the shaft.

“That’s a long note for a short hole,” he said.

“Yes,” Lumi said. “Thank you. That’s what I said, with numbers.”

“Numbers.” He looked pleased for her in a way that had nothing to do with numbers. “Wells don’t lie, you know. They just answer a bigger question than the one you asked.” He hopped off the rim. “Ignore me. I’m sentimental about holes.”

---

The wolves came at the atrium stair while they were still arguing.

Three, then a fourth, lean the way the spur ones had been lean, coming down out of the garden’s green light into the Undercroft dark because the dark smelled like five warm bodies and a cooking pot.

The stair was the good news. One at a time was the good news.

Marta took the bottom step and made it a door. Knife low, shoulder set, boot planted on the worn lip. The first wolf came down fast and met her and went back up faster, opened along the ribs. The second tried the side of the stair where the root scaffolding made a ramp, and Bram’s lid caught it across the muzzle with a sound that filled the whole Undercroft twice over — the roots did that, took a noise and gave it back doubled.

“*Left*—” Niko said, seeing the third go wide for the gap between two ribs.

Pell was already there with a spear-butt in its chest.

Lumi’s spark cracked in the gap and the light came off the pale roots and made the whole space flash white for half a breath, and the wolves decided the doubled noise and the white flash and the four sharp things were somebody else’s garden.

They went. One left blood on the third step.

Bram looked at his lid. “I want it on record that this lid has now been in more fights than I have.”

“It’s the same fights, Bram.”

“It’s doing more of the work.”

Marta wiped her knife and stood at the bottom of the stair a moment longer than she needed to.

Then she went to her pack and took something out of the top of it, small and knotted, and put it in her breast pocket. Bram found a reason to be busy with the rope.

“Rope,” she said. “All of it. Pell — you don’t come.”

“I wasn’t going to offer.”

“You’re going to carry paper instead.” Marta jerked her chin at Niko. “Copy everything. The rim warnings, the trench, the capped channels, what the well said. Two copies. One goes with her, one stays with us. If the shelf takes the shoulder and we’re still under a garden, somebody upstairs should know what we were doing down here instead of helping.”

“That’s morbid,” Bram said.

“That’s filing.”

Niko copied until his hand cramped. Pell rolled the sheets into her sleeve — the torn one, tied off around them — and stood in the atrium light looking at the five of them for a second too long.

“Mossgate’s hanging maps early,” she said. “Whole families. The Wall’s got no room left at eye height.” She hitched her pack. “Don’t hang yours yet.”

Then she was up the stair and gone.

---

Marta tied the first anchor around a root rib and tested it. Untied it. Tied it again. Tested it. Untied it and tied it a third time while Bram very carefully did not comment, and Lumi very carefully found something to write down.

Niko held the coil and watched her hands and understood that she had been in a hole before and had come out of it with fewer people than she went in with.

“You don’t have to be the first one down,” he said.

“I do, actually.” She hauled the knot. “It’s not bravery. If it holds me it holds all of you.”

“That’s not—”

“Niko.” She looked up. “What’s the note when it’s good?”

He blinked. “What?”

“The well. Pell says the note’s stressed. Fine. When we’ve done whatever it is we’re about to do — what does a good note sound like?”

He hadn’t thought about it. He thought about it now, and it was the first hopeful arithmetic he’d done in a week.

“Higher,” he said. “Shorter. Water sitting closer to the top.”

“Good.” She fed the rope over the lip. “Then that’s the job. Make the hole sing higher.”

The Jester leaned over the shaft, sniffed the air coming up out of it, and made a small surprised noise, as though a room he'd expected to smell of dust smelled of weather instead.

“Well,” he said, to no one. “That’s not the cellar.”
