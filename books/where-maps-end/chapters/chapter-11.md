# Chapter 11 — The Upside-Down Fall

The courtyard took them before the light finished leaving.

Broken walls made a crooked ring. Weeds held the seams. Old survey nails still bit stone here and there, stubborn as Guild stamps. The sky sat open above like a roof that had given up politely.

Marta checked the gaps with her eyes the way Bram checked a pot for cracks. Lumi set her crate down and sat on it as if the books had negotiated the pause. Bram hung the pot from a stub of iron that might once have been a hook for something less edible.

“Walls,” he said.

“Walls,” Marta agreed. “Inner court only. No exploring teeth in the dark.”

Niko walked the inner circuit once with chalk ready and found nothing worth a full sheet—just scrap: *fortress court — nails — grove below still silent — Stag left via parting — wolves pressed.* His hand wanted to write more. The light refused.

Bram made stew with actual weight, as promised. Onions from Root Camp. Lake fish dried thin. Something green he refused to name until after they’d eaten it.

“Was that—” Lumi began, after.

“Food,” Bram said.

“I was going to ask the plant.”

“The plant already answered.”

Marta ate standing, back to a wall-tooth, watching the open gate-gap that faced the ridge. Niko ate sitting, maps rolled under his knee so they wouldn’t blow. Far below, the Silent Grove kept its removed sound. Ordinary night noises lived only on the ridge—wind, a night bird that sounded unsure of its own job, the soft complaint of Bram’s kettle learning a new nail.

“Morning,” Bram told the kettle, because the hour was wrong and habits didn’t care.

Lumi laughed once into her bowl.

“It’s night.”

“I’m practicing optimism,” Bram said.

Niko looked at the scrap with the Stag note and felt the chalk dust on his thumb. Soft roots. Bleach. Shore. Parting. The list had grown teeth without becoming a speech.

“If the Stag’s leaving,” he said, “the wolves don’t go home. They go somewhere else.”

“Roads,” Marta said. “People. Loud food.”

“Mossgate,” Lumi said, then winced at her own sentence. “Not tomorrow. Not— I don’t know the speed.”

“Nobody does,” Marta said. “That’s why we sleep behind walls and leave clever for daylight.”

Bram offered seconds without asking who wanted them. Lumi took half. Niko took a spoon and then remembered he was still holding chalk and had to choose a hand.

“If we hang a map after this,” Lumi said, “what do we write about the Stag? ‘Saw it’ feels small.”

“Small is honest,” Marta said. “Big words are for people who didn’t shake.”

“I shook,” Bram said. “My pot shook. We are a shaking expedition.”

“Good,” Marta said. “Shaking means you noticed.”

A beetle clicked somewhere in the weed seam. Bram listened as if it might request stew. It didn’t. Niko chalked a tiny mark anyway: *beetle — court weeds — ordinary?* He underlined the question mark until the chalk broke a little.

They slept in a lean of canvas against inner stone. Marta took first watch. Niko woke once to the sound of her knife tip tapping a nail—counting, or thinking, or keeping the dark honest. He rolled over and let the tap become weather.

---

Dawn made the broken edges look almost kind.

Bram said “Morning” and meant it. Marta had already checked the packs. Lumi had already dropped a book and blamed gravity with academic confidence.

“We need water that isn’t rumor,” Marta said. “Ridge cleft past the outer teeth. Old marks point that way. We look. We don’t invent side quests.”

“I never invent,” Lumi said. “I discover by accident.”

“Same mess. Quieter name.”

They left the court through a gap that had once been a gate and was now a shrug. The ridge path beyond held more nails, some bent, one still flying a scrap of cloth that had forgotten its letters. Below, the hollow of the Silent Grove stayed emptied of sound like a held debt.

Niko did not look into it long. Looking felt like knocking.

The path bent around a fallen wall-tooth and opened into a cleft the maps had never bothered to argue about—too small for Guild pride, too odd for honest ink.

Water fell upward.

Not spray. Not a trick of wind. A full fall of bright water climbing a stone face as if the world had hung the river wrong and refused to apologize. Mist gathered at the top instead of the bottom. Stones at the foot were dry as old bread. The sound was almost ordinary—rush, pour, slap—except it traveled in the wrong direction through Niko’s bones.

Nobody spoke for a while.

Crystal pollen from somewhere drifted through the mist and glittered like a second, lazier waterfall.

“That’s—” Lumi started, stopped, corrected herself with her whole face. “I don’t have a page for that.”

“Good,” Marta said. “Pages would lie.”

Bram set the pot down carefully, as if manners mattered to physics.

“Can we drink it?” he asked.

Niko watched a droplet climb a fern frond, hesitate, then keep going up.

“You can drink almost anything once,” Lumi said, then looked alarmed at her own mouth. “No. Wait. Don’t. I don’t know what ‘once’ does here.”

Bram nodded, satisfied with caution as a seasoning.

Niko chalked without pretending the chalk could hold it: *cleft past fortress — water falls up — mist at top — dry foot — leave measured for later.* His hand shook less than at the Stag. Wonder was a different kind of weight.

Beyond the upside-down fall, past the mist shelf where a sane river would have been stone and spray, lights hung in the farther dark of distance—small, steady, wrong for campfires and wrong for stars. Not many. Enough. Like windows someone had forgotten to turn off in a place that shouldn’t have windows.

Niko’s chest did a stupid hopeful thing.

“Do you see—”

“I see,” Marta said.

Lumi squinted, glasses useless against mist and want.

“We could—”

“No,” Marta said.

Not unkind. Not soft. A door closed with a hand still on the latch.

Bram squinted too, then looked at the pot as if it might volunteer.

“They’re pretty,” he said.

“Pretty gets people killed,” Marta said. “Pretty also gets people late. We’re survey, not curiosity tourists. We came for maps that disagree. We sleep, we chalk, we go where the cascade is chewing—not where lights wink.”

Niko wanted to argue. The lights wanted him to argue. The fall wanted him to stand forever with his mouth open.

He rolled the scrap so the upside-down note sat beside the Stag witness.

“We leave them,” he said, and hated how adult it sounded in his own voice.

“We leave them,” Marta confirmed. “Write that you saw. Write that you didn’t go. That’s the kind of honesty Walls keep.”

Lumi hugged her crate.

“I hate being responsible.”

“Practice,” Marta said. “It fits worse before it fits.”

They filled skins from a side seep that fell the normal way, shamefaced and ordinary. Bram tasted first.

“Good,” he said.

“For what?” Lumi asked.

“For going down.”

Niko glanced once more at the climbing water and the patient lights beyond. The lights did not beckon. They only existed. That was worse. Beckoning you could refuse with drama. Existence you carried.

On the path back toward the fortress spur that would take them around the hollow, glass-wolf gray flickered in scrub—two lean shapes, not a pack charge, testing the ridge the way hunger tests a door.

Marta’s knife was already in her hand.

“Not a fight if they don’t ask,” she said. “If they ask—”

The nearer wolf asked.

It came low for the pot smell, ribs sharp, eyes too bright. Marta met it with boot and flat of blade—noise and line, not glory. Bram’s lid rang once off shoulder-glass. Lumi’s spark cracked the scrub. Niko shouted the second wolf’s angle off the rock rib before it completed the pincer.

They did not kill. They did not need to. The pair peeled away toward easier deer memory, leaving a scratch on Marta’s greave and a new dent opinion in Bram’s lid.

“Fixable,” Bram said, checking canvas and pride.

Niko chalked: *ridge wolves — lean — pressed still — driven off — lights left beyond up-fall.*

“Cascade weather,” Lumi said quietly. “They’re already shopping for new rooms.”

“Then we move before Mossgate becomes a room,” Marta said. “There’s a glass ruin marked on an old cutter scrap from the quarry road—domes, irrigation, trees through glass. Not on our three Guild sheets. If the basin’s habits are sick, places that still run might tell us how. We look. We don’t wake anything that snores unless I say.”

“Things snore in ruins?” Bram asked.

“Everything snores in ruins,” Marta said. “Including bad ideas.”

Niko thought of soft root fibers in a tunnel that wasn’t cut. Of bleach bowls. Of a forest that bowed for something leaving. Of lights he had agreed not to touch.

He touched the chalk stub in his pocket instead.

They left the upside-down fall behind them singing the wrong way up the stone, and the distant lights stayed distant—an open page they refused to turn.

Ahead, the ridge path bent toward marks that promised glass and green and questions with worse manners than wolves.
