# Chapter 8 — If They Weren’t Wrong

Ferry camp was lanterns, wet rope, and stew that finally had weight.

Bram looked offended by how good it was, then accepted destiny and asked for seconds. A ferry hand dealt cards badly at the next ring. Lumi lost to Marta twice and accused the deck of academic bias. Night insects stitched the dark between boats. Somewhere a dog barked at a reflection and lost interest.

Before the cards, there had been the small ordinary work of stopping. Boots off. Socks near the fire but not in it. Rope counted. Water tasted. Bram had borrowed a pot-ring and returned someone’s ladle cleaner than he found it, which made the ferry hand stare as if cleanliness were a foreign creed.

“You can stay,” the hand had said.

“We are staying,” Marta had said.

“I mean you’re the good kind of survey.”

“There’s a kind?”

“There’s always a kind.”

Lumi had lost the first hand of cards before the stew was ready and claimed the deck smelled of bias. Marta had won without smiling. Bram had watched the pot instead of the cards and somehow still given advice that helped nobody.

“Salt,” he had said, to the game or the stew. Nobody asked which.

Niko sat with the maps on a crate and the lake’s black sheet breathing behind him. He had chalked the shoreline again after supper until Marta took the stub out of his hand and put it in his pocket like confiscating a toy.

“Eat,” she had said.

He had eaten. The stew still sat warm in him. The maps still sat wrong in his lap.

Bram stirred the borrowed pot and did not pretend not to watch Niko’s face. Marta sharpened a stake. Lumi sat on her crate with a pencil and the look of someone waiting for a sentence to arrive.

“Say it,” Lumi said.

“Say what?”

“The face. You’ve been wearing it since the shore.”

Bram made a show of tasting stew that did not need tasting.

“If he doesn’t say it, the pot will,” Bram said. “Pots hate secrets. They boil them out.”

“That’s not how pots work,” Lumi said.

“It’s how this pot works.”

Niko tapped the three locus marks—same oval, three lies, or three truths.

“What if they weren’t wrong?”

Lumi’s pencil stopped.

“The Guild maps,” Niko said. “1712 river. 1839 forest. 1987 grass. Same place. Everyone says error. Clerk said mistake. Root Camp said human error. What if each one was right when it was drawn?”

Bram frowned at onions in the pot that were already gone.

“Ground doesn’t change that fast.”

“Doesn’t it?” Niko asked. “Dry ditch where the map wants riverbank. Parting trees. Shore that walks. Tunnel that isn’t cut. Lumi’s chart failed a moss sample.”

“That’s a printing—” Lumi began, then wilted. “Maybe not only printing.”

Marta set the stake down.

“You’re saying the basin moves.”

“I’m saying the maps might be honest and the years are the difference,” Niko said. “I don’t know how. I don’t know what moves it. I only know boot prints keep disagreeing with proud ink, and proud ink disagrees with itself across time.”

He spread the three sheets side by side on the crate. Same oval. Different worlds. The lantern made the ink look wet.

“Mile-Three ditch,” he said, mostly to keep his hands busy. “Post where riverbank should be. Parting trees off the Coil. Soft roots in a tunnel that isn’t on any year. Shore that won’t pick a map. A brightness chart that loses to a plug labeled RC-12.”

“That’s a list,” Marta said.

“Lists are how I think.”

“Lists are how you worry,” Marta said. “Close enough. Keep going.”

The ferry hand at the next ring laughed at a bad card. Nobody in their circle laughed with him.

---

Lumi spoke first, careful.

“If geography used to… rearrange… then surveys would look like contradictions when stacked. Academy hates that sentence. It sounds like folklore.”

“Folklore had grandparents saying the beach walked,” Niko said.

“Folklore also says don’t go east,” Marta said. “And that one’s useful even if nobody explains it.”

“Useful how?” Lumi asked.

“People who ignore useful warnings don’t come back to argue,” Marta said. “I like arguments that continue.”

Bram tasted the stew again, unnecessarily.

“If the ground moves, do the onions move with it?”

“Bram,” Lumi said.

“I’m serious. Food grows where land is. If land was river, then forest, then grass, then the lunch changes. That’s not philosophy. That’s a menu.”

Niko almost smiled.

“So you believe me.”

“I believe dinner,” Bram said. “You’re close enough.”

Lumi pulled her knees up.

“If you’re right, every textbook map is a photograph of a moment, not a law. That makes my whole—” She waved at her crate. “—hobby into weather.”

“Weather’s real,” Marta said.

“Weather’s annoying.”

“So are maps,” Marta said. “We still use them.”

Niko looked at the 1712 sheet. The river-mouth lines felt less like a lie and more like a memory someone else had paid for.

“I keep thinking the Wall maps aren’t wrong at each other,” he said. “They’re late to each other.”

“That’s a prettier sentence,” Marta said. “Don’t trust pretty.”

“I’m not trying to be pretty.”

“I know. That’s why I said it.”

Bram pointed his spoon at the dark water.

“If the shore walks, does the fortress sit still?”

“Ask it when we get there,” Marta said.

“I will ask the stew first,” Bram said. “Stew answers faster.”

Lumi chewed her pencil, remembered it was chalk-adjacent and bad for teeth, and stopped.

“Root Camp will say human error again,” she said. “Even if we bring them a walking beach.”

“Then we don’t bring them a speech,” Marta said. “We bring scraps. Boots. Dates. Let them hate the paper, not us.”

“I can live with that,” Niko said.

“You can walk with that,” Marta said. “Living comes later.”

---

They let the fire talk for a while. Bram cleaned the pot with the care of a man rewriting a letter. Lumi lost a third card game without playing—Marta had put the deck away. A moth bumped the lantern and left a soft dust of itself on the glass.

Niko rolled and unrolled the maps once, uselessly, then stopped.

“Keep noticing,” Marta said, quieter. She looked at the dark water. “Don’t decide you’re right until the ground agrees in daylight.”

“I wasn’t deciding,” Niko said. “I was asking.”

“Good. Asking keeps people alive longer than deciding.”

“What do we do with the asking?” Lumi said. “Besides write it where Root Camp can’t grade it.”

“We walk,” Marta said. “We sleep where boats can see us. We don’t chase every hole that looks interesting. We test one thing at a time.”

“Crystal Caverns next?” Niko asked.

“Ridge marks after, if the nails hold. Fortress if we’re lucky. Stupid if we’re not.”

Bram sat back on his heels.

“If the years are the difference,” he said, “then lunch in 1712 was fish, lunch in 1839 was deer, lunch in 1987 was grass and complaints.”

“That’s not how grassland works,” Lumi said.

“It’s how hunger works.”

Niko almost laughed. The almost was enough.

Lumi wrote on the crate lid because her book was full: *hypothesis — maps right at date / basin changed. Do not publish. Do not tell Root Camp yet. Tell Niko’s boots.*

She glanced up.

“If you’re right, something made it change. Or stopped making it change.”

“Later,” Marta said, but softer than usual.

“Later keeps getting heavier,” Lumi said.

“That’s what later does,” Marta said. “We carry what we can. We leave what kills us.”

Niko rolled the maps. Out on the lake, a fish jumped. The Singing River’s memory still hung in his ears, thin gold. For the first time since Mossgate, the blank sheet on the Wall felt like it might someday deserve ink that wasn’t a compromise.

A ferry hand walked past and nodded at the stew pot like greeting a relative. Bram nodded back. Marta checked the stake once more and set it where ankles wouldn’t find it in the dark.

Bram said “Morning” to the night, habit-drunk, and nobody corrected him.

Lumi put her pencil behind her ear and missed. The pencil rolled under the crate. She left it there until morning.

Before sleep, Niko walked the edge of the camp ring once. Lanterns. Wet rope. The lake’s black breath. Behind him, Bram muttered recipe adjustments to a pot that had already forgiven him. Lumi retrieved her pencil from under the crate and immediately lost it again in a sleeve. Marta sat with her back to a post, eyes half closed, listening for the kind of quiet that meant trouble rather than rest.

“Still asking?” she said without opening her eyes.

“Yes.”

“Good. Stop before you start answering alone.”

“I wasn’t—”

“You were arranging,” Marta said. “I can hear it from here.”

Niko came back to the crate. The maps waited. The hypothesis waited. The blank sheet on the Wall waited in a town he couldn’t see.

He slept with the question intact.

He did not sleep with an answer.
