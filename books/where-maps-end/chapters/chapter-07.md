# Chapter 7 — Where the Shore Walks

The trees gave up before the water did.

The quarry road had become lake road without announcing the change—less stone, more mud, then a brightness ahead that made Lumi shield her eyes with a book. Bram complained that brightness wasn’t lunch. Marta said brightness was a warning that people would try to sell them fish. Both were correct.

The warning arrived early.

Three lean figures stepped out of the scrub where the mud thickened—not fishers, not Guild. Pack-thieves’ eyes. One had a hooked stick. One had a knife that had known better days. The third hung back with a sling and bad timing.

“Toll,” the hooked one said. “Lake road’s expensive.”

“No,” Marta said.

The hooked stick came for Bram’s pot strap. Bram let the pot swing with the grab and turned the thief’s own pull into a faceful of iron. Marta stepped inside the knife’s arc and put the man in the mud with a short, ugly trip. Lumi’s spark cracked the air above the sling—wrong aim for killing, perfect for making a wrist open and drop.

Niko saw the third figure cut wide for Lumi’s crate. “Crate—”

Marta’s boot found the man’s knee before the crate found teeth or hands. Bram sat on the hooked one’s back long enough to count to three, then stood, pot still honest.

“Lunch,” Bram said to nobody, breathing hard. “Still mine.”

The three left the way desperate people leave—fast, without dignity, without the packs. The sling stone lay in the mud like a failed argument.

“Anyone hurt?” Marta asked.

“Mud,” Lumi said, looking at her sleeve. “And my sense of lake hospitality.”

“Hospitality’s at the dock,” Marta said. “Move before they grow friends.”

They moved.

---

Great Lake arrived as light first—wider sky, cooler air—then as a sheet of steel-blue that made Niko’s maps feel small. Fisher sheds clung to a dock like barnacles. Nets dried. Someone shouted about rope prices. A gull stole something and was cursed with professional warmth. It smelled like honest work and fish that had opinions.

Bram inhaled like a religious man.

“Lunch has a hometown,” he said.

“Don’t marry it,” Marta said.

“I’m loyal to pots.”

Marta bought ferry tokens without asking who wanted to go. Lumi dropped a book and a fisher caught it without looking, handed it back, and kept mending net.

“Thank you,” Lumi said.

“Books float worse than people,” the fisher said. “Don’t test either.”

They stood a moment on the dock boards while the lake wind rearranged Lumi’s hair and Bram’s patience. Niko already had the three Guild sheets half open. Marta sighed the sigh of someone who had expected that.

“Ten minutes,” she said. “Then ferry. Then you can invent pebbles later.”

“I’m not inventing—”

“Surveying. I know. Ten minutes.”

Bram found a barrel to lean the pot against and immediately began judging fish by smell. A child offered him a stone. He offered the child a crumb. The trade was considered fair by both parties and by no adults.

Lumi asked a net-mender if the lake had a preferred season for honesty. The net-mender said “winter” and then “never” and then laughed like both were true.

---

Niko walked the shore with the sheets open against the wind. Bram trailed him with the pot as a windbreak. Lumi watched the meter, then the water, then the meter again because the water was winning.

1712 wanted river-mouth here.  
1839 wanted forest to the waterline.  
1987 wanted a neat beach and a road stub.

Where he stood, the water licked stones that weren’t on any of them the same way. Ten paces north, the beach matched 1987. Ten paces south, roots and drowned trunks argued for 1839. The fisher kids skipped stones from a spit that 1712 would’ve called mid-channel.

Niko chalked until his fingers cramped.

“You’re going to invent a fourth map,” Marta called.

“I’m inventing accuracy,” Niko said.

“Same disease,” Bram said, almost fond.

A fisher woman watched him. Not mocking. Tired-amused. She had a knife and a basket and the patience of someone who had explained weather to outsiders before.

“The shore walks,” she said. “Grandparents said it. We say the water’s rude. Same story.”

“Walks how?” Niko asked.

“Out. In. Steals a path. Gives back a mudflat. Kids grow up learning which year the dock was honest.”

“Does it walk east?” Niko asked, and regretted the word.

She shrugged toward the far spit.

“We don’t go that end much. Bad luck. Or good sense. Pick one.”

Lumi looked up from her book.

“Is there a measured rate? Seasonal? Storm-driven? Substrate shift?”

The fisher woman blinked.

“It moves when it moves. We move nets.”

“That’s… not a rate.”

“It’s a living.”

Bram, from a polite distance: “Does rude water still make edible fish?”

“Rude water makes the best fish,” the woman said. “Polite water is boring.”

Niko chalked *shore walks — grandparents / rude water — east spit avoided* on scrap and pinned it before the wind could steal the thought.

Marta’s hand found his shoulder—light, redirecting.

“Ferry. Before the lake writes a fifth opinion.”

Bram bought dried fish with the gravity he reserved for onions. The seller called him “cook” without being told. Bram accepted the title like a knighthood.

---

The ferry was a flat barge with a patient mule of a motor. Midcrossing, the lake wind stole Lumi’s loose page. Bram caught it with the pot lid like he’d been training for that exact sport. Lumi stared at him.

“Thank you.”

“It had writing,” Bram said. “Writing’s edible to the lake.”

“Pages aren’t food.”

“Everything’s food once,” Bram said, then frowned. “Don’t write that down.”

“Too late,” Lumi said, and didn’t write it.

Niko watched the receding dock and the spit that people didn’t favor. He did not stare long. He did not dress it in smoke. He put it next to *Don’t go east* in his head and left both doors closed.

A ferry hand offered Marta a card game for the crossing. She declined with a look that ended hobbies. Bram offered the hand a piece of dried fish instead. That worked better.

Lumi tried to explain substrate to the ferry hand. The ferry hand explained rope. They both nodded as if translation had occurred.

Midlake, the wind flattened the maps against Niko’s chest. He held them there and watched the dock shrink. Marta stood with her weight easy, as if barges were just another kind of road. Bram guarded the pot lid like a tournament shield. Lumi counted whitecaps and lost count and started again because counting felt like control.

“Do you think the shore walks on both sides?” Niko asked.

“I think you’re going to check,” Marta said.

“That’s not an answer.”

“It’s a schedule.”

---

On the far side, the trail bent toward a sound that wasn’t wind.

Water over stone—but tuned. Each rock in the shallows rang a different note as current passed, a messy music that somehow stayed gentle. No one conducted. No one explained.

The Singing River.

Lumi stopped talking.

Bram set the pot down and listened with his whole face.

Marta’s mouth did the almost-smile it had done for the ladle in Mossgate.

Niko stood in the spray-cool air and let the maps hang forgotten in his hand. The river did not care which year the Guild had stamped. It sang because stones and speed existed. Light broke on wet rock. A dragonfly hung over a note and left without taking it.

For a while nobody surveyed. Nobody corrected. The current did the talking and the stones answered without pride.

A child from a ferry family ran past, barefoot, and shouted the notes out of order on purpose. An adult told them to quit. The child did not quit.

Bram tried to match a low note with the pot rim and failed. Lumi tried with her voice and failed better. Marta did not try. Niko watched a leaf ride a bright seam of water until it spun and vanished under a singing stone.

They walked the bank until the music thinned to ordinary water again. No clue attached itself. No monster rose. Wonder spent itself and left a clean place in Niko’s chest.

“Beautiful,” Lumi said finally, inadequate and true.

“Loud lunch,” Bram said, also true in his language.

“We could camp here,” Niko said.

“We could drown in pretty,” Marta said. “We sleep where the boats can see us.”

She pointed them toward the ferry dock’s camp ring.

“Tomorrow you can argue with shores again.”

On the walk back, Lumi hummed one wrong note from the river and looked embarrassed when Bram hummed a worse one to match. Marta did not hum. Niko almost did, and didn’t, and kept the almost.

They passed a fisherman mending a net who nodded at Marta’s two fingers and got the same back. Ordinary. Good.

At the camp ring, ferry hands were already stringing lanterns. Someone offered Marta a dry patch near the boat rail. She took it. Bram claimed a cook-ring with the confidence of a man who had been born owning fire. Lumi sat on her crate and wrote *Singing River — wonder — no rate — child out of tune on purpose* and then underlined *wonder* so she wouldn’t turn it into homework later.

Bram started a thin supper with the dried fish and a pinch of Root Camp salt that had survived the quarry. The smell pulled a ferry cat close; the cat received nothing and left with dignity intact. Marta checked rope. Niko checked chalk. Ordinary work after extraordinary water.

Niko walked the shore one last time while the light went copper. Ten paces north still liked 1987. Ten paces south still argued for 1839. The spit the fishers avoided still sat where luck and sense shared a fence.

“You’ll wear a trench with your boots,” Marta said behind him.

“I’m matching years to mud.”

“Mud wins.”

“Sometimes,” Niko said. “Sometimes paper was just early.”

Marta didn’t answer that. She handed him a strip of dried fish instead. He ate. The lake kept moving.

If the shore walked, maybe the river in 1712 had walked too.

He didn’t say it yet.

He kept it warm under his tongue like a coal.
