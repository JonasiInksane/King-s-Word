# Chapter 19 — The Wheel Still Turns

They slept on the shore of a lake under a sky that was not one, and Niko woke up sure he had overslept until he remembered there was nothing to oversleep.

Bram had the pot on. The stars hadn’t moved. Lumi had chalked a line of them on a flat stone, badly, and was comparing her drawing to the ceiling with the expression of a woman losing an argument.

“Same,” she said. “Exactly the same. I did it four hours ago and it’s the same.”

“Should it be different?”

“A *sky* would be different. That’s what a sky does, that’s the entire point of the sky, it’s a clock.” She sat back on her heels. “This one’s stopped. Or it runs slow enough that four hours is nothing to it.”

Marta divided the last of the hard bread into five and did not comment.

They walked the shore with the lanterns low, because low was enough and because none of them wanted to spoil it.

The water didn’t lap. It sat. Their boots on the shingle were the loudest thing in the world.

Bram found the drift about two hundred paces along: a long ridge of pale scurf pushed up against a rock spit, ankle deep, dry on top and wet underneath.

He crouched and picked some up and let it fall through his fingers.

“Moths,” he said. “All of them. Dead ones.”

Thousands. Wings the size of a thumbnail, silvered, the light gone out of them, heaped and packed and going grey.

“They fall,” Lumi said. “They fall when they die, we saw one fall — and the eels eat them, that’s the— the pool eats its own ceiling, that’s the whole—” She was talking to herself by then. “So why is there a pile.”

Niko went out along the spit until he could see round it.

The water on the far side of the rock wasn’t black. It was grey and slightly clouded, and a skin of pale scum lay on it, and nothing under it lit up at all.

“Something’s stopped them getting to it,” he said.

“Or stopped it getting to them,” Bram said. “Same argument. Different end of the plate.”

They found the reason at the overflow.

---

The pool ended at a stone lip, and past the lip the water went down a race, and in the race stood the wheel.

It was cart-sized and made of stone, which was wrong for a wheel and clearly hadn’t stopped anyone. Pale root ran through its spokes and out into the housing, and the housing went into the wall, and the wall went into the dark and kept going.

Niko put his hand on the rim and felt nothing.

Then he stood there long enough to be embarrassed about it, and felt it: a movement so slight it was more of a rumour, the rim easing under his palm the way a sleeping animal’s ribs ease.

“It’s turning,” he said.

Marta put her hand next to his.

“That’s not turning.”

“It’s turning. It’s just doing it in a bigger week than ours.”

The race below the wheel was choked. Dead moths, packed and matted and years deep, wadded into the gate slots where the water was supposed to run out — so the pool sat high and still and didn’t skim, and the drift piled at the spit instead of going through, and the eels went hungry on one side and gorged on the other, and the water clouded and stopped feeding anything downstream at all.

Lumi laughed. It came out wrong-shaped.

“It’s *leaves in a gutter,*” she said. “It’s the most ancient magnificent impossible— it’s leaves in a gutter. Two thousand years of leaves in a gutter.”

“It’s a house with nobody in it,” Bram said, hauling a wad of matted wings out of the slot and slapping it on the stone. “Ask any house.”

The Jester was inspecting the wheel’s housing with his hands behind his back like a man at a fair.

“Wheels don’t mind which way,” he said. “They mind stopping. Stopping is when they start having opinions.”

“Is that a yes or a no,” Marta said.

“It’s a wheel.” He straightened. “I like it. Very rude. Very patient.”

---

The work was filthy and it took hours.

They cleared the slots. Marta and Bram did most of the hauling; Niko crawled into the race with a lantern to find the second gate nobody had known was there; Lumi lay on her stomach at the lip with her arm in freezing black water, timing her spark to nothing at all and getting the seized pin to move on the fourth try without noticing she'd waited for the pool's slow settle to do it.

The wheel’s spindle took the four of them on the bar and the fifth counting.

“Now,” Niko said, and they leaned, and it did not move, and they leaned again, and it went round a hand’s breadth with a sound like a mountain clearing its throat.

Water went into the race.

Niko sat down in the shingle and watched it go, and it kept going, and somewhere in the wall the sound of it changed and deepened and became a river he couldn’t see.

“Where does it go,” he said. “Marta. Where does it *go.*”

“Down.” She was breathing hard. “Down and out. That’s what down and out means.”

“The capped arms upstairs pointed south and east.”

“I know what they pointed at.”

“The ditch. The shelf—”

“Niko.” She put her hand flat on his head like he was a dog or a much younger brother and then took it off again. “Yes. Probably. We’ll find out from the wrong end, at speed, the way we find out everything.”

Then Lumi said, “Wait — wait, no, we’ve done half of it,” and got up out of the water dripping and furious with herself.

“The pool’s going to run,” she said. “Good. The pool runs, the eels get their food back, the water clears, the head comes back, fine, wonderful. But the drift.” She pointed at the ridge of dead moths along the spit. “That’s not rubbish. That’s the *bottom of the food chain*, and it’s been piling in the wrong place for longer than Mossgate’s been a town, and if we open the gate now the whole lot goes out the race in one night and the pool gets nothing.”

“So we scatter it,” Bram said.

“We— yes. Yes, that’s exactly it, we scatter it, thank you.”

“You could have just said feed the fish.”

So they fed the fish.

They spent the back half of the day walking the shore with cook pots and Lumi’s sample crate and Bram’s good hat, scooping dead stars out of the drift and flinging them out across the black water in long pale arcs.

The eels came up.

Green light, first one, then twenty, then more than anyone counted, unspooling under the surface and rolling and taking the fallen moths down. The clouded side of the pool started to move for the first time since they’d found it.

Lumi stopped mid-throw with her arm still up.

“Early flowering,” she said.

Niko looked over.

“Root Camp. The moss on the Coil, the moss on the *shelf* — it flowered early, and I said seasonal, and my chart said seasonal, and it wasn’t.” She was staring at the ceiling. “Light. They flower to light. Not sun light — this. This comes up through the whole basin and tells everything what month it is. And this has been sitting in the dark going quiet for years because a gutter was blocked, so everything up there has been reading a clock that’s wrong.”

Nobody had anything to add to that.

Bram threw another handful of stars.

“I want to say something,” he said. “Where I’m from, when one falls, you wish.”

“Then wish,” Marta said.

“I’m wishing for a bath,” Lumi said.

“I’m wishing for daylight,” Marta said. “The real one.”

Niko threw his handful out over the water and watched the eels come up green underneath it and did not say what he wished for, which was to be allowed to come back here one day when nobody was dying.

---

The duskhawks came at the drift.

Two of them, out of the dark above, and Niko never saw them arrive — he saw the stars go out in a line, one cluster after another blinking dark as something crossed in front, and shouted before he knew why.

“Down—”

The first one came through the lantern light like a thrown axe. Grey, silent, a wingspan wider than Bram was tall, and it hit the drift where the moths were thickest and came up with its beak full and its head turning to look at them with an eye like a wet coin.

The second one went for Lumi’s crate because it was pale and moving.

Marta got there first with the pot lid, not the knife, and the noise of it in that cavern was enormous — the whole dark took the clang and handed it back from every direction at once. The hawk broke off, appalled. Bram shouted and made himself huge. Niko threw a double handful of dead moths straight up into the air in front of the second bird, which turned into it out of pure instinct, filled its mouth, and lost interest in the crate entirely.

They went back up into the black with their wings creaking.

“They hunt the thin places,” Niko said, when his heart came back. “Look — where the stars are sparse. That’s where they were sitting. They read the ceiling.”

“Everything down here reads the ceiling,” Lumi said.

Bram picked up his hat, which had a hole in it.

---

They found the Low Gate on the way back along the shore, because the pool had dropped a hand’s width once the race began to run, and a shelf of stone stood out of the water that hadn’t before.

It led to a wall. The first wall they’d touched in this place.

And set into it, low, was a door.

It was small. Smaller than the seal in the garden — you would have to stoop. Dark stone, no join Niko could see, no handle, nothing carved on it that anyone would call writing.

The cold came off it at three paces.

Not draught. Cold, standing in the air in front of it like the cold off a butcher’s slab, so that Niko’s breath fogged in a place where his breath had not fogged all day.

Roots had come to the frame. Pale root, the same as everywhere — but where the garden’s roots had grown out from their stone, and the well brackets had grown around their iron, these had gone *in.* They pressed into the frame from every side, thickened and blunted, packed into the seam like fingers into a fist.

Nobody stepped closer.

Marta held the lantern up for exactly as long as it took to see all of it, and no longer.

“If a door feels colder than the last one,” she said, “it isn’t the last one.”

“Is that a saying,” Lumi said.

“It’s a saying.”

Niko looked at the Jester.

He had come up beside them without any bells at all, which Niko would think about later and dislike. He was not smiling. He was not doing anything with his face. He looked at the small cold door the way a man looks at a name he knows on a list he wasn’t expecting to read.

Then he turned around and walked back toward the water.

“Lights past the fall,” Marta said. “Same rule.”

“Same rule,” Niko said.

He chalked it standing up, hurrying, because the cold was in his hands: *low door — far shore — roots grown inwards — cold — LEFT IT.*

Behind them, out beyond the lantern’s reach, the cavern went on and on with its false stars burning over water they would never walk. Niko tried to work out what fraction of the room they had actually put their boots on, got as far as *a hundredth,* and stopped, because it wasn’t a hundredth.

The race was loud by the time they got back to the wheel.

And under the noise of it, from somewhere down inside the wall where the water had gone, the air began to move against Niko’s face — in, and then out, and then in again, timed like something very large and very old getting its breath back.
