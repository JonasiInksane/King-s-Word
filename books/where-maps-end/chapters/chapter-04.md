# Chapter 4 — Parting Trees

The Coil stopped pretending it was a road.

Needles swallowed the last of the wheel ruts. The path narrowed until shoulders brushed damp bark. Somewhere above, wind moved the canopy and the light under it shifted in slow bands, green to gold to green again.

Niko’s chalk stub had grayed both fingertips. He kept the maps rolled. Looking at them every ten steps would only make the 1987 sheet feel like a joke told too late.

Bram walked with the pot quiet for once. Either the sock had worked or the forest had eaten the rattle.

Lumi’s crate snagged a branch. She freed it, apologized to the branch, and kept going.

Marta did not apologize to anything.

---

They found the first deer by its lunch.

Crystal moss chewed low along a root shelf, pale threads bitten clean. Droppings nearby glittered faintly, as if the animal had been digesting light.

Bram crouched, pinched a scrap of moss, sniffed, and put it back.

“Deer,” he said. “Recent. They’re eating well.”

“Antlers sparkle when they do,” Lumi said. “Academy diagram. Page—”

“I’ve seen them,” Marta said.

Niko touched the chewed edge. Cool. Slightly sticky.

“What eats the deer?”

“Wolves,” Bram said. “Glass ones. Don’t look like glass. Just mean. They leave the bones for beetles, beetles spread spores, spores feed moss, moss feeds deer.” He stood, knees cracking. “Then we come through and complain about onions.”

Lumi wrote while walking and nearly walked into Marta’s pack.

“Sorry. Loop. I like loops. Loops make sense.”

“Until they don’t,” Marta said.

A scratch mark ran along a trunk at hip height—four lines, not fresh, not ancient. Bram eyed it the way he eyed mushrooms.

“Old,” he decided.

“How old?” Niko asked.

“Older than lunch. Younger than the tree.”

“That’s not a number.”

“I’m not a calendar.”

They moved on.

---

Deeper in, the Coil bent around a hollow where the air was cooler. Water sounded somewhere left—thin, not a river, maybe a seep. Niko almost unrolled the 1712 sheet. He didn’t. Marta’s pace said they were still marching, not surveying.

A crystal deer stepped into the path ahead.

It was smaller than Niko expected. Antlers held a soft glitter, as if someone had crushed the moss and poured it along the bone. It looked at them without panic, ears tipping once, then stepped aside into the undergrowth. The glitter went with it. For a few breaths the needles where it had stood held a faint shine, then that faded too.

Lumi made a sound she would later deny.

Bram watched the place it had stood.

“Good meat,” he said quietly. “We’re not hungry enough.”

Marta was already walking.

Niko followed, chalking nothing. Some things didn’t need a margin. He wanted them to stay what they were.

---

The path of leans began without announcement.

One young tree bent away from the Coil, trunk whole, no break, no scrape. Then another. Then three in a row, all bowed the same direction, making a soft corridor that left the Pilgrim Coil and ran into denser green like an invitation written in wood.

Deer tracks entered that corridor and did not come back out onto the Coil.

Niko stopped.

He knew this shape. He had chalked a smaller version of it hours ago and kept it to himself.

“Marta.”

She had already stopped. Her eyes were on the corridor, not on him.

“Parting trees,” she said.

Lumi came up between them, book half open.

“Parting as in—”

“As in the forest moves,” Marta said. “Young wood. No breaks. Something walks through and the trees get out of the way.”

Bram shifted the pot.

“Something.”

“You don’t follow it for a shortcut,” Marta said. “People who do get a shorter story.”

Niko looked from the Coil to the lean-corridor and back.

“Is it a stag?”

“It’s a reason to stay on the path,” Marta said.

“That’s not—”

“It’s what you get until we need more.”

Lumi closed her book without finding the page.

“Academy calls them path-bends. Unreliable. Associated with—” She stopped. “Associated with not writing confident footnotes.”

Niko crouched at the nearest trunk. Bark smooth. No cut. The lean was gentle and wrong in the same way the mile-marker had been wrong: the world arranged like it remembered a different arrangement.

He chalked on the 1839 margin this time, because the forest belonged to that year more than the others: *parting corridor off Coil — young trees lean, unbroken — deer tracks enter*.

“Can we look a little way in?” he asked.

“No,” Marta said.

“Ten steps.”

“No.”

Bram had already taken two steps toward the corridor, peering, not entering.

“Smells like wet leaves,” he reported. “And something that ate well.”

“Back,” Marta said.

He came back.

They stood a moment longer, four people and a path that wanted to be two paths. Then Marta took the Coil again, and the corridor watched them leave the way empty doorways watched.

---

Afternoon stretched.

The forest gave them ordinary difficulties. A root that loved ankles. A patch of blue-glow moss Marta steered them wide of before Lumi could kneel. Bram spent a stretch of walking trying to free a strap that had twisted under the kettle. He won. The kettle bumped his hip anyway.

Niko asked questions when he couldn’t help it.

“How often do you see parting trees?”

“Often enough to have a rule,” Marta said.

“Do they always mean the same animal?”

“They mean don’t be clever.”

Lumi, from behind:

“I read a paper that said—”

“Later,” Marta and Niko said together, which surprised both of them.

Bram hummed three notes and lost the fourth.

---

The wolf came from downhill of the Coil.

Niko saw the flank first—pale hide catching wrong light, lean, not glass. Just mean. Bram’s loop had named them before they had a body.

“Left—” he said.

Marta was already turning.

It went for the packs, low and hungry, testing. Marta met the rush with a short knife and a boot to the shoulder. Bram swung the pot. The lid rang off skull. The wolf staggered, snarled, came again for Lumi’s crate strap.

Lumi’s hands found light the way they found footnotes—late, awkward, useful. A sharp crack of mana snapped between trunks. The wolf flinched from the spark more than the steel.

Niko yanked the crate aside and kicked wet needles into its face. Bram stomped once where a paw sought purchase. Marta’s knife opened a line along the shoulder—not deep, enough.

“Out,” she said—to the animal, to them, same tone.

It left bleeding into the brush downhill. Not dead. Gone enough.

Bram checked the pot for dents. One new. He looked almost offended.

“That was my lunch pot.”

“It’s still a pot,” Marta said. She wiped the knife on ordinary moss. “Anyone bit?”

Nobody was. Lumi’s hands shook once and then remembered how to hold a pencil.

“Glass wolves,” she said. “Don’t look like glass. Just—”

“Mean,” Bram finished. “I said.”

Niko chalked nothing. The Coil kept being a path.

---

They passed a fallen log freckled with stone beetles. Bram stopped long enough to tip the log with his boot. The beetles poured, black and uninterested, carrying pale dust on their shells.

“Spores,” Lumi whispered, delighted. “That’s the return trip. Moss side.”

“Don’t put them in a jar,” Marta said.

“I wasn’t—”

“You were inventing a jar.”

Bram let the log settle. One beetle climbed onto his boot, considered the journey, and left.

Niko watched the pale dust catch a slant of light and thought of the deer’s antlers. Same glitter, different job.

They walked until the light thinned to the color of old tea.

---

Near first dark they found a moss-ring clearing the Coil admitted with a wider breath of sky.

A ring of older trees. Soft ground. A fire scar in the middle that had been used and left polite. No blue glow in the ring itself. Marta walked the edge once, kicked a stone, listened to nothing special, and nodded.

“Here.”

Bram set the pot down like forgiveness. Lumi collapsed onto her crate and stayed there, books sliding. Niko unrolled all three maps while he still had light.

1839: forest. Honest, for once.  
1987: grassland with a road—embarrassed ink.  
1712: river—still a rumor downhill.

He added a small arrow where the parting corridor had left the Coil, then stopped himself from drawing what he hadn’t walked.

“You’re frowning at paper again,” Lumi said.

“The leans.”

“Path-bends.”

“Parting trees,” Niko said. “Marta’s word fits better. The wood gives way. It doesn’t break.”

Bram poked the fire scar with a stick.

“Like people getting off a bench,” he said.

Lumi blinked.

“What?”

“Crowded bench. Someone big sits. Everyone slides. Bench doesn’t snap.” Bram shrugged. “Maybe. Or I’m hungry.”

“You’re always hungry,” Marta said, already stringing a line for wet socks.

“Yes.”

Niko almost asked if a river could slide the same way a bench crowd did. He put the chalk away instead. The question would keep.

---

Dinner was thin stew and the last of the dried fruit. Bram tasted the water from a skin, said “Good,” and this time Lumi didn’t ask for what. Crystal moths came for the steam and left when the steam thinned.

After bowls, Niko walked the clearing’s edge with Marta’s permission and no more than that. From the north side, if he turned his head just so, he could still see the faintest suggestion of the lean-corridor through trunks—an absence of tangle, a politeness in the wood.

He came back and sat. Lumi’s head was already nodding over a page. Bram oiled the pot lid. Marta sat with her back to a tree, eyes half closed.

“If the trees move,” Niko said, “does the map’s forest stay wrong, or does it become right later?”

Lumi’s eyes opened.

“Both. Neither. Depends when you stand there with a pen.”

“That’s not comforting.”

“It’s not meant to be. It’s surveying.”

Bram held up the lid, checked his reflection in the dull metal, and put it down.

“I miss—”

He stopped.

Lumi waited. Marta did not help him.

“Stew with actual weight,” Bram finished.

“We’ll take Coil at first light,” Marta said. “Root Camp spur if the marks hold. No chasing corridors.”

“I wasn’t going to chase,” Niko said.

“You were going to measure,” Marta said. “Different. Still gets people lost.”

Night settled in pieces. A wolf called once, far off—too far to be a problem, close enough to make Lumi’s pencil pause. The call was answered by nothing. Bram fed the fire one stick. The crystal moths did not return.

Niko lay down with the maps under his pack flap. The parting trees held their corridor in the dark whether anyone watched or not.

He slept before he finished deciding what that meant.
