# Chapter 6 — The Smooth Cut

Root Camp pointed them toward stone.

Morning left with Bram saying “Morning” to a Scholar who was already mid-argument about lids. The Scholar said “Morning” back without looking, which Bram accepted as a full conversation. Lumi tried to return her borrowed cot flap ties and was told to keep them. Marta paid for water they had already drunk, because that was how Root Camp stayed a place you could return to.

Niko pinned the quarry scrap into his roll beside the Guild sheets. The kettle doodle in the margin looked back at him. He left it alone.

“Quarry first,” Marta said. “Lake after. No inventing a third destination because a board has open questions.”

“I like open questions,” Lumi said.

“I like closed roads,” Marta said. “We compromise by walking.”

The quarry road left before the Scholars finished arguing about lids. Marta liked that. Bram liked the part where the trees thinned and the ground stopped pretending to be soft. Lumi liked the part where her meter had something hard to poke. Niko liked the nails in the stone—small honest things that matched or didn’t match his copied scrap.

The road was a scar through trees: old cart beds, iron stubs where winches had been, towers missing their tops. Cut faces rose gray and honest. Tool marks everywhere—chisels, wedges, the ugly grammar of people taking rock.

Bram liked it immediately.

“Less moss,” he said. “More lunch rock. Metaphorically.”

“Don’t lick the walls,” Lumi said.

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You were considering it.”

“I consider many things. I do fewer.”

Marta walked the rim path without looking down. A pilgrim ribbon hung from one survey nail, pale and useless in wind.

“Why mark a quarry?” Niko asked.

“People get lost in holes,” Marta said. “Or proud.”

“Same thing sometimes,” Bram offered.

They passed a broken winch tower and a cart bed full of rainwater. A frog sat on the rim and declined to become a sample. Lumi still wrote *frog — declined* and then crossed it out because even she had limits.

Bram tried the cart-bed water with a fingertip, made a face, and wiped it on his trousers.

“Not lunch.”

“Was anyone suggesting lunch?” Lumi asked.

“I’m preventing suggestions.”

Niko compared a nail’s position to his Root Camp scrap and found it a handspan off. He chalked the difference small. Off was still information.

---

A local cutter—old, dust in the lines of his face—watched them from a shade lean-to. He was sharpening nothing. Just watching. Holding a stone like company.

“Guild?” he asked.

“Independent,” Marta said.

“Same disease.” He spat politely aside. “Don’t sleep in the smooth holes.”

Niko’s head turned.

“Smooth?”

The cutter jerked a thumb toward a darker slit under the cut face.

“Tunnels. Too clean. Not our work. Old growth roots in the wall—people say. Kids dare each other. Kids come out. So far.”

“Old growth,” Lumi repeated, already writing.

“That’s what people say,” the cutter said. “I say don’t sleep there. Saying’s cheaper than funerals.”

“Have you been inside?” Niko asked.

“Far enough to know I prefer lunch outside.”

Bram nodded as if that were a complete geology lesson.

“Can we look?” Niko asked Marta.

Marta looked at the slit, then at the sky, then at their water.

“Look. Don’t nap. Don’t get clever.”

The cutter snorted.

“Clever’s what digs the second hole.”

---

They approached the slit together and then not together—Marta first, Niko close, Lumi with the meter raised like a question, Bram at the mouth with sunlight still on half the pot.

The slit was cooler than the quarry air.

Inside, the walls forgot how tools worked. No chisel bites. No drill scars. Stone curved as if something had pressed through soft clay and the clay had decided to become rock afterward. Niko ran his fingers along it and felt no seam he trusted.

“This isn’t on any of the three maps,” he said.

“Quarries grow holes,” Marta said. “Maps don’t love holes.”

“This wasn’t cut.”

“Lots of things weren’t cut. Still holes.”

Lumi held her meter to the wall. The needle did nothing interesting. She looked disappointed and relieved.

“Mana’s quiet,” she said. “Or bored. Or I’m holding it wrong.”

“You’re holding it like a threat,” Bram said from the mouth, pot half in sun. “Walls don’t like threats.”

“Walls don’t like anything.”

“Smells like wet cellar,” Bram offered. “And green.”

Niko walked three careful paces deeper. The air got cooler. The curve of the wall stayed too perfect. A drip somewhere far in measured time without clocks.

Niko saw them then—pale fibers in a crack, thin as thread, not dry. Fresh enough that when he touched one, it gave. Not brittle. Not ancient dust.

“Roots,” he said.

Lumi crouched. Her crate bumped the wall. She apologized to the wall, then frowned at herself.

“Root fibers. Could be from above. Trees send feelers. Or—” She stopped. “Or old growth, like he said.”

“They’re soft,” Niko said.

“Old growth can be soft if it’s been wet,” Lumi said, but she didn’t sound like she believed herself.

Marta took one look, one breath, and nodded them out.

“We came to see. We’ve seen. Out.”

“I want to mark—”

“Mark outside.”

Bram did not argue. He was already backing into sun like a man escaping a pantry that smelled wrong.

The crack beside the slit answered for itself.

Stone beetles poured—not the idle freckle under a forest log, but a thick dark rush, shells clicking, pale spore-dust like smoke. Warmth. Green smell. Soft fibers disturbed. Something had decided they were lunch rock after all.

“Back—” Marta started.

Bram was already swinging. The pot lid became shield and hammer. Beetles rang off metal. Marta’s boot crushed a knot of them against stone. Lumi snapped her meter shut and used the hard case like a paddle, muttering apologies to science between hits.

Niko saw the second pour coming from higher—spoil pile shifting. “Above!”

Marta yanked Lumi left. Bram kicked the pile’s edge and the rush broke into thinner rivers they could step past. A few beetles reached Niko’s sleeve; he scraped them off with chalk-stub knuckles and kept moving toward open light.

Then it stopped the way swarms stop—interest spent, or sun won. Shells retreated into cracks. Spore-dust hung and settled on honest chisel marks.

Bram wiped the lid on his trousers.

“Not lunch,” he said.

“Was anyone suggesting—” Lumi began, then looked at her meter case. Beetle smear. “Never mind.”

“Anyone bit?” Marta asked.

“Pride,” Bram said. “And the lid.”

---

They marked outside. Niko’s chalk on a blank scrap: *quarry tunnel — walls too smooth — fresh root fibers in crack — beetle surge at mouth — local: old growth / don’t sleep*. He pinned the scrap into his map roll with more care than the Guild sheets got.

The cutter watched. He had not left his lean-to. He spat politely aside again.

“Kids’ story,” he said. “Basin’s full of them. Beetles too.”

“Do the roots move?” Niko asked.

The cutter laughed once.

“Everything moves if you stare long enough. Go stare at the lake. Water’s better company.”

“How far?” Marta asked.

“Hours if you don’t fall in holes. Longer if you do.”

Lumi closed her notebook.

“Academy has a category for anomalous voids,” she said. “It’s mostly a way to not say ‘we don’t know.’”

“Then write ‘we don’t know,’” Marta said.

“That’s not how footnotes work.”

“It is how boots work.”

Niko looked back at the slit. Cool dark. Soft fibers. No tool marks. The cut faces around it were honest with chisels. This one was honest in a different language.

“Why dismiss soft roots?” he asked.

“Because soft roots make people invent monsters,” Marta said. “And monsters make people sleep in holes to prove they’re brave.”

“I’m not inventing.”

“You’re noticing. Keep noticing. Don’t decorate.”

---

They took shade under the broken winch tower and ate. Bram fried the Root Camp onions with something that had been ration and was now almost food. The smell made the cutter’s dog appear from nowhere, sit politely, and leave when Bram gave it a burnt edge.

Lumi kept glancing at the slit.

“If the walls were pressed, something pressed them,” she said. “That’s not a footnote. That’s physics. Bad physics. Or good physics with a rude author.”

“Lunch first,” Bram said. “Physics after salt.”

“I hate that you’re right about the order.”

“I’m right about food. Other things are rumors.”

Marta drank, wiped the mouth of the skin, and passed it without ceremony.

“We don’t go back in,” she said. “We don’t camp near it. We don’t tell kids it’s a dare.”

“I wasn’t going to recruit kids,” Lumi said.

“You were going to write a category.”

“That’s different.”

“It’s louder.”

The cutter wandered close enough to accept a scrap of onion and refuse a theory.

“Lake road’s clear,” he said. “Unless it isn’t. Water moves. Stone pretends not to.”

“Does stone pretend?” Niko asked.

“Everything pretends,” the cutter said. “Some things get paid for it.”

He left them the shade and took his dog and his stone.

Niko looked at his three dated maps. None of them owed this tunnel an apology. The tunnel didn’t care. It sat under the cut like a throat that had forgotten how to be a mouth.

Lumi drew a quick cross-section of the slit in her margin and labeled it *void — no tool marks — soft fibers*. Then she wrote *do not sleep* twice, once for the cutter and once for herself.

“Which map gets this?” she asked Niko.

“None of them,” he said. “New scrap. New honesty.”

“That’s going to be a thick roll by Mossgate.”

“Good,” Niko said. “Thick means we looked.”

Bram packed the pot and checked the lid twice, as if the tunnel’s green smell might have followed them into metal.

“I liked the chisels better,” he said. “Chisels make sense. This—” He jerked his chin at the slit. “This is a cupboard that opens the wrong way.”

“Don’t open it again,” Marta said.

“I wasn’t going to cook in it.”

“That sentence worries me more than it should.”

Niko stood. He did not prove anything.

He kept looking until Marta said move, and then he looked back once, the way he had looked at the blank sheet on the Wall.

The slit kept its cool dark. The soft fibers kept their softness. The road toward the lake kept being a road.

They took it.
