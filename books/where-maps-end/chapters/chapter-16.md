# Chapter 16 — What the Roof Hides

The pattern held for a day and a half, which was the answer nobody had wanted.

Two more pale rims above the salt post. A seep that had been drinkable on the way out and tasted of pennies now — Bram spat it into the fern and declined to explain, which explained enough. At the second rim Lumi crouched, counted something under her breath, and quit counting.

“It isn’t spreading like a stain,” she said. “Stains go out from the middle. This comes up. The shelf fails from underneath, all at once, and then the top notices.”

“Then we stop reading verges,” Marta said, and turned them north.

They walked the cutter path backwards. Nobody said the word *garden* for the first hour. Marta set a pace that made conversation expensive.

The Jester got quieter the closer they came. Not solemn. He still tipped his hat at a stump. But his bells stopped arguing with his feet, and Niko noticed that too and chalked nothing, because *the man went quiet* was not a survey fact.

The domes came up out of the trees in the late afternoon, glinting the way they had the first time.

Niko went straight to the nearest channel and crouched.

The water still ran. It ran a thumb’s width below a dark mineral line on the stone — an old high mark, long and level and dry.

“Marta.”

She came. Looked where he pointed. Put her finger in the water, then on the line, then back.

“How old’s that line?”

“Older than us standing here,” Niko said. “It didn’t drop last week. It’s been dropping.”

“Good,” she said, which meant *bad, and useful.*

Lumi wanted to argue about evaporation. She got three sentences into it and abandoned herself halfway. “No, wait. Evaporation would foul the taste. It’s clean. It’s just — less.” She wrote *LESS* in her book with far too much pressure.

Bram had already found a frog.

It was small, green, and sitting in a channel elbow like a rent-paying tenant. He picked it up. Lumi told him it shouldn’t live here. He agreed pleasantly, put it back, and forgot about it forever.

The Jester was looking up.

He had been looking up for a while, actually, with his head tilted so far back his collar bells lay on his shoulders.

“Roofs have floors too, you know,” he said, and started climbing before anyone answered.

“Hey—” Marta said.

He was already at the second rung. There was a ladder, was the thing. Nobody had seen a ladder. It ran up the inside of a dome pillar behind a curtain of ivy — iron rungs seated in pale root the way a tooth sits in a gum, the wood grown round the metal instead of split by it.

Marta swore, tested the third rung with her whole weight, and went after him.

---

The Rafters were a floor the way a nest is a house.

Glass catwalks ran out from the pillar into the crowns of the trees that had punched through the dome — narrow, ribbed underneath with the same pale root, laced between branches like something the trees had agreed to. Green light came up from below. Ordinary daylight came down through the broken crown. The two met in the middle and made an hour that didn’t belong to any part of the day.

And the cocoons hung everywhere.

Hundreds of them. Sealed cases the length of Niko’s forearm, slung in the branch forks and along the catwalk rails, each one glowing a soft steady yellow, none of them flickering, none of them warm.

Nobody had lit them. That was the whole thing. Nobody had lit them and they were lit.

Lumi sat down on the catwalk. Just sat, legs through the rail, book unopened on her knees.

“Oh,” she said.

Niko stood and let it happen to him. The light didn’t do anything. It didn’t pulse or answer or spell out a warning. It hung there being beautiful in a room with no floor.

“Those are eggs,” Bram said.

“Cocoons,” Lumi said.

“Hm.” He leaned closer to one. “Can you eat the ones that glow?”

“*Bram.*”

“I’m asking the question. Somebody has to ask the question.”

“Vein moths,” Lumi said. “Or a cousin of them. Pollinators. They’re cased — properly cased, not spun-and-left. This is stock. Somebody put a season in a box and shut the lid.” She stopped. “That’s not a scientific sentence. I’ll fix it later.”

Marta walked the catwalk to its end and back, testing it, and then leaned on the rail with the basin spread out beyond the broken crown — ridge, fern, the far grey smear of the fortress spur, and somewhere past it roads she couldn’t see.

“Don’t touch them,” she said. “Any of them. Not one.”

“I wasn’t—”

“You were thinking about it in a loud voice.”

They ate lunch up there, because Bram insisted the air was better and because nobody wanted to leave. He tried to get a small fire going in his pot lid and the draft killed it three times before he called the draft names and gave up. So it was cold bread, and the last of the hard cheese, and a jar of honey Marta produced from somewhere in her pack without comment and set down between them like a fine levied on herself.

“You’ve been carrying that since Mossgate,” Lumi said.

“Mm.”

“Since *Mossgate.*”

“Eat it before I remember I’m sensible.”

They sat with their legs hanging over the rail, five of them in a row, in a room full of lanterns nobody had lit.

The Jester ate his bread crust-first, which Niko decided was the strangest thing about him so far.

---

The stair down was under the atrium floor, where a slab had settled a hand-width and made a shadow that Niko only saw because he was looking for water.

The Undercroft was the roof’s other half.

Root scaffolding — thick as ship ribs, pale as peeled willow — rose out of the dark and braced the whole glass dome from underneath. Not growing wild. Grown *to*. Each rib forked where the load forked. Where a pane had cracked above, a rib had thickened below.

Bram put his hand flat on one and sniffed his palm.

“Quarry,” he said.

Niko’s stomach did something complicated.

He crouched at the base of the nearest rib and found what he was already afraid of: fibers in the split of the stone, fine and soft and pale, and *fresh* — the same wet-string fresh as the ones in the too-smooth tunnel two weeks and half a basin away.

“Old growth,” Lumi said. Then, immediately: “I hate that I said that. That’s what the cutter said. Say something better than the cutter, Lumi.”

“It’s the same,” Niko said. “Same as the quarry. That’s all I’ve got. It’s the same and it’s not old.”

“That’s two places,” Marta said.

“Two places is a line.”

“Two places is two places.” She said it without heat, and then, because she was fair: “Chalk it.”

He chalked it.

The cistern head sat at the Undercroft’s heart — a stone basin as wide as a threshing floor, with four channels leaving it like the arms of a star.

Three of them were capped. Not silted, not collapsed. *Capped*, with fitted plugs of stone and pale root, deliberate as corks.

The fourth ran, and it ran back inward, up into the garden.

Lumi got there before Niko could finish the thought out loud.

“It used to share,” she said. “It used to send water *out*. Three ways out. And now it drinks its own cup.”

Niko followed the capped arms with his eyes and his hands and then with his chalk, and the directions came out ugly and clear: one south and east toward where the Trade Road ditch ran dry; one long west under the ridge; one that pointed at nothing he could name yet, which was the one he wanted most.

“The shelf above the salt post,” he said. “It’s downhill of this. Isn’t it.”

“Everything’s downhill of something,” Marta said. Then she looked at the capped stone for a long moment. “Yes.”

The beetles came out of the lattice while they were still standing there.

Stone beetles nest where nobody walks, and nobody had walked here in a long time, and they came out of the root braces in a clattering pour — thumb-sized, hand-sized, one the size of a boot that Bram would describe later, at length, without being asked.

“Lid,” Marta said.

Bram rang his lid off a rib and the sound went up into the roots and came back wrong and doubled, which startled the beetles worse than the noise. Lumi burned a green fern bundle and pushed the smoke low. Marta stamped a line and kept it. Niko saw the second pour starting behind them out of a fork in the scaffolding and shouted the direction before it could become a surround, and they gave that fork the smoke and the lid together.

The beetles went back into the dark to have opinions about it.

“I liked them better as a food chain,” Bram said, checking his lid for a new dent and finding one.

The Jester had spent the fight standing perfectly still beside a capped channel arm, looking at the plug of stone and root the way a man looks at a bill he doesn’t remember running up.

Niko caught it. He didn’t ask. He was learning that asking directly cost you the answer.

Instead he crouched by the last capped arm — and felt cold air on his face.

The cap wasn’t seated flush. Beside it, where the basin met the floor, a shaft went down into the Undercroft’s stone. Not a channel. A hole with a lip worn smooth, wide enough to drop a pack down, and breathing up at him with air that smelled like rain that hadn’t happened yet.

He held his palm over it and said nothing for long enough that Marta came and put her palm over it too.

“How deep?” she said.

Niko dropped a pebble in, and they waited for it to land, and waited, and were still waiting when Bram said, “That’s rude,” and the sound finally came back up.
