# Chapter 14 — Five for Stew

By midday the Glass Garden was a glint behind them, and the Jester had already tried to wear a fern as a scarf.

“It matches nothing,” Lumi said, removing it.

“That’s the point of scarves,” he said. “They argue with the shirt.”

Marta did not smile. She also did not throw him off a ridge, which Niko filed as provisional acceptance.

They took a cutter path that skirted low toward older Trade Road spurs—the kind of route that promised people, wagons, and news that traveled faster than chalk. Cascade weather wanted roads. Marta wanted to see which roads were already flinching.

Bram walked with the pot and the new arithmetic of bowls.

“Five,” he told the lid once, as if warning it.

“I can eat two,” the Jester offered.

“You can eat what you’re given,” Bram said. “Twice is harder.”

The Jester beamed as if Bram had recited poetry.

“I like him,” he told Niko. “He seasons threats.”

Niko didn’t know what to do with being told things. He chalked while walking: *Jester — keeps pace — notices trees / water — memory holes — not useful like a tool — useful like weather.*

Useless minutes arrived on schedule. A lizard claimed a warm nail and refused politics. Bram sneezed; nobody blessed him; he looked spiritually complete anyway. Lumi explained spore counts for eleven steps and then forgot the end of her own sentence and laughed at herself before anyone else could.

They stopped once to drink at a seep Bram approved with a grunt. The Jester tried to pay the seep with a bow. Marta told him water didn’t take tips. He tipped it anyway, with a leaf.

“You’re going to be exhausting,” Marta said.

“I excel at hobbies,” he said.

Niko asked him, because the asking wouldn’t leave him alone, whether he’d been to Mossgate.

“I have been to places that became other places,” the Jester said. “If Mossgate is still arguing with itself on a wall of paper, I will like it. Walls that keep receipts are rare.”

“You talk like you’ve seen the Map Wall,” Lumi said.

“I talk like I’ve seen people,” he said. “People invent walls. Then they invent guilt to hang on them. Charming species.”

Marta started walking again before the charm could grow legs.

The Jester listened to Lumi’s next half-explanation as if it were music.

“You’re wrong about the timing,” he said pleasantly, when she finished not finishing. “Or the dungeon is. Hard to tell which of you misbehaved.”

Lumi stopped walking.

“Wrong how?”

He pointed his stick at a patch of crystal moss that looked healthy until you crouched—edges pale, not bowl-bleach yet, a tired color.

“It’s tired in the roots,” he said. “Not the leaves. Leaves are liars. Roots gossip.”

Niko crouched. Soft pale threads under the mat, not the quarry’s thick fibers—finer, stressed. He did not say root-creatures. He chalked *moss tired under — Jester said roots gossip — unproven / keep.*

Marta watched the Jester’s face.

“Lucky guess.”

“I am extremely lucky,” he said. “Also I have eyes. Eyes are fashionable again, I notice. Good. I was worried.”

They camped early near a waystone that still held a Guild rest-circle board, letters faded: *Don’t camp on blue-glow moss. If the grove goes silent, leave. Hang your map.*

Marta read it aloud like a prayer that had paid rent.

The Jester tilted his head.

“Quiet groves,” he said. “Rude rooms. I used to put bells on my boots so rooms couldn’t sneak up. It never worked. Rooms are patient.”

“That’s not comforting,” Lumi said.

“Comfort is a sauce,” he said. “Not always on the meat.”

Bram made five bowls. The fifth looked the same as the others on purpose. The Jester accepted his with ceremony and then ate like a person who had forgotten pride.

Before the serious talk, there was the unserious kind: Bram accusing the Jester of stealing salt by looking at it wrong; the Jester confessing at once and returning a pinch he hadn’t taken; Lumi laughing until Marta told her to breathe between jokes. Niko let the sound sit in him like a second meal.

After, when the fire was low and the waystone’s shadow long, talk went soft and useless in the way Niko was learning to need.

“When we hang the maps,” Lumi said, “do we hang the weird ones too? The scraps. The ones that aren’t proud.”

“Proud maps lie prettier,” Marta said. “Hang honest. Guild can sulk.”

“I want to hang the upside-down fall,” Niko said. “And write that we left the lights.”

“Good,” Marta said. “Leaving is also a survey.”

The Jester poked the fire with his stick and made a spark jump like a trained insect.

“Lights beyond water that falls wrong,” he said, almost to himself. “I wouldn’t go either. Not yet. Some doors are prettier if they stay doors.”

Niko looked up sharp.

“You know them?”

“I know wanting,” the Jester said, sunny again. “Wanting is older than maps. Older than brown shirts. Possibly older than stew, though I refuse to believe that.”

Bram grunted approval of the theology.

Marta shifted the subject with a boot on a branch.

“Tomorrow we cut toward the Mossgate approaches,” she said. “Not into town. Approaches. If wolves are shopping for roads, that’s where the shopping shows. We redirect if we can. We don’t die proving a theory.”

“Redirect how?” Niko asked.

“Noise. Fire lines if we must. Food trails away from people if Bram can stand wasting meat. Signs for other travelers. If a shelf of moss is failing and pushing the loop, we stop people from camping in the sick parts. Small work. Ugly work. Real.”

Lumi nodded, already scribbling a list that would become three lists.

“What if small isn’t enough?” Niko asked.

“Then we do small twice,” Marta said. “Then we admit we need a better shelf of tricks. We don’t invent heroics because the maps are watching.”

“Maps don’t watch,” Lumi said.

“People who hang them do,” Marta said.

Bram poked the fire.

“If we waste the fish, supper gets philosophical,” he said. “I accept philosophy. I do not accept empty bowls without warning.”

“Warning,” Marta said.

“Cruel,” Bram said, and looked oddly comforted.

The Jester lay back with his head on his folded ridiculous coat and looked at stars through leaves.

“Small work saves more gardens than speeches,” he said. Then, lighter: “Also speeches. Speeches wake people under glass. Mixed review.”

Niko felt heat in his ears and said nothing.

Lumi, merciful for once, asked Bram whether onions had opinions about cascade weather. Bram answered for three full minutes. Nobody stopped him. The waystone listened like a polite aunt.

Sleep came in pieces. On Niko’s watch, the Jester wasn’t sleeping either. He sat at the edge of firelight turning a small flat stone over and over.

“You’re supposed to rest,” Niko said.

“I did,” the Jester said. “For a few years. I’m full.”

He held up the stone. In the fire it looked ordinary. In his fingers it looked chosen.

“When the ground used to move,” he said, casual as weather, “it wasn’t trying to confuse your Guild. It was rearranging the furniture so the house could breathe. If the furniture stoppers vanish, the house holds its breath too long. Then animals do the rearranging with teeth.”

Niko’s pulse thudded in his chalk hand.

“Is that what happened?”

The Jester smiled, bells quiet for once.

“That’s a joke that will matter later,” he said. “Or a memory wearing a joke’s coat. I can’t tell which until somebody laughs at the wrong time.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It’s five bowls of almost,” the Jester said gently. “You people like almost. You chalk it.”

Niko did. On scrap, small: *furniture / breath / stoppers vanish — Jester — joke? keep.*

Near dawn, a runner from a Harvester cart passed their ring without stopping—face tight, two-finger greeting to Marta cut short.

“Wolves on the south spur,” the runner called. “Not hunting deer. Hunting noise. Tell anyone with ears.”

Then gone.

Marta was already packing.

“Approaches,” she said. “Now.”

Bram poured the last tea into five cups with grim fairness. The Jester accepted his, toasted the waystone, and spilled half on his own sleeve immediately.

“Terribly dressed,” he told himself, delighted. “Consistent.”

Lumi snorted tea and blamed him with her eyes.

Niko rolled his scraps—Stag, bleach, up-fall lights left, garden, five bowls, furniture joke—and felt the weight shift from witness toward work.

Behind them, somewhere in glass they couldn’t see, channels would still be ticking.

Ahead, the roads were starting to remember they could bleed.
