# Chapter 15 — The Road Remembers Teeth

The south spur smelled like fear before it looked like it.

Not smoke. Not blood. The sharp animal smell of too many bodies choosing the same thin idea: *away*. Cart ruts braided wrong. A child’s dropped crystal marble sat in dust like a period nobody had time to write. Far off, glass-wolf voices braided too—more than a pair, less than a legend, enough.

Marta raised a fist. Stop. Listen.

Niko listened until his ears filled with his own blood. Then, under that: claws on stone. A deer crashing. A man swearing at a mule that had decided philosophy.

“Not Mossgate yet,” Marta said. “Close enough that mistakes become funerals.”

Lumi’s hands shook on her crate straps. She hid it by tightening them too much.

“Bleach signs on the verge,” she said. “Look— crystal moss pale along the ditch. Same tired under. Loop’s pushing eaters out.”

The Jester crouched, bells carefully pinched silent in his fist like a polite crime. He sniffed the pale moss, then the healthy patch beyond it, then grinned as if the ditch had told a dirty joke.

“The road’s wearing the forest’s headache,” he said. “Poor road. It only wanted commerce.”

“Can you fix it?” Bram asked, practical as a ladle.

“I can point,” the Jester said. “Pointing is free. Fixing costs lunch and luck.”

He pointed—not at the wolves’ noise, at a side shelf of slope where moss still held a healthier glitter, and beyond it a dry drainage cut that ran away from the spur toward scrub nobody lived in.

“Hungry things follow easy food and easy paths,” he said. “Give them a ruder path with better snacks, they argue with themselves. Animals love arguing.”

Marta looked at Bram.

“Wasting meat.”

Bram already had the pack open.

“I hate this,” he said, slicing dried fish into a trail that offended his soul. “I hate this and I’m doing it. Nobody thank me with speeches.”

“I will thank you with silence,” Marta said.

“Accepted.”

They worked ugly and small. Fish trail into the drainage cut. Bram’s pot-lid bangs staged uphill to sound like a louder camp than they were. Lumi’s spark used spar—bright punctuation, not a storm. Niko chalked arrows on scrap for any following survey idiot and wedged them under stones facing the cut, then hated how little paper weighed against teeth.

The first wolves came like rumors made of glass and rib.

Three. Then a fourth lagging, mouth foam-dry. They hit the fish smell, hit the lid noise, hit each other’s hunger. One broke toward the spur anyway—toward mule swearing and human panic.

Marta met that one.

Knife. Boot. Shoulder. No poetry. Bram’s pot rang off its hip hard enough to change its religion. Niko saw the lagging fourth slide for Lumi’s open side and shouted; the Jester, who almost never fought, stuck his stick in a loop of root and tripped the animal into a humiliating sprawl, then stepped back as if embarrassed by competence.

“Well,” he said. “That’s embarrassing. For the wolf. Mostly.”

Lumi’s spark cracked the air over its face. It chose the drainage cut and the fish religion after all.

The spur’s mule stopped philosophizing long enough for its person to drag it toward Mossgate’s safer rumor.

Niko’s hands shook through the chalk: *south spur — wolves redirected into dry cut — fish waste — lid / spark / stick trip — bleach on verge — temporary.*

“Temporary,” Marta agreed, reading his face. “We just bought hours. Maybe a day. Cascade doesn’t take receipts.”

A second pressure arrived before pride could.

Not wolves—people. A small Scholar pair and a Survivor with a torn sleeve, coming the wrong way, eyes too wide.

“Fortress road’s coughing wolves,” the Survivor said to Marta without hello. “You the ones with the pot?”

“We’re the ones with the pot,” Bram said, wounded by fame.

“There’s a pale shelf above the old salt post,” one Scholar said, words tripping. “Moss going white in a sheet. Beetles already loud. If that sheet goes, the next ditch is the trade shoulder. We don’t— we can’t—”

“Show us,” Marta said.

They ran a short, ugly run that wasn’t adventure.

The pale sheet was worse than the cavern bowl because it was outdoors and honest about spreading. Crystal moss the color of old bone in a fan downslope toward the ditch that fed the spur. Beetle clicks underneath like impatient rain. A crystal deer stood at the healthy edge and would not cross, antlers duller than Coil days.

Lumi whispered a word that wasn’t a curse and wasn’t a prayer.

“If this sheet fails into the shoulder,” she said, “beetles bloom on the road. Spores. Then deer avoid the verge. Then wolves stop having a pantry that isn’t us.”

Niko saw Mossgate in his head—streets, Map Wall, kids sorting spent crystals, Bram’s wrong mushroom tossed, four buns. Ordinary. Chewable. Worth ugly work.

“What do we do that isn’t dying on it?” he asked.

The Jester walked the healthy edge with his stick, not stepping on the pale. His face went still in a way that made the bells seem louder.

“Water,” he said softly. “This shelf wants a drink it’s not getting. Something under used to share. Something under is late.” He glanced at Niko, sudden sunny again. “Don’t look at me like I’m an answer. I’m a person in terrible clothes. But if you can wet the healthy edge and cut a shallow trench between pale and ditch—rude little moat—you slow the gossip. Roots gossip. Water interrupts.”

Marta didn’t ask how he knew. Asking would cost daylight.

“Bram, skins. Lumi, trench line—shallow, don’t bury yourself. Niko, mark the pale rim so nobody camps it. I’ll keep teeth off you. Jester— if you’re going to be useful, be useful. If you’re going to joke, joke while carrying water.”

“I can do both,” he said. “I’m talented.”

They worked until shoulders burned.

Water on the healthy edge. A shallow cut that looked insultingly small and mattered anyway. Niko’s chalk rim notes: *DO NOT CAMP — bleach sheet — trench — wet edge — not safe / safer.* The Survivor helped without being asked. One Scholar cried quietly and kept digging. Beetles clicked opinions. A wolf tested the scrub once; Marta’s flare was gone, but Bram’s lid and a thrown stone and the Jester’s sudden bright whistle—ugly, undignified, perfect—turned it.

When they stopped, the pale sheet had not healed. It had stopped winning for a moment.

Lumi sat in the dirt and laughed one cracked laugh.

“Academy would call this unscientific intervention.”

“Academy can file a complaint with the beetles,” Marta said.

Niko looked at the Jester. The man’s wrists were muddy. One bell was clogged with grit and rang like a cough. He looked younger and older at once, delighted the trench existed, frightened of something he wouldn’t name.

“The garden,” Niko said quietly. “The channels that still run. If shelves want drinks they’re not getting— is that the same kind of thirst?”

The Jester’s smile slipped, then returned wearing a thinner coat.

“Gardens hate being the only ones still doing their jobs,” he said. “It makes them lonely. Lonely systems break in interesting ways. We should check on my glass gossip soon. Before the basin asks it to do everyone’s homework.”

Marta heard. Filed. Did not salute the warning.

“Tonight we camp off-road, upwind of pale,” she said. “Tomorrow we find whether this spur is one bruise or a pattern. If it’s a pattern toward Mossgate, we pull every ugly trick we have—and we use that garden if it’s still running for a reason.”

Bram stared at his reduced fish stores with mourning.

“Five bowls become four thinner bowls,” he said. “I accept this pain.”

“I’ll eat less,” the Jester said.

“You’ll eat what keeps you walking,” Bram said. “Ghosts don’t dig trenches.”

“I’m only lightly ghost.”

“You’re muddy,” Lumi said. “Ghosts are lazier.”

They made a mean little camp in scrub that smelled of crushed fern and sweat. No cards. No ferry laughter. Stew with less weight and more meaning. The Survivor shared salt and left before full dark with the Scholars, carrying Niko’s rim warnings like contraband hope.

After food, when hands were idle and talk could be the scene, Niko sat with the maps—the three Guild sheets that had started all this—and did not ask them to apologize.

“If we stop the cascade,” he said, “the maps still disagreed. The years still happened. We don’t fix history. We just keep the next map from being a funeral.”

Marta nodded once.

“That’s enough job for one expedition.”

Lumi sat rewiring a spark between two fingers the way the academy had drilled—gather, compress, release—until the Jester, watching the sprout by his boot that had no business being that green, said lightly:

“Heat grows outwards, you know. Always hunting the thin places.”

He poked the sprout with his stick, already halfway into a different thought.

“Ignore me. I collect useless weather.”

Lumi didn’t thank him. She scowled at her fingers and kept drilling. Two tries later the spark came cleaner—smaller, meaner—like it had found a thinner place without asking her permission. She stared at her hand as if it had cheated.

“I changed the compress,” she said. “Not because of anything.”

“Mm,” the Jester said, pleased with the sprout. “Well done, compress.”

Niko chalked: *heat grows outwards — Jester — spark cleaner after — Lumi denies — keep.*

Far down the spur, wolf voices moved along the drainage cut where fish had lied for them. Not gone. Bent.

Above Mossgate’s unseen roofs, night would be lighting ordinary windows.

Niko lay down with dirt in his hair and a volume of work ahead that didn’t care about Guild years.

The road had remembered teeth.

They had reminded it, briefly, of other meals.

Tomorrow would ask whether brief was a seed or a joke—and whether the man with bells was a washed-out hire, a collector of useless weather, or something that preferred not to be thanked.
