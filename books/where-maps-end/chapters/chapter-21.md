# Chapter 21 — Hang Your Map

The trade shoulder took them four hours because of Marta’s leg, and by the time they reached it the beetles were in the verge on both sides of the road.

Not a swarm. Worse than a swarm. A slow front — a hand-deep carpet of stone beetles coming up out of the dying shelf and moving downslope through the fern toward the only warm crowded wet thing for a mile, which was two hundred people and their animals in a queue.

Pell found them at the salt post with her spear in both hands and blood on her boots that wasn’t hers.

“You’re alive,” she said. “Your ditch is running.”

“We noticed,” Bram said.

“There’s water in a dry ditch and I don’t have time to ask you about it.” She looked at Marta’s leg and her face closed. “Sit her down.”

“No,” Marta said.

“Marta,” Lumi said.

“Sit me *up*, then. Against something. I can talk.”

They sat her against the salt post with her leg out straight, and she ran the next six hours from there, and Niko spent all six of them running her orders up and down the shoulder until his legs stopped belonging to him.

---

It was Bram who saw it.

He came back from the ditch at a dead run with his sleeves black to the shoulder and his face lit up in a way that had no business being there.

“The silt,” he said.

“What?”

“Everything that came out of that grate.” He was pointing back downslope, hauling breath. “Two thousand years of rot. Leaf, root, dead wood, the whole plug — it’s laid out down that ditch in a wet ribbon a mile long and it is the *best meal in the basin.*”

Niko turned and looked at the beetle front, and at the road, and at the ditch, and the shape of it came together in his head so fast it hurt.

“They’re not coming for the people,” he said. “They’re coming for the wet.”

“They’re coming for the *dead* wet. People are just in the way of it.”

“Marta—”

“Heard him.” She was already talking to Pell. “We’re not fighting a front. We’re moving a queue. Everybody off this half of the shoulder and up onto the high verge — north side, past the rock. Nothing wet stays on the road. Nothing dead stays on the road. Fish, hide, offal, the mule—”

“The mule’s not dead,” a carter said.

“Then keep it that way and keep it moving.”

It was the ugliest work Niko had ever done and none of it looked like a story.

They dragged a tipped cart out of the verge with eleven strangers. They cut a spilled crate of salted river fish open and Bram threw it, handful by handful, down the slope into the ditch, and cried about it, and kept throwing. Lumi burned green fern in two long smoking lines to make the beetles turn, and got the timing of it right without a single page of her book open, and did not once say *actually*. Niko walked the front on the fern side, reading the line the way he’d read tracks all volume — where it moved fast, where it stalled, where it split — and shouted the splits before they became surrounds.

Twice he was wrong. Once he was right about a spur nobody else had seen, and it saved a wagon with three children in it, and he wrote the spur on the back of his hand afterwards because he had no paper left.

The wolves came at full dark, because of course they did — three of them working the tail of the queue where the noise was thinnest.

Marta couldn’t stand.

“Niko,” she said. “Call it.”

He called it. Lid and fire on the flanks, spears at the road, everyone loud, nobody chasing. Pell took one down. The other two took a dead mule from the verge instead of a living one from the traces, which was a trade Niko had deliberately left on the table, and which he would think about for a long time afterwards.

The Jester spent the whole night carrying water to people who were digging, and once, when a Scholar boy went down under a cart wheel in the dark, he was inexplicably already there.

He did not fight anything. He didn’t seem to notice he hadn’t.

---

The beetles went into the ditch some time after midnight.

Not all at once. The front bent, the way a queue bends when someone opens a second door — a slow turn out of the fern, down the slope, into a mile of black wet rotting feast, and then more of them, and then all of them, and by the small hours the road was just a road with tired people on it and the noise had moved a quarter mile downhill and become somebody else’s problem, namely the ditch’s.

Lumi sat down in the middle of the trade shoulder.

“It ate it,” she said. “The ditch ate it. That’s a functioning— that’s a *sink*, that’s what a sink is—”

“Breathe between sentences,” Marta said from the salt post.

“I’m breathing *inside* them.”

Niko sat down too, because his legs had already decided.

That was when the moths came.

He saw them first as a wrongness in the dark over the shelf: a faint grain in the air, like the beginning of snow that never arrives. Then a pale drift of it, coming up the basin from the south-west, from over the ridge, from a place a day and a half away where five people had spent an afternoon cutting seams in glass cases with pocket knives.

They came over the shoulder in their thousands, low, soft, unhurried, and every one of them carried its own small light.

The whole road stopped.

Two hundred people, a night of beetles and wolves and hauling, and every one of them stood in the dark on a broken shoulder of the south approaches with their tools still in their hands and watched moths cross the sky. Somewhere down the queue a woman started crying. Somebody else told a child to look up, look *up*.

They went out over the shelf and down over the wet ditch and away north toward the moss and the fern and the whole long dying middle of the Greenwood Basin, doing the only job they had ever had.

Bram had his hat off. The hat with the hole in it.

“Two thousand years,” he said.

“You don’t know that,” Lumi said.

“I know they were hungry.”

Niko looked for the Jester and found him sitting on a rock a little apart, elbows on knees, watching the light go over. He wasn’t smiling exactly. He looked like a man watching a child he’d given up on walk into a room.

Then he noticed Niko looking, and pulled a face, and said something about how nobody had ever thrown him a parade.

---

By dawn the shelf was drinking.

Niko walked the pale fan at first light and put his hand flat on it and the crystal moss was *wet*, wet from underneath for the first time since anyone had come here, water pushing up out of the rock the way it had pushed up in 1712 and had not pushed since.

It wasn’t healed. It wasn’t going to be. Half the fan was bone-white and would stay bone-white for years — a long pale scar down the slope above the salt post that Guild surveyors would draw for two generations without knowing what it was.

But at the top edge, where the trench had been, the moss had flowered in the night.

Small, and out of season, and the right season for the first time in a very long while.

At the healthy rim a crystal deer stood watching him with its antlers dull and its ears forward, and after a while it stepped down onto the wet moss and started to eat.

---

They came into Mossgate two days later with Marta on a Harvester cart, which she permitted after a negotiation that Niko declined to witness.

The town was full and loud and half-packed to leave. Over the following week the carts came off the streets one at a time, and the crates went back into cellars, and by the eighth day the south gate queue was just a queue again.

Marta’s leg would keep her off long roads until the turn of the season. The Guild surgeon said she would walk fine and feel it in cold weather for the rest of her life, and Marta said that was a bargain and asked what she owed.

Bram bought a new pot on the third day. He complained about the balance for a month. He never once said what happened to the old one, and when a stallholder asked about the dent in his lid he said it had been in more fights than he had.

Lumi wrote nine pages in one sitting, tore up four, and sent the rest to her academy with a covering note that Niko read over her shoulder: *Some of this contradicts the third-year chart. I would like to keep my post but I am not withdrawing any of it.*

Niko redrew the survey from memory for four days.

It came out worse than the original and more honest. He put a dotted line where he wasn’t sure of the distance, and a query mark on the Undercroft, and a note under the Understars that said *bigger than the light — not measured*, because that was the truth and it would have been a lie to draw an edge on it.

He drew the Low Gate as a small square with nothing inside it.

He drew the lights past the upside-down waterfall as three dots with *left them* written beside it, which was what they had done, and which was also a survey.

---

The Map Wall was busier than Niko had ever seen it, because half of Mossgate had hung something that week and most of them had come back to take it down.

The Guild clerk was unimpressed by all five of them.

“You were contracted to establish why three dated sheets of the same locus disagree,” she said, going down the pages. “This is a water report.”

“It’s a water report about why the maps disagree,” Lumi said.

“It doesn’t say why the maps disagree.”

“No,” Niko said. “It says the ground moved, and it says we don’t know what moved it, and it says here’s a floor nobody had drawn, and here’s another one under that.” He put his hand on the stack. “You can pay us for the water report. The rest is free.”

The clerk stamped it. She read the Understars page twice and stamped that too, more slowly.

They hung the maps at the end of the day, in the last of the light, at the only free space left, which was low down and awkward and near the corner where the rain got in.

Ruined chalk on ruined paper. A dotted line. A square with nothing in it.

Niko stood back and looked at the whole Wall — centuries of it, the returned and the faded, *Bridge out. Go left. Left is worse.* and a deer that was not a deer, and a blank sheet up near the top with three words on it and nails brighter than the paper.

“It’s a bad map,” he said.

“It came back,” Marta said, leaning on her stick. “That’s the only test the Wall has.”

Lumi had flour on her book again from the buns, because Marta had bought buns on the way, four of them for five people the way she always did, and had been informed about the arithmetic and had ignored it.

Niko was still looking at the three dated sheets — 1712, 1839, 1987 — hanging where they had always hung, disagreeing politely with each other for a hundred and eighty years.

“The quarry fibres were the same as the Undercroft ones,” he said. “Both fresh. Both soft.”

“You said that already.”

“Everyone tells me they’re gone.” He was aware of Lumi going still beside him, and Bram behind them not eating for once. “The fibres are fresh, and the roots at the cold door had grown the wrong way, and something capped three channels on purpose, and the wheel was still turning.”

He said the rest of it quietly, to the Wall, because it was the only thing there that kept receipts.

“I don’t think they’re extinct.”

Marta shifted her weight off her bad leg.

“Then where are they?” she said.

Nobody had an answer, and the light went off the top of the Wall, and somewhere below them in the street a child was sorting spent mana crystals into piles by colour and singing badly.

---

They left again eleven days later, south, because the ditch needed watching and because Marta could ride a cart as far as the salt post and complain the whole way.

The new water had already changed the slope. Fern where there had been dust. A smell of wet stone. Three separate people had put stones in the ditch to make a crossing, which meant it was a real thing now and not a thing that five people did once.

The Jester stopped walking.

He had spotted something in the wet grass at the ditch lip and he went down on one knee in the mud in his ridiculous coat and stayed there.

Niko came back.

It was a flower. A small one, five white petals with a rim of pale blue, growing out of the silt where the Throat had emptied itself — the kind of thing that only exists because a lot of very old dirt got moved somewhere new.

The Jester looked at it for a long moment.

“I’ve never seen this one before,” he said.

He put one finger against a petal, very carefully, the way you touch something you expect to be warm.

Then he laughed — not his usual laugh, the loud one for other people. A short quiet one, mostly breath, and he kept his head down while he did it.

“Good,” he said.

He got up, brushed the mud off his knees, made it worse, and went on down the road ahead of the rest of them with his bells going.

“Let’s go.”
